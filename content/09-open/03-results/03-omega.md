## The invariant omega and the log-Jacobian ideal

This section reports the third research result of the milestone S7. It connects the route of the purely inseparable equations with the route of Cossart and Piltant in Section 2.3. Volume 7, Section 3.6, gives the same result with all the details.

### The problem

Cossart and Piltant prove the resolution of threefolds with the invariant $\iota = (m, \omega, \kappa)$ (Volume 6, Section 3.2). The definition of $\omega$ uses the initial form of the equation in well adapted coordinates. Their theorem that $\iota$ does not go up holds in each dimension, but it assumes condition (E) on the singular locus, and in dimension $3$ they reach condition (E) with the embedded resolution of surfaces in regular schemes of dimension 3. For a fourfold this step needs the embedded resolution of threefolds in regular schemes of dimension 4, which is open in characteristic $p$ (Volume 6, Section 4.1). The question of this section is: what is $\omega$ for the equations $z^p + g$, and does it go up without condition (E)?

### The result

**Theorem 3.3.1.** Let $k$ be a perfect field of characteristic $p$, let $S = k[[u_1, \dots, u_n]]$, $E = \operatorname{div}(u_1 \cdots u_e)$, and $h = Z^p + f$ with $f$ clean and $\operatorname{ord} f \ge p$. Then:

1. $\omega(x) = \rho(f)$ at the closed point $x$;
2. $\omega(x)$ does not depend on the well adapted coordinates;
3. if $k$ is algebraically closed and $f$ is a polynomial, then $\omega$ does not go up under the blowup of the point, or under the blowup of a regular center that satisfies (H1) and (H2) of Section 3.1.

Part 3 holds in each dimension $n$, and it does not assume condition (E). Example 3.1.2 is a Hironaka-permissible blowup at which $\omega$ goes up from $2$ to $3$, and condition (E) fails there. So part 3 needs a condition on the center, as Theorem 3.2.6 of Volume 6 does.

### The proof

Part 1. The initial form of $f = u^r G$ is $U^r \operatorname{in}(G)$, so $\partial F / \partial U_j \ne 0$ for a free index $j$ if and only if $\partial_j \operatorname{in}(G) \ne 0$. This is the condition for $\omega = \epsilon - 1$ in Volume 6 and for $\rho = d - 1$ in Volume 7, and $\epsilon = d$ by Volume 6, Proposition 3.2.4(4).

Part 2. The ideal $\Theta(f)$ is generated by the images of $f$ under the logarithmic derivations, and this module does not depend on the coordinates. A change of the well adapted variable $Z$ adds a $p$-th power to $f$, and this does not change $\Theta(f)$. The exponents $r_i$ are the smallest orders along the components of $E$ of the elements of $\Theta(f)$. So $u^{-r} \Theta(f)$ and its order $\rho$ do not depend on the coordinates.

Part 3. Apply part 1 at the point and at the point over it, and use Volume 7, Theorems 3.2.4 and 3.4.5.

### The Lean proof

The Lean library proves part 1 and part 3 for polynomials over a field of characteristic $p$, with the standard axioms only, in `Singularity/Invariant/CossartPiltantOmega.lean`. The definition `Singularity.cpOmega` is Definition 3.2.5 of Volume 6. Part 1 is `Singularity.cpOmega_eq_logJacobianOrder`. Part 3 is `Singularity.cpOmega_cleanPart_le` for the point blowup and `Singularity.cpOmega_cleanPart_le_center` for the center with (H1) and (H2). The increase in Example 3.1.2 is `Singularity.centerExample_cpOmega_lt`. Part 2 has no Lean proof, because the library does not have the logarithmic derivations of $K[[x]]$ and the changes of the coordinates.

### What the result does not give

1. It is only for the purely inseparable equations over a perfect field. Cossart and Piltant also need the Artin-Schreier case and residue fields that are not perfect, for example at the generic points of the centers.
2. It does not give the non-increase for the centers of the second kind of [CP19], and it does not use the invariant $\kappa$.
3. As in Sections 3.1 and 3.2, it does not say that $\omega$ goes down.
4. Part 2 has no Lean proof.

The result explains the experiment E4 of Volume 6, Section 4.3, in which $\omega$ never goes up on random walks of point blowups for $n = 3$ and $n = 4$.
