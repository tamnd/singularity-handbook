# The research results

This chapter has the results of the project on the open case. Chapter 1 gives the cases of resolution that the project has proved, and Chapter 2 gives the problem and the routes to it. The results of this chapter do not prove a case of $\mathrm{Res}(p, 4)$. Each one is a step on a route of Section 2.3, and each one says which step it is.

Section 3.1 is the first result. It is about the purely inseparable equations $z^p + g = 0$ of Section 2.3, and the blowup of a regular center of positive dimension. A residual order of the log-Jacobian ideal does not go up under this blowup when the center satisfies two conditions of equimultiplicity, in each number of variables. An example shows that the second condition cannot be omitted.

Section 3.2 is the second result. It finds the singularities at a point where the residual order $\rho$ of the log-Jacobian ideal is $0$. After a change of the formal coordinates the equation is $z^p + x^r$ or $z^p + x^r x_j$, a binomial equation, and binomial varieties have a resolution in each characteristic.

Section 3.3 is the third result. It compares the residual order $\rho$ of the log-Jacobian ideal with the invariant $\omega$ of Cossart and Piltant. For the equations $z^p + g$ over a perfect field the two invariants are equal. So $\omega$ does not depend on the coordinates, and it does not go up under the blowups of Section 3.1, in each dimension and without the condition (E) of Cossart and Piltant.

Section 3.4 is the fourth result. It finds the points where the residual order $\rho$ of the log-Jacobian ideal stays under a point blowup. They lie in the projective space of a linear subspace, the analogue for $\rho$ of the directrix of Hironaka. If this subspace is $0$, then $\rho$ goes down at each point after one blowup. The result does not say that $\rho$ goes down in general, and this is the open step of route R1.

Section 3.5 is the fifth result. It studies the codimension $\tau_\rho$ of the subspace of Section 3.4. At a point where $\rho$ stays, $\tau_\rho$ does not go down if the new point is in the second case of Section 3.4. An example for $p = 2$ shows that $\tau_\rho$ can go down when the old point is in the second case and the new point is in the first case.

Section 3.6 is the sixth result. At a point where $\rho$ stays and both points are in the first case, $\tau_\rho$ does not go down if $p$ does not divide $|r| + d$. From the second case to the first case, $\tau_\rho$ can go down, by Section 3.5.

Section 3.7 is the seventh result. It shows that $\tau_\rho$ can go down from the first case to the first case when $p$ divides $|r| + d$, with an example for $p = 2$ in five variables. So the hypothesis of Section 3.6 is necessary, and with Section 3.5 each combination of the cases has an answer. The pair $(\rho, -\tau_\rho)$ is not a resolution invariant, and route R1 needs a different secondary invariant.

Section 3.8 is the eighth result. If $\rho$ stays, the old point is in the first case and $p$ does not divide $|r| + d$, then the new point is also in the first case. So $\tau_\rho$ can go down only in two combinations of the cases, and the section gives a table of all the combinations.

Section 3.9 is the ninth result. It shows with an example for $p = 3$ that the hypothesis on $|r| + d$ in Section 3.8 is necessary. If $p$ divides $|r| + d$, the new point can be in the second case. In the example and in all the steps of a random test, a component of the divisor leaves at such a step.

Section 3.10 is the tenth result. If $\rho$ stays, the old point is in the first case and no component of the divisor leaves, then the new point is in the first case and $\tau_\rho$ does not go down, with no hypothesis on $p$. So $\tau_\rho$ can go down only in two kinds of steps, and the section lists them.

Section 3.11 is the eleventh result. The full codimension $\widetilde\tau$, which uses all of $\widetilde\Theta(G)$ in both cases, does not go down at a step from the second case. An example for $p = 2$ shows that it can go down at a step from the first case.

Section 3.12 is the twelfth result. The case of a point is the type $d - \rho$, where $d$ is the residual order: the first case is the type $0$, and the second case is the type $1$. So the triple $(\rho, d, \tau_\rho)$ does not get worse at a step where $\rho$ stays and no component of the divisor leaves, and $d$ goes down by one at each step from the second case to the first case. The steps where the triple can get worse all have a leaving component.

Section 3.13 is the thirteenth result. Along a kangaroo path, which leaves the first case at a kangaroo point and comes back to it, $\tau_\rho$ does not go down at the points of the second case, and the full codimension at the end is at least $\tau_\rho$ at the start. An example for $p = 2$ shows that $\tau_\rho$ can go down at the last step. The conjecture that $\tau_\rho$ at the end is at least $\tau_\rho$ at the start is true if $\tau_\rho$ at the start is at most $1$, and Example 3.13.6 shows that it is false in general.

Section 3.14 is the fourteenth result. It asks whether each run of point blowups where $\rho$ stays is finite. The answer is no: an example for $p = 2$ follows a curve along which the order is $\rho$, and a second example starts at an isolated point of the locus where $\rho$ is at least its value at the start. The second example refutes the first form of the hypothesis H4, with the order of $\widetilde\Theta(G)$ along the curve. The corrected form says that each infinite run follows a formal curve along which the order of $G$ is at least $\rho$, and the computer tests do not contradict it. So the centers of a resolution with this invariant must be in the locus of the order of $G$, with $\rho$ as a second entry.

Section 3.15 is the fifteenth result. The second half of the hypothesis H4 holds in each dimension: along an infinite run that follows a regular formal curve, the order of $G$ along the curve is $\rho$ or $\rho + 1$. The whole hypothesis holds for $n = 2$. For $n = 3$ the first half is false, since a run that takes the corners of the divisor for ever follows no curve. In the example of Cossart and Piltant for $n = 4$ the curve of the run is transcendental. In both examples the run lies on a surface, so the hypothesis H4' asks for a subvariety of positive dimension, and the centers of a resolution can be surfaces. A third example shows that H4' is false too. The run takes the corners for ever and lies on no subvariety along which the order of $G$ is at least $\rho$. So the centers must also come from curves in the divisor that the run does not follow.

Section 3.16 is the sixteenth result. It treats the runs of point blowups where each step is at the origin of a chart, in each dimension. If such a run uses all the charts an infinite number of times, then $\rho$ drops to $0$ after a bounded number of stretches that use all the charts. If it uses $k$ charts an infinite number of times, with $2 \le k \le n - 1$, then the $k$ corner curves have the order of $G$ at least $\rho$. So the corrected question of Section 3.15, which asks for a curve in the divisor, has a positive answer for these runs in each dimension. An example in four variables uses three charts for ever, and no surface along it has the order of $G$ at least $\rho$.

Section 3.17 is the seventeenth result. It treats the runs in three variables that take the corners of the divisor for ever, with translations in the free coordinate. The translations form a pseudo Cauchy sequence of transcendental type in the sense of Kaplansky. For the points $\mu x + B_0$ no such run keeps $\rho \ge 1$, and for the points of degree less than $p$ in the free coordinate such a run has only a finite number of nonzero translations, so the question of Section 3.15 has a positive answer for it. The point $x^2 y z$ for $p = 3$ shows that $\rho$ need not drop. The conjecture V of the research notes, on the exponents of the translations, is open.

Section 3.18 is the eighteenth result. It completes Section 3.17: for each clean point in three variables, a run that takes the corners of the divisor for ever with free translations has only a finite number of nonzero translations. So the question of Section 3.15 has a positive answer for each such run. The key step is an inequality of Kaplansky type, which shows that the tie set of the limit data contains only $1$ and powers of $p$. The result does not say that $\rho$ drops, and the conjecture V is still open.

Section 3.19 is the nineteenth result. It treats the runs in four variables that take the corners of the divisor for ever, with a translation vector in the two free coordinates. The finiteness of the translations is false here: a family of points keeps $\rho = 1$ for each choice of the translations in one direction, and its points are not products with a line. In each known example the run lies on a regular hypersurface and the corner curves have the order of $G$ at least $\rho$, so the question of Section 3.15 has a positive answer. Each such run of degree less than $p$ in the free coordinates lies on a regular hypersurface, its initial forms are eventually a power of a line (the hypothesis H1), and its corner curves have the order of $G$ at least $\rho$ (Conjecture L°). In degree at least $p$ there is no planar run when the degree is $p$ or $g$ is in the $p$-th powers of the free coordinates, and Conjecture L° holds in the second case. A restart of the run at each later point then proves Conjecture P, that no clean polynomial in the free coordinates has a planar run, in each characteristic, so each such run lies on a regular hypersurface. The corner curves stay open in degree at least $p$. The form with corner surfaces is false, also for the runs on one line with an infinite number of nonzero translations, and the initial forms are not always a power of a line in degree at least $p + 1$.

Section 3.20 is the twentieth result. It shows that the lifting test for a base blowup of $z^p + g$ along a regular center holds if and only if the hypersurface has multiplicity $p$ at the generic point of the center, so no center in the multiplicity $p$ locus fails. The algorithm of Cossart still blows up centers that fail the test, for every $p$, and such a center can pass through a point of multiplicity $p$.

Section 3.21 is the twenty-first result. It asks how far the method of Jung, in the form of Abhyankar and Cutkosky, goes for 4-folds in characteristic $p > 24$. Under embedded resolution of divisors in $\mathbb{P}^4$, the proof of Cutkosky gives local uniformization of 4-folds, without a finite set. A resolution then needs a patching theorem in the form of Zariski, and no proof of it by patching is known, also in characteristic 0. The Hermitian 4-fold shows that the bound of Albanese on the multiplicities gives no global projection of local degree less than $p$, and the section proves Jung's method for one variety that has such a projection. It also gives the folklore resolution of hypersurfaces of bounded degree in large characteristic, and an example that is $z^2 - x^p$ in disguise and defeats the Hasse derivative definition of maximal contact.

Section 3.22 is the twenty-second result. It treats the $p$-closed foliations on $\mathbb{A}^4$ that a torus fixes, which enter through the purely inseparable covers of degree $p$. For the diagonal maximal torus they are classified by the subspaces of $\mathbb{F}_p^4$, and weighted blowups with weights less than $p$ make them regular. For tori of rank 1, 2 and 3 there are invariant foliations with no toric resolution in any coordinates. For a linear action of $\mathbb{G}_m$ with positive weights prime to $p$, one weighted blowup reduces the problem to threefolds with a twist $\alpha$. The untwisted case is resolved chart by chart by the theorems of Posva, and for the additive twisted case the section shows that $\alpha$ comes from invariant divisors. Whether the additive example of rank one has a resolution is open.

Section 3.23 is the twenty-third result. It treats Problem V7 for valuations with value group $\mathbb{Z}^2$ and residue field $k$ on function fields of dimension 4. In rank 2 it proves local uniformization when the residue field of the coarsening is finitely generated, with the theorem of Knaf and Kuhlmann and the reduction of Novacoski and Spivakovsky, and it gives an example with a residue field that is not finitely generated. In rank 1 it reduces the problem to a rational projection and proves it when each step of the tower has the ball property, which includes an explicit valuation that is not of the type of Knaf and Kuhlmann. It also gives defect extensions of three kinds with value group exactly $\mathbb{Z}^2$ and a test family whose status is open.

Section 3.24 is the twenty-fourth result. It treats the global step of resolution of 4-folds. With the theorem of Temkin on inseparable local uniformization, resolution of 4-folds by projective morphisms is equivalent to the degree $p$ step $\mathrm{PILU}_4$ of local uniformization together with Zariski's fundamental theorem $\mathrm{Fund}_4$. The proof of Cutkosky for 3-folds reduces $\mathrm{Fund}_4$ to principalization in dimension 4 and to a factorization statement for regular local rings of dimension 3. The section proves that this statement is false, gives a toric pair of 4-folds where the proof breaks, explains why the step of Abhyankar does not extend to dimension 4, and states what is open.

Section 3.25 is the twenty-fifth result. It continues Section 3.24. It shows that the proof of Cutkosky needs only a weak form of principalization in dimension 4 except in one step, reduces this weak form to ideals with cosupport in a divisor with simple normal crossings, and shows that it contains resolution of the 4-folds with a projective birational morphism to a regular 4-fold. It then gives the correct local input of patching along curves, relative strong factorization $\mathrm{RSF}_3$, proves it for the toric ring of the counterexample of Section 3.24 by 14 explicit blowups, and with it repairs the counterexample near its bad curve. It also proves local strong factorization for valuations of rank 1 and rational rank 3 in each characteristic, and that 36 more toric rings are not reachable with regular centers.

Section 3.26 is the twenty-sixth result. It continues Section 3.25 in the toric case. It shows that the condition (A$^{\rm w}$) of the proof of Cutkosky holds for every toric pair when Step 3 is read in its weak form, and gives an exact obstruction and a sufficient condition for the strong form. It decides by a finite game whether a regular cone is reached from the octant by toric blowups, and with it proves toric $\mathrm{RSF}_3$ for all regular cones with entries at most 4 and for all but two classes with entries at most 5. On 801 toric pairs it tests a repair of the bad cones, gives a counterexample to the drop of the number of bad cones under this repair, and proves the drop under a condition on the neighbouring cones.

Section 3.27 is the twenty-seventh result. It continues Section 3.26. It shows that a regular cone is directly reachable from the octant exactly when its matrix lies in a monoid generated by permutations and elementary matrices. It proves the Pivot Theorem: if all cones of a subdivision of the cone can share one ray, then the cone has a certificate, and it gives a form with several rays and the converse of both. With it, an infinite family of cones that are not directly reachable gets certificates with a proof. Computations give certificates for all regular cones with entries at most 6 and decide the pivot condition for all but 127 classes with entries at most 5. The section also corrects the route data for the bad cones and the citations on the strong factorization conjecture of Oda.

Section 3.28 is the twenty-eighth result. It continues Section 3.27. It shows that a certificate lets us choose the subdivision of the cone among the restrictions of star sequences of the octant, and that this choice is what toric $\mathrm{RSF}_3$ has in addition to the conjecture of Oda. It describes the sets of pivots of the multi-pivot theorem as tilings of the cone by closed stars of rays, proves a parity rule for them, and with this rule explains why one certificate of the cone $F_4$ has no set of pivots. One common star gives a set of pivots to every certificate. So the multi-pivot theorem is not a reduction of $\mathrm{RSF}_3$, and its form with at most $k$ pivots is the same as a bound on the number of pivots of a certificate. This bound is false for $k = 1$, and Section 3.30 shows that it is false for every $k$.

Section 3.29 is the twenty-ninth result. It continues Sections 3.24 and 3.25. It proves that embedded resolution of 3-folds with an isomorphism clause over the snc locus gives the weak principalization $\mathrm{Princ}_4^{\rm w}$, by a divisor that contains a set of generators and by combinatorial blowups of a locally monomial ideal, and that the form without the clause gives a very weak principalization over each field. It proves that embedded local uniformization of 3-folds gives $\mathrm{PILU}_4$ for each valuation without defect, in each multiplicity, by a toric chart lemma for $p$-th roots of monomials. For valuations with defect it reduces $\mathrm{PILU}_4$ to one open statement (D). With these results embedded resolution of 3-folds becomes the central open node of the road map.

Section 3.30 is the thirtieth result. It continues Section 3.28 and Volume 7, Section 4.5. It proves that a directly reachable cone around a star center meets the thin cone $\sigma_n$ of Proposition 3.27.21 in a set of length at most $\frac{2n}{n-1}$ along the cone, so every pivot set of every certificate of $\sigma_n$ has at least $\frac{n-1}4$ rays. So the bound $\mathrm{C}(k)$ on the number of pivots is false for every $k$, by the cone $\sigma_{4k+2}$, and the same method gives at least $\frac{k+1}4$ pivots for $F_k$. The second part shows that the quantity $h$ of Volume 7, Section 4.5, is not defined at the known rises of the residual slope $\omega$, and it reports computed trees in which each branch has at most one rise. A bound on the number of rises stays open.

Section 3.31 is the thirty-first result. It continues Section 3.29 and works on the statement (D) of Problem 3.29.23. It shows that on a fixed model (D) is the end case $\rho = 0$ of Volume 7, Section 3.5, for a boundary chosen on the model, so (D) is the form of Cossart's problem in dimension 4 along one valuation, with arbitrary models. The approximation of $b$ does not change the test on a given model. The section computes the factor between the log-Jacobian ideals of two models, which is the ordinary Jacobian determinant up to monomials, and it shows what a log form of $\mathrm{ELU}_3$ gives. It proves (D) when the valuation is discrete of rank one on $L$, with or without defect. So (D) is open only for the value groups other than $\mathbb{Z}$.

Section 3.32 is the thirty-second result. It reviews the track FO against the preprint [MN26] of Martínez-Núnez, which resolves each variety with a torus action of complexity at most 3 over an algebraically closed field of characteristic $p$. It reads the proof, finds that its input in dimension 3 is only [CP19] and [CJS20] on the base, and states the theorem without equivariance, because the step for equivariance cites a paper in characteristic 0. It proves that a torus action passes to the quotient by an invariant foliation, so $\mathrm{FQ}_4$ holds for each foliation invariant under a torus of rank at least 1, and Problem 4.10 of note 04 is solved in its quotient form. It also proves $\mathrm{PIRes}_4$ for semi-invariant data, resolution of hypersurfaces whose exponents lie in a hyperplane, a projective resolution of affine 4-folds with a torus of dimension at least 2, and the cases of reductive groups and finite quotients. The foliation form of track FO stays open.

Each section gives the statement, a complete proof, the place of the result on the map, and the Lean proof. A result of this chapter is new only in the sense that we do not know a published statement of it. An outside expert has not yet read it, and the section says so until this happens.
