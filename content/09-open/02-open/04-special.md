## Special classes and the next steps

Some classes of varieties have a resolution in all dimensions and in each characteristic. The proofs use extra structure, usually the action of a torus, and they replace the analysis of singular points by combinatorics. In this section we give these classes, we compute one example, and we say what the project does next.

### Toric varieties

Let $N \cong \mathbb{Z}^n$ be a lattice, and let $\sigma \subseteq N_\mathbb{R}$ be a rational polyhedral cone that contains no line. The cone gives an affine toric variety $U_\sigma$ over each field $k$. A fan gives a toric variety by gluing. The cone $\sigma$ is *regular* if it is generated by a part of a basis of $N$. Then $U_\sigma$ is a product of an affine space and a torus, so it is regular. A subdivision of the fan gives a proper birational toric morphism, which is an isomorphism over the open subset of the cones that are not subdivided.

**Theorem 2.4.1.** Each toric variety over a field of any characteristic has a resolution by a toric morphism, which comes from a regular subdivision of its fan.

The proof is combinatorial [KKMS, Ful93]. It subdivides each cone until all the cones are regular, and it does not use the characteristic of $k$. Thus toric varieties are no test for the difficulties of Section 2.2.

**Example 2.4.2.** Let $N = \mathbb{Z}^2$ and let $\sigma$ be the cone generated by $u_1 = (1, 0)$ and $u_2 = (1, 2)$. The determinant of $(u_1, u_2)$ is 2, so $\sigma$ is not regular. The variety $U_\sigma$ is the quadric cone $V(xy - z^2)$ in $\mathbb{A}^3$, in each characteristic. Add the ray through $u_0 = (1, 1)$. It divides $\sigma$ into the cones generated by $(u_1, u_0)$ and by $(u_0, u_2)$. Their determinants are
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1, \qquad \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = 1.$$
So both cones are regular, and the subdivision gives a resolution of the quadric cone. The new ray gives the exceptional curve, a projective line. In characteristic 2 the equation $xy - z^2$ is also $xy + z^2$, and the same subdivision works.

### Binomial varieties and torus actions

A *binomial variety* is a variety that is defined, locally, by binomial equations $x^\alpha - c x^\beta$. Locally it is a toric variety in the general sense, which need not be normal. The local torus actions need not glue to a global action.

**Theorem 2.4.3 (Włodarczyk; Abramovich and Schober).** Each binomial variety has a resolution, in each characteristic.

Włodarczyk gives a functorial resolution of locally toric varieties by cobordant blowups [Wlo22]. Abramovich and Schober give a resolution by weighted blowups [AS22]. In both proofs the invariant is built from the monomials, and the derivatives of Section 2.2 are not used.

Martínez-Núñez extends the toric case to varieties with a torus action of complexity at most 3 [MN26]. The complexity is the dimension of the variety minus the dimension of a general orbit. The proof describes the variety by a polyhedral divisor on a quotient of dimension at most 3. Then it resolves the quotient with Theorem 2.1.4, and it finishes with a toric resolution. This is one of the few unconditional results in dimension 4 and higher for a large class. The class contains fourfolds with an action of a torus of dimension 1, but it does not contain a general fourfold.

### Computer search

Bérczi uses AlphaEvolve to search for lexicographic ranking functions on a model of blowups in dimension 4 and characteristic 3 [Ber26]. The model uses the family $z^3 + g + z^2 h$ and a restricted class of centers. The search finds a ranking function with five entries that decreases with a bounded delay on the test cases, and the paper states two conjectures. This is not a proof, and the paper does not claim one. It shows that a search for invariants on a model is possible at a small scale.

### What the project does next

The project works on the routes of Section 2.3 in three ways.

1. The library proves in Lean the parts that all routes need. These are the regular locus, the order and the residual order, the Hilbert-Samuel function, and the blowup. Volume 1 and Volume 7 report these results.
2. The lab computes examples. It reproduces the examples of Hauser and Perlega [HP19a], and it tests each candidate invariant on the examples of Section 2.2. A candidate that goes up on a certified example is refuted, and the lab publishes the example. The lab does not decide that a statement is true.
3. The map records the status of each node. A node changes to the status "proved" only when a Lean proof with the standard axioms exists and the checks of the project accept it.

The goal is Conjecture 2.1.1. It is open. A result of the project in dimension 4 will be stated in this chapter with its exact hypotheses, and the hypotheses will be on the map.
