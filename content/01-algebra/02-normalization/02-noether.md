## The normalization lemma of Noether

A finitely generated algebra over a field is finite over a polynomial ring. This is the normalization lemma of E. Noether. It reduces many questions about finitely generated algebras to questions about polynomial rings, which are regular and are unique factorization domains. In Section 2.3 we use it to reduce the finiteness of the integral closure to the case of an integrally closed base.

Let $k$ be a field and let $A$ be a $k$-algebra. Elements $y_1, \dots, y_s \in A$ are *algebraically independent* over $k$ if the map $k[Y_1, \dots, Y_s] \to A$ with $Y_i \mapsto y_i$ is injective. Then the subring $k[y_1, \dots, y_s] \subseteq A$ is a polynomial ring.

**Theorem 2.2.1 (Noether normalization).** Let $k$ be a field and let $A \ne 0$ be a finitely generated $k$-algebra. Then there are elements $y_1, \dots, y_s \in A$ that are algebraically independent over $k$, such that $A$ is a finite $k[y_1, \dots, y_s]$-module.

The proof changes the generators of $A$ with a substitution $x_i \mapsto x_i - x_n^{r^i}$. The key point is the following remark about exponents. Let $r \ge 2$ be an integer. Each integer $e \ge 0$ has exactly one expansion $e = c_0 + c_1 r + \dots + c_m r^m$ with digits $0 \le c_j < r$. Thus, if $a = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$ and $b = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$ are vectors of integers with $0 \le a_i, b_i < r$, and
$$a_n + a_1 r + a_2 r^2 + \dots + a_{n-1} r^{n-1} = b_n + b_1 r + b_2 r^2 + \dots + b_{n-1} r^{n-1},$$
then $a = b$.

*Proof.* Let $x_1, \dots, x_n$ generate $A$ as a $k$-algebra. We use induction on $n$.

Let $n = 0$. Then $A$ is the image of $k$. Since $A \ne 0$ and $k$ is a field, the map $k \to A$ is an isomorphism, and we take $s = 0$.

Let $n \ge 1$. If $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are algebraically independent, we take $s = n$ and $y_i = x_i$. Otherwise there is a polynomial $f \ne 0$ in $k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ with $f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = 0$. Write
$$f = \sum_{a \in E} c_a X_1^{a_1} \cdots X_n^{a_n}$$
with a finite set $E$ of exponent vectors and $c_a \ne 0$ for each $a \in E$. Choose an integer $r \ge 2$ that is larger than each exponent $a_i$ that occurs in $E$.

*Step 1.* For $1 \le i \le n - 1$ let $z_i = x_i - x_n^{r^i} \in A$. Then $x_i = z_i + x_n^{r^i}$. In the polynomial ring $k[Z_1, \dots, Z_{n-1}, T]$ let
$$g = f(Z_1 + T^{r}, Z_2 + T^{r^2}, \dots, Z_{n-1} + T^{r^{n-1}}, T).$$
Then $g(z_1, \dots, z_{n-1}, x_n) = f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = 0$.

*Step 2.* We find the leading term of $g$ as a polynomial in $T$ with coefficients in $k[Z_1, \dots, Z_{n-1}]$. For $a \in E$, the monomial $c_a X^a$ becomes
$$c_a (Z_1 + T^{r})^{a_1} \cdots (Z_{n-1} + T^{r^{n-1}})^{a_{n-1}} T^{a_n} = c_a T^{e(a)} + (\text{terms of lower degree in } T),$$
where $e(a) = a_n + a_1 r + \dots + a_{n-1} r^{n-1}$. By the remark above, the numbers $e(a)$ for $a \in E$ are all different. Let $a^* \in E$ be the vector with the largest $e(a)$, and let $N = e(a^*)$. Then no other term reaches the degree $N$, and
$$g = c_{a^*} T^N + (\text{terms of degree less than } N \text{ in } T).$$
The coefficient $c_{a^*}$ is a nonzero element of $k$.

*Step 3.* Let $A' = k[z_1, \dots, z_{n-1}] \subseteq A$. By Step 2, $c_{a^*}^{-1} g$ is a monic polynomial in $T$ with coefficients in $k[Z_1, \dots, Z_{n-1}]$. By Step 1, $x_n$ is a root of this polynomial after the substitution $Z_i \mapsto z_i$. Thus $x_n$ is integral over $A'$. Then each $x_i = z_i + x_n^{r^i}$ is integral over $A'$, because the integral closure of $A'$ in $A$ is a subring. So $A = A'[x_n]$ is integral over $A'$ and finitely generated as an $A'$-algebra, and $A$ is a finite $A'$-module [AM69, Corollary 5.2].

*Step 4.* The ring $A'$ is generated by $n - 1$ elements, and $A' \ne 0$ because $1 \ne 0$ in $A$. By the induction hypothesis there are $y_1, \dots, y_s \in A'$, algebraically independent over $k$, such that $A'$ is a finite $k[y_1, \dots, y_s]$-module. A composition of finite maps is finite (Section 2.1), so $A$ is a finite $k[y_1, \dots, y_s]$-module. $\square$

If $A$ is a domain, then the number $s$ is the transcendence degree of $\operatorname{Frac} A$ over $k$, because $\operatorname{Frac} A$ is algebraic over $k(y_1, \dots, y_s)$. It is also equal to $\dim A$. Indeed, $\dim A = \dim k[y_1, \dots, y_s]$ because the extension is integral (Section 2.1), and $\dim k[y_1, \dots, y_s] = s$ [Har, Chapter I, Proposition 1.7 and Theorem 1.8A].

**Example 2.2.2.** Let $A = k[x, y]/(xy - 1)$, the coordinate ring of a hyperbola. The element $x$ is not a good choice for $y_1$: the ring $A = k[x, x^{-1}]$ is not finite over $k[x]$, because $x^{-1}$ is not integral over $k[x]$. The substitution of the proof gives $z = x - y^{r}$ with $r = 2$. Then $y$ is a root of $T(z + T^2) - 1 = T^3 + zT - 1$, which is monic in $T$. Thus $A$ is finite over $k[z]$.

### The Lean proof

Mathlib states Theorem 2.2.1 as `exists_finite_inj_algHom_of_fg k A`. For a field `k` and a nonzero finitely generated `k`-algebra `A`, it gives a number `s` and an injective `k`-algebra map `g : MvPolynomial (Fin s) k →ₐ[k] A` such that `g` is finite. The proof in Mathlib uses the same substitution as the proof above, and the namespace `NoetherNormalization` has the steps.

### Notes and references

Atiyah and Macdonald give the theorem for an infinite field $k$ as an exercise [AM69, Chapter 5, Exercise 16]. Over an infinite field, a linear substitution $x_i \mapsto x_i - \lambda_i x_n$ is sufficient. The substitution with powers of $x_n$ works over each field and is due to Nagata [Nag62]. Noether proved the theorem in 1926 for the study of the integral closure, and this is also its use in the next section.
