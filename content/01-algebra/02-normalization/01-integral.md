## Integral dependence

This section recalls the definitions and the facts from [AM69, Chapter 5] that we use. We give the proofs that are short, and references for the others.

**Definition 2.1.1.** Let $A \subseteq B$ be rings. An element $b \in B$ is *integral* over $A$ if it is a root of a monic polynomial with coefficients in $A$, that is, if
$$b^n + a_1 b^{n-1} + \dots + a_n = 0$$
for some $n \ge 1$ and some $a_1, \dots, a_n \in A$. The *integral closure* of $A$ in $B$ is the set of the elements of $B$ that are integral over $A$. The ring $B$ is *integral* over $A$ if each element of $B$ is integral over $A$.

Lean: `IsIntegral`, `integralClosure` (in Mathlib)

The definition also applies to a ring map $A \to B$ that is not injective. We then use the image of $A$.

The basic facts are as follows [AM69, Proposition 5.1 and Corollaries 5.2 to 5.4].

1. An element $b \in B$ is integral over $A$ if and only if the ring $A[b]$ is a finitely generated $A$-module.
2. If $b_1, \dots, b_n \in B$ are integral over $A$, then $A[b_1, \dots, b_n]$ is a finitely generated $A$-module. In particular, the integral closure of $A$ in $B$ is a subring of $B$.
3. Let $A \subseteq B \subseteq C$ be rings. If $B$ is integral over $A$ and $c \in C$ is integral over $B$, then $c$ is integral over $A$.

The third fact is the transitivity of integral dependence. We use it in Section 2.3 in the following form.

**Lemma 2.1.2.** Let $A \subseteq B \subseteq L$ be rings such that $B$ is integral over $A$. Then the integral closure of $B$ in $L$ is equal to the integral closure of $A$ in $L$.

Lean: `isIntegral_trans` (in Mathlib)

*Proof.* An element of $L$ that is integral over $A$ is integral over $B$, because a monic polynomial over $A$ is a monic polynomial over $B$. The converse is the third fact above. $\square$

### Integrally closed domains

**Definition 2.1.3.** A domain $A$ is *integrally closed* if it is equal to its integral closure in $\operatorname{Frac} A$. A domain is also called *normal* when it is integrally closed. The integral closure of $A$ in $\operatorname{Frac} A$ is the *normalization* of $A$, and we write it $\tilde{A}$.

Lean: `IsIntegrallyClosed` (in Mathlib)

The normalization $\tilde{A}$ is integrally closed [AM69, Corollary 5.5]. Integral closure commutes with localization: for a multiplicative subset $S$ of $A$, the normalization of $S^{-1} A$ is $S^{-1} \tilde{A}$ [AM69, Proposition 5.12]. Thus a localization of an integrally closed domain is integrally closed, and a domain is integrally closed if and only if all its localizations at maximal ideals are integrally closed [AM69, Proposition 5.13].

**Proposition 2.1.4.** A unique factorization domain is integrally closed.

Lean: `UniqueFactorizationMonoid.instIsIntegrallyClosed` (in Mathlib)

*Proof.* Let $A$ be a unique factorization domain and let $f \in \operatorname{Frac} A$ be integral over $A$. Write $f = g/h$ with $g, h \in A$, $h \ne 0$, such that $g$ and $h$ have no common prime factor. Let
$$f^n + a_1 f^{n-1} + \dots + a_n = 0$$
with $a_i \in A$. We multiply by $h^n$ and get
$$g^n = -h\,(a_1 g^{n-1} + a_2 g^{n-2} h + \dots + a_n h^{n-1}).$$
Assume that $h$ is not a unit, and let $\pi$ be a prime factor of $h$. Then $\pi$ divides $g^n$, so $\pi$ divides $g$. This contradicts the choice of $g$ and $h$. Thus $h$ is a unit, and $f = g h^{-1} \in A$. $\square$

**Example 2.1.5.** Let $k$ be a field. The polynomial ring $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is a unique factorization domain by Gauss's lemma [Mat, Section 20], so it is integrally closed. The ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is integrally closed for the same reason.

**Example 2.1.6.** Let $k$ be a field and let $A = k[t^2, t^3] \subseteq k[t]$. This is the coordinate ring of the cusp $y^2 = x^3$ (Example 1.4.4). Then $\operatorname{Frac} A = k(t)$, because $t = t^3/t^2$. The element $t$ is integral over $A$, because it is a root of $T^2 - t^2$. Thus $k[t] \subseteq \tilde{A}$. Since $k[t]$ is integrally closed (Example 2.1.5) and has the same field of fractions, $\tilde{A} = k[t]$. Here $\tilde{A}$ is a finite $A$-module, generated by $1$ and $t$.

### Finite and integral ring maps

A ring map $A \to B$ is *finite* if $B$ is a finitely generated $A$-module. A finite ring map is integral [AM69, Proposition 5.1]. Conversely, if $B$ is a finitely generated $A$-algebra and is integral over $A$, then $B$ is a finite $A$-module [AM69, Corollary 5.2]. If $A \to B$ and $B \to C$ are finite, then $A \to C$ is finite: if $b_1, \dots, b_m$ generate $B$ over $A$ and $c_1, \dots, c_n$ generate $C$ over $B$, then the products $b_i c_j$ generate $C$ over $A$.

An integral extension keeps the dimension. Let $A \subseteq B$ be rings such that $B$ is integral over $A$. Then $\dim A = \dim B$. This follows from the theorems of lying over and going up [AM69, Corollary 5.9 and Theorems 5.10 and 5.11].

### Notes and references

The facts of this section are in [AM69, Chapter 5] and [Mat, Section 9]. In Mathlib, the ring $\tilde{A}$ is `integralClosure A (FractionRing A)`. The general form `IsIntegralClosure C A L` says that a ring $C$ is the integral closure of $A$ in $L$, and Mathlib uses it to move results between isomorphic models of the integral closure.
