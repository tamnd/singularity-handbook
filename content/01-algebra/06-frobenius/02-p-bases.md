## p-bases and differentials

In characteristic $0$, the module of differentials $\Omega_{K/k}$ of a finitely generated field extension has dimension equal to the transcendence degree, and it is $0$ for a finite extension. In characteristic $p$ this fails: for $k = \mathbb{F}_p(t)$ and $K = k(t^{1/p})$, the extension is finite, but $\Omega_{K/k} \ne 0$. The right tool to compute $\Omega_{K/k}$ is a $p$-basis.

In this section $K$ is a field of characteristic $p$ and $k \subseteq K$ is a subfield. Let $L = kK^p$ be the subring of $K$ generated by $k$ and $K^p$. Each $a \in K$ has $a^p \in L$, so $K$ is algebraic over $L$, and $L$ is a field. For a subset $B \subseteq K$ and an exponent $\alpha : B \to \{0, \dots, p - 1\}$ with finite support, we write $b^\alpha = \prod_{b \in B} b^{\alpha(b)}$. These are the *$p$-monomials* in $B$.

### p-bases

**Definition 6.2.1.** A subset $B \subseteq K$ is *$p$-independent over $k$* if the $p$-monomials in $B$ are linearly independent over $L = kK^p$. It is a *$p$-basis of $K$ over $k$* if, in addition, the $p$-monomials span $K$ over $L$, that is, if $K = L(B)$. A $p$-basis of $K$ is a $p$-basis over the prime field $\mathbb{F}_p$, that is, over $K^p$.

For each subset $B$, the ring $L[B]$ is spanned over $L$ by the $p$-monomials, because $b^p \in L$. It is a field, because it is algebraic over $L$. So $L[B] = L(B)$.

**Lemma 6.2.2.** Let $B \subseteq K$.

1. $B$ is $p$-independent over $k$ if and only if $b \notin L(B \setminus \{b\})$ for each $b \in B$.
2. Each $p$-independent set is contained in a $p$-basis. In particular, $K$ has a $p$-basis over $k$.

*Proof.* For each subfield $M$ with $L \subseteq M \subseteq K$ and each $c \in K$, the element $c$ is a root of $T^p - c^p \in M[T]$. Over $K$ this polynomial is $(T - c)^p$, so the minimal polynomial of $c$ over $M$ is $(T - c)^j$ for some $j \le p$. Its constant term is $\pm c^j$, so $c^j \in M$. If $1 \le j < p$, then $j$ is prime to $p$, and $c^j, c^p \in M$ give $c \in M$. So if $c \notin M$, then $j = p$. Thus $[M(c) : M]$ is $1$ or $p$, and in the second case $1, c, \dots, c^{p-1}$ is a basis of $M(c)$ over $M$.

For 1, assume the condition, and let $b_1, \dots, b_m$ be distinct elements of $B$. Let $L_i = L(b_1, \dots, b_i)$. Then $b_i \notin L_{i-1}$, so $[L_i : L_{i-1}] = p$ with basis $1, b_i, \dots, b_i^{p-1}$. By the tower of bases, the $p$-monomials in $b_1, \dots, b_m$ are a basis of $L_m$ over $L$, so they are linearly independent. Conversely, if $b \in L(B \setminus \{b\})$, then $b$ is a linear combination over $L$ of the $p$-monomials in finitely many elements of $B \setminus \{b\}$. This is a linear relation between $p$-monomials in $B$, so $B$ is not $p$-independent.

For 2, the union of a chain of $p$-independent sets is $p$-independent, because a linear relation involves only finitely many monomials. By the lemma of Zorn, each $p$-independent set $B_0$ is in a maximal $p$-independent set $B$. Let $c \in K$ and assume that $c \notin L(B)$. We claim that $B \cup \{c\}$ is $p$-independent, which contradicts the maximality. By 1, we must show that $b \notin L(B \setminus \{b\}, c)$ for each $b \in B$. Let $M = L(B \setminus \{b\})$, and assume that $b \in M(c)$. Then $M(b) \subseteq M(c)$. Both have degree $p$ over $M$, since $b \notin M$ and $c \notin M(b) = L(B)$. So $M(b) = M(c)$ and $c \in L(B)$. This is a contradiction. Thus $K = L(B)$, and $B$ is a $p$-basis. $\square$

### The differentials of a field extension

**Theorem 6.2.3.** Let $B$ be a $p$-basis of $K$ over $k$. Then $\Omega_{K/k}$ is a $K$-vector space with basis $(db)_{b \in B}$.

Lean: none yet

*Proof.* By the universal property of $\Omega_{K/k}$, it is sufficient to show the following. For each $K$-vector space $V$ and each family $(v_b)_{b \in B}$ in $V$, there is exactly one $k$-derivation $D : K \to V$ with $D(b) = v_b$ for each $b \in B$.

Uniqueness. Let $D$ be a $k$-derivation. Then $D(a^p) = p a^{p-1} D(a) = 0$ for each $a \in K$, and $D = 0$ on $k$. By the Leibniz rule, $D = 0$ on the ring $L$ that $k$ and $K^p$ generate, and $D$ is $L$-linear. Since $K$ is spanned over $L$ by the $p$-monomials in $B$, the Leibniz rule shows that $D$ is determined by the values $D(b)$.

Existence. Let $P = L[T_b : b \in B]$ be the polynomial ring and let $\varepsilon : P \to K$ be the map of $L$-algebras with $T_b \mapsto b$. It is surjective, because $K = L[B]$. Its kernel contains the elements $T_b^p - b^p$, because $b^p \in L$. The quotient of $P$ by these elements is spanned over $L$ by the monomials $T^\alpha$ with $\alpha(b) < p$, and $\varepsilon$ maps them to the $p$-monomials in $B$, which are linearly independent. So the kernel of $\varepsilon$ is the ideal generated by the elements $T_b^p - b^p$. Define $D_0 : P \to V$ by
$$D_0(g) = \sum_{b \in B} \varepsilon\Big(\frac{\partial g}{\partial T_b}\Big) v_b.$$
This sum is finite, and $D_0$ is an $L$-linear derivation along $\varepsilon$: $D_0(gh) = \varepsilon(g) D_0(h) + \varepsilon(h) D_0(g)$. Since $\partial(T_b^p - b^p)/\partial T_c = 0$ for each $c$, we get $D_0(T_b^p - b^p) = 0$. For $g \in P$ and a generator $h = T_b^p - b^p$ of the kernel, $D_0(gh) = \varepsilon(g) D_0(h) + \varepsilon(h) D_0(g) = 0$. So $D_0$ vanishes on the kernel of $\varepsilon$, and it gives a derivation $D : K \to V$ with $D(b) = v_b$. It is $L$-linear, so it is a $k$-derivation. $\square$

**Corollary 6.2.4.** If $K$ has a finite $p$-basis $B$ over $k$, then $[K : kK^p] = p^{|B|}$ and $\dim_K \Omega_{K/k} = |B|$. In particular, any two $p$-bases of $K$ over $k$ have the same number of elements.

Lean: none yet

*Proof.* The $p$-monomials in $B$ are a basis of $K$ over $kK^p$, and there are $p^{|B|}$ of them. The second statement is Theorem 6.2.3. $\square$

For a finite extension $K/k$ in characteristic $0$, $\Omega_{K/k} = 0$. In characteristic $p$, $\Omega_{K/k} = 0$ if and only if $K = kK^p$, by Theorem 6.2.3. For a finite extension this holds if and only if $K/k$ is separable [Mat, Section 26].

**Proposition 6.2.5.** Let $k$ be a perfect field and let $K$ be a finitely generated field extension of $k$ of transcendence degree $n$. Then $[K : K^p] = p^n$, and $\Omega_{K/k}$ has dimension $n$ over $K$. More exactly, if $t_1, \dots, t_n$ is a transcendence basis such that $K$ is separable over $k(t_1, \dots, t_n)$, then $t_1, \dots, t_n$ is a $p$-basis of $K$ over $k$.

Lean: none yet

*Proof.* Since $k$ is perfect, $K$ has a transcendence basis $t_1, \dots, t_n$ such that $K$ is a finite separable extension of $E = k(t_1, \dots, t_n)$ [Mat, Section 26]. Since $k = k^p \subseteq K^p$, we have $kK^p = K^p$, and $E^p = k(t_1^p, \dots, t_n^p)$.

First, $[E : E^p] = p^n$. By Proposition 6.1.5, $k[t]$ is a free module over $k[t^p] = k[t_1^p, \dots, t_n^p]$ with the $p^n$ monomials $t^\alpha$, $0 \le \alpha_i < p$, as a basis. So $E^p[t] = E^p \otimes_{k[t^p]} k[t]$ has dimension $p^n$ over $E^p$. It is a domain that is finite over a field, so it is a field, and it is $E$.

Second, $K = K^p E$. The extension $K/K^p E$ is separable, because $K/E$ is separable. It is also purely inseparable, because $a^p \in K^p$ for each $a \in K$. So it is trivial.

Third, the Frobenius is an isomorphism $K \to K^p$ that maps $E$ onto $E^p$. So $[K^p : E^p] = [K : E]$. Compute $[K : E^p]$ in two ways:
$$[K : K^p][K^p : E^p] = [K : E^p] = [K : E][E : E^p].$$
Thus $[K : K^p] = [E : E^p] = p^n$. By the second step, $K = K^p(t_1, \dots, t_n)$, so the $p^n$ monomials $t^\alpha$ with $0 \le \alpha_i < p$ span $K$ over $K^p$. Since $[K : K^p] = p^n$, they are linearly independent. Thus $t_1, \dots, t_n$ is a $p$-basis, and Theorem 6.2.3 gives the dimension of $\Omega_{K/k}$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example 6.2.6.** Let $k = \mathbb{F}_p(t)$ and let $K = k(a)$ with $a^p = t$. Then $K^p = \mathbb{F}_p(a^p) = k$, so $kK^p = k$, and $\{a\}$ is a $p$-basis of $K$ over $k$. By Theorem 6.2.3, $\Omega_{K/k} = K \, da \ne 0$, although $K/k$ is finite. The set $\{t\}$ is a $p$-basis of $k$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$, by Proposition 6.2.5.

The next example is a regular curve over an imperfect field that is not smooth. The local rings of a variety over a field $k$ can be regular even where the module of differentials has the wrong rank. Thus, over an imperfect field, regular and smooth are different, and resolution asks for regular.

**Example 6.2.7.** Let $p$ be an odd prime, let $k = \mathbb{F}_p(t)$, let $f = y^2 - x^p + t$ in $k[x, y]$ and let $A = k[x, y]/(f)$. Then $A$ is a regular ring of dimension $1$, but $A$ is not smooth over $k$.

Node: SG0000000C · Lean: none yet

*Proof.* The polynomial $x^p - t$ is irreducible in $k[x]$, because $t$ is not a $p$-th power in $k$ (Example 6.1.4, and the argument of Lemma 6.2.2). So it is not a square in $k(x)$, and $f$ is irreducible. Thus $A$ is a domain of dimension $1$. Let $\mathfrak{q}$ be a prime of $k[x, y]$ that contains $f$, and let $P = k[x, y]$. The local ring $P_\mathfrak{q}$ is regular [Mat, Section 19].

If $y \notin \mathfrak{q}$, then the derivation $\partial/\partial y$ of $P$ gives $\partial f/\partial y = 2y \notin \mathfrak{q}$, because $p \ne 2$. By Proposition 1.8.2, $P_\mathfrak{q}/fP_\mathfrak{q}$ is regular. If $y \in \mathfrak{q}$, then $x^p - t = y^2 - f \in \mathfrak{q}$. The ideal $\mathfrak{n} = (x^p - t, y)$ is maximal, because $P/\mathfrak{n} = k[x]/(x^p - t)$ is a field. So $\mathfrak{q} = \mathfrak{n}$. The local ring $P_\mathfrak{n}$ is regular of dimension $2$, and its maximal ideal is generated by $x^p - t$ and $y$. By Lemma 1.1.1 these two elements are linearly independent in $\mathfrak{n}P_\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{n}^2 P_\mathfrak{n}$. Then $f = y^2 - (x^p - t) \notin \mathfrak{n}^2 P_\mathfrak{n}$, and $A_\mathfrak{n} = P_\mathfrak{n}/(f)$ is regular by Proposition 1.2.6. The local ring of $A$ at the prime $0$ is a field. Thus $A$ is regular.

The module $\Omega_{A/k}$ is the quotient of $A\,dx \oplus A\,dy$ by $df = 2y\,dy - p x^{p-1}\,dx = 2y\,dy$. At the point $\mathfrak{n}$, the element $y$ vanishes, so $\Omega_{A/k} \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak{n})$ has dimension $2$. A smooth algebra of dimension $1$ over $k$ has a module of differentials that is locally free of rank $1$. Thus $A$ is not smooth over $k$ at $\mathfrak{n}$. $\square$

Over the field $k' = k(a)$ with $a^p = t$, the polynomial is $f = y^2 - (x - a)^p$, and it lies in the square of the maximal ideal $(x - a, y)$. So $A \otimes_k k'$ is not regular at this point. Thus $A$ is regular, but it is not geometrically regular over $k$ in the sense of Definition 5.1.1. This is the phenomenon of Example 5.1.3 in dimension $1$.

### Notes and references

The theory of $p$-bases goes back to Teichmüller. Matsumura treats it with the theory of separability in [Mat, Section 26], and EGA treats the differentials of field extensions in characteristic $p$ in [EGA4, Chapter 0]. Theorem 6.2.3 holds also when $B$ is infinite, with the same proof. Example 6.2.7 is the example of Volume 0, Section 2.4, of a regular variety that is not smooth.
