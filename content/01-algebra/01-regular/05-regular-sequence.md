## Regular sequences and the residue field

In this section we prove that the maximal ideal of a regular local ring is generated by a regular sequence, and we use this result to compute the projective dimension of the residue field. The second result is the first half of the theorem of Serre, which characterizes regular local rings by homological algebra. The second half of that theorem is the tool that proves that a localization of a regular local ring is regular. Section 1.6 proves it.

Throughout the section, $(R, \mathfrak{m}, \kappa)$ is a Noetherian local ring and all modules are modules over $R$.

### Regular sequences

**Definition 1.5.1.** Let $M$ be an $R$-module. A sequence $x_1, \dots, x_n$ of elements of $R$ is *$M$-regular* if the following two conditions hold.

1. For each $i$ with $1 \le i \le n$, the element $x_i$ is a nonzerodivisor on $M/(x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})M$. That is, $x_i m \in (x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})M$ implies $m \in (x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})M$.
2. $M \ne (x_1, \dots, x_n)M$.

If $M = R$, we say that the sequence is *regular*.

Lean: `RingTheory.Sequence.IsRegular` (in Mathlib)

If the sequence satisfies only condition 1, it is *weakly regular*. If $M$ is finitely generated and each $x_i$ is in $\mathfrak{m}$, condition 2 follows from condition 1, since $M \ne \mathfrak{m} M$ by Nakayama's lemma when $M \neq 0$. The definition gives a recursion: the sequence $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ is $M$-regular if and only if $x_1$ is a nonzerodivisor on $M$ and $x_2, \dots, x_n$ is $M/x_1M$-regular. The Lean form of this recursion is the Mathlib lemma `RingTheory.Sequence.isWeaklyRegular_cons_iff`.

**Example 1.5.2.** Let $k$ be a field and let $R = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]_{(x_1, \dots, x_n)}$, as in Example 1.2.3. The coordinates $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are a regular sequence, because $R/(x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})$ is the local ring of the polynomial ring $k[x_i, \dots, x_n]$ at the origin, which is a domain, and $x_i$ is not zero in it. In the ring $R = k[x, y]_{(x, y)}/(xy)$ of Example 1.2.9, the element $x$ is a zero divisor, because $x \cdot y = 0$. But $x + y$ is a nonzerodivisor. The ring is reduced, so its zero divisors are the elements of its two minimal primes $(x)$ and $(y)$ [Mat, Theorem 6.1], and $x + y$ is in neither. Now let $R = k[x, y]_{(x, y)}/(x^2, xy)$. A prime ideal contains $x^2$, so it contains $x$, and thus the prime ideals of $R$ are those of $k[y]_{(y)}$ and $\dim R = 1$. But $x \ne 0$ and $x\mathfrak{m} = (x^2, xy) = 0$, so each element of $\mathfrak{m}$ is a zero divisor. In this ring the only regular sequence in $\mathfrak{m}$ is the empty sequence, although $\dim R = 1$.

### The maximal ideal is generated by a regular sequence

**Theorem 1.5.3.** Let $(R, \mathfrak{m})$ be a regular local ring of dimension $d$. Then there is a regular sequence $x_1, \dots, x_d$ in $R$ that generates $\mathfrak{m}$.

Node: SG0000002G · Lean: `Singularity.Algebra.exists_isRegular_ofList_eq_maximalIdeal`

*Proof.* We use induction on $d$.

Let $d = 0$. Then $\mu(\mathfrak{m}) = 0$, so $\mathfrak{m} = 0$, and the empty sequence generates $\mathfrak{m}$. The empty sequence is regular, because $R \ne 0$.

Let $d \ge 1$, and assume the result for each regular local ring of dimension $d - 1$. Lemma 1.3.2 gives an element $x \in \mathfrak{m} \setminus \mathfrak{m}^2$. By Proposition 1.2.6, the ring $\bar{R} = R/(x)$ is a regular local ring of dimension $d - 1$. By the induction hypothesis there is a regular sequence $t_1, \dots, t_{d-1}$ in $\bar{R}$ that generates the maximal ideal $\bar{\mathfrak{m}} = \mathfrak{m}/(x)$ of $\bar{R}$. Choose $y_i \in R$ with image $t_i$ in $\bar{R}$. We show that $x, y_1, \dots, y_{d-1}$ is a regular sequence that generates $\mathfrak{m}$.

First, the sequence generates $\mathfrak{m}$. Let $J = (x, y_1, \dots, y_{d-1})$. The image of $J$ in $\bar{R}$ is $(t_1, \dots, t_{d-1}) = \bar{\mathfrak{m}}$, and $J$ contains the kernel $(x)$ of $R \to \bar{R}$. Thus $J$ is the inverse image of $\bar{\mathfrak{m}}$, which is $\mathfrak{m}$.

Second, $x$ is a nonzerodivisor on $R$. By Theorem 1.3.3, $R$ is a domain, and $x \ne 0$ because $x \notin \mathfrak{m}^2$.

Third, $y_1, \dots, y_{d-1}$ is a weakly regular sequence on $R/xR = \bar{R}$. The element $y_i$ acts on each $\bar{R}$-module through its image $t_i$. Thus the conditions of Definition 1.5.1 for $y_1, \dots, y_{d-1}$ on the $R$-module $\bar{R}$ are the same as the conditions for $t_1, \dots, t_{d-1}$ on the $\bar{R}$-module $\bar{R}$, and these hold by the choice of the $t_i$.

By the recursion after Definition 1.5.1, the sequence $x, y_1, \dots, y_{d-1}$ is weakly regular. All its elements are in $\mathfrak{m}$, so it is regular. $\square$

The proof shows more. Let $x_1, \dots, x_d$ be any regular system of parameters. The classes of the $x_i$ are a basis of $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ by Lemma 1.1.1, so $x_1 \notin \mathfrak{m}^2$, and the classes of $x_2, \dots, x_d$ are a regular system of parameters of $R/(x_1)$ by Corollary 1.2.7. The same induction then shows that $x_1, \dots, x_d$ is a regular sequence [Mat, Section 17]. The Lean theorem proves only the existence of one such sequence, which is all that we use below.

In the terms of [Mat, Section 17], Theorem 1.5.3 says that the depth of $R$ is at least $\dim R$. Since the depth is at most the dimension, a regular local ring is a Cohen-Macaulay ring.

### Projective dimension

**Definition 1.5.4.** The *projective dimension* $\operatorname{pd}_R M$ of an $R$-module $M$ is the least $n \ge 0$ such that there is an exact sequence
$$0 \to P_n \to \dots \to P_1 \to P_0 \to M \to 0$$
with each $P_i$ projective. If there is no such $n$, then $\operatorname{pd}_R M = \infty$, and by convention $\operatorname{pd}_R 0 = -\infty$.

**Lemma 1.5.5.** Let $M$ be a nonzero finitely generated $R$-module and let $x \in \mathfrak{m}$ be a nonzerodivisor on $M$. Then $\operatorname{pd}_R(M/xM) = \operatorname{pd}_R M + 1$.

Lean: `ModuleCat.projectiveDimension_quotSMulTop_eq_succ_of_isSMulRegular` (in Mathlib)

*Proof.* For a finitely generated module $N$ over the Noetherian local ring $R$ and $n \ge 0$, we have $\operatorname{pd}_R N \le n$ if and only if $\operatorname{Tor}^R_{n+1}(N, \kappa) = 0$ [Mat, Section 19]. Since $x$ is a nonzerodivisor on $M$, the sequence
$$0 \to M \xrightarrow{\;x\;} M \to M/xM \to 0$$
is exact. The long exact sequence of $\operatorname{Tor}^R(-, \kappa)$ contains the maps $\operatorname{Tor}_i(M, \kappa) \to \operatorname{Tor}_i(M, \kappa)$ given by multiplication by $x$. They are zero, because $x$ acts as zero on $\kappa$. Thus for each $i \ge 1$ there is a short exact sequence
$$0 \to \operatorname{Tor}_i(M, \kappa) \to \operatorname{Tor}_i(M/xM, \kappa) \to \operatorname{Tor}_{i-1}(M, \kappa) \to 0.$$
It shows that $\operatorname{Tor}_{i}(M/xM, \kappa) = 0$ if and only if $\operatorname{Tor}_i(M, \kappa) = 0$ and $\operatorname{Tor}_{i-1}(M, \kappa) = 0$. The Tor criterion then gives the result. $\square$

**Theorem 1.5.6.** Let $(R, \mathfrak{m}, \kappa)$ be a regular local ring of dimension $d$. Then $\operatorname{pd}_R \kappa = d$.

Node: SG0000002H · Lean: `Singularity.Algebra.projectiveDimension_residueField`

*Proof.* Theorem 1.5.3 gives a regular sequence $x_1, \dots, x_d$ that generates $\mathfrak{m}$. Let $R_i = R/(x_1, \dots, x_i)$. Then $R_0 = R$ is free, so $\operatorname{pd}_R R_0 = 0$. For $i \ge 1$ we have $R_i = R_{i-1}/x_iR_{i-1}$, and $x_i$ is a nonzerodivisor on $R_{i-1}$. By Lemma 1.5.5, $\operatorname{pd}_R R_i = \operatorname{pd}_R R_{i-1} + 1$. Thus $\operatorname{pd}_R R_d = d$, and $R_d = R/\mathfrak{m} = \kappa$. $\square$

### The theorem of Serre

Theorem 1.5.6 is the first half of the theorem of Serre [Ser55], [Mat, Theorem 19.2]. Let $M$ be a finitely generated module over a regular local ring of dimension $d$. We can compute $\operatorname{Tor}^R_i(M, \kappa)$ with a projective resolution of $\kappa$ of length $d$, so it is $0$ for $i > d$, and the Tor criterion gives $\operatorname{pd}_R M \le d$. Thus a regular local ring has finite global dimension. The second half of the theorem is the converse: a Noetherian local ring of finite global dimension is regular.

The converse is the step that we need. Let $\mathfrak{p}$ be a prime ideal of a regular local ring $R$. Projective resolutions localize, so $R_\mathfrak{p}$ has finite global dimension, and by the converse $R_\mathfrak{p}$ is regular [Mat, Theorem 19.3]. This is the node SG0000000H. It is the first step to the openness of the regular locus, which is the node SG0000000K. Section 1.6 proves the converse and this corollary, with the Ext criterion in place of the Tor criterion.

### The Lean proof

The Lean proof of Theorem 1.5.3 is in the file `Singularity/Algebra/RegularSequence.lean`. The theorem `Singularity.Algebra.exists_isRegular_of_ringKrullDim_eq` proves the result for each dimension $n \in \mathbb{N}$ by induction on $n$. As in the proof of Theorem 1.3.3, it quantifies over all rings, so that the induction hypothesis applies to the quotient ring. The lifts $y_i$ come from the Mathlib lemma `List.map_surjective_iff`. The equality $J = \mathfrak{m}$ is proved by taking the inverse images of the two ideals of $\bar{R}$, with `Ideal.comap_map_of_surjective'`. The third step uses two facts: the $R$-module $R/xR$ is isomorphic to $\bar{R}$ (the definition `Singularity.Algebra.quotSMulTopEquivQuotSpan`), and regularity does not change when the scalars go through $R \to \bar{R}$ (the Mathlib lemma `RingTheory.Sequence.isWeaklyRegular_map_algebraMap_iff`).

The Lean proof of Theorem 1.5.6 applies the Mathlib theorem `ModuleCat.projectiveDimension_quotient_eq_length`, which is the iteration of Lemma 1.5.5. The module is `Shrink (R ⧸ maximalIdeal R)`, because the projective dimension in Mathlib is defined in a category of modules of a fixed universe.

### Notes and references
