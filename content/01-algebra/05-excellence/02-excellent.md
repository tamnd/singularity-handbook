## Excellent rings

An excellent ring has three properties. Its formal fibres are geometrically regular (Section 5.1). The regular locus of each algebra of finite type over it is open. Its chains of prime ideals behave as for a ring of finite type over a field. This section defines the second and the third property, and then the excellent rings.

### The J-2 property

**Definition 5.2.1.** A Noetherian ring $A$ is *J-1* if $\operatorname{Reg}(A)$ is open in $\operatorname{Spec} A$. It is *J-2* if each finitely generated $A$-algebra is J-1.

A J-2 ring is J-1, and each finitely generated algebra over a J-2 ring is J-2. Theorem 1.7.7 of Nagata is a criterion for J-1. For a field of characteristic $0$, Section 1.8 proves the J-2 property.

**Proposition 5.2.2.** A field of characteristic $0$ is J-2.

*Proof.* This is Theorem 1.8.10: for each algebra $R$ of finite type over a field of characteristic $0$, the set $\operatorname{Reg}(R)$ is open. $\square$

**Theorem 5.2.3.** Every field is J-2. More generally, a complete Noetherian local ring and a Dedekind domain whose field of fractions has characteristic $0$ are J-2.

We prove Theorem 5.2.3 only for a field $k$. A finitely generated $k$-algebra is of finite type over $k$, so this case is Theorem 1.9.5, part 1. In the literature it is [EGA4, Section 6.12]. The other cases are in [EGA4-7, Section 7.8] and [Mat, Section 32], and we do not prove them. By the criterion of Nagata (Theorem 1.7.7), the statement for a field $k$ reduces to this: for each domain $D$ of finite type over $k$, the set $\operatorname{Reg}(D)$ contains a nonempty open set. When $k$ is perfect, $D$ is smooth over $k$ on a nonempty open set. When $k$ is imperfect, this can fail. For example, let $k = \mathbb{F}_p(t)$ and $D = k(a)$ with $a^p = t$. Then $D$ is a field, so it is regular, but $\Omega_{D/k} \ne 0$ (Example 6.2.6), so $D$ is not smooth over $k$ at its only point. Thus Section 1.9 uses the flat descent of regularity (Theorem 1.9.1) in its place. The Lean library of the project proves the case of characteristic $0$ (Proposition 5.2.2), the case of a perfect field (Theorem 1.8.17) and the case of an arbitrary field (Theorem 1.9.5).

### Universally catenary rings

**Definition 5.2.4.** A Noetherian ring $A$ is *catenary* if, for each pair of primes $\mathfrak{p} \subseteq \mathfrak{q}$ of $A$, all the maximal chains of primes from $\mathfrak{p}$ to $\mathfrak{q}$ have the same length. It is *universally catenary* if each finitely generated $A$-algebra is catenary.

A quotient of a catenary ring is catenary, and a localization of a catenary ring is catenary, because the primes of $A/I$ and of $S^{-1}A$ are primes of $A$ with the same order. A quotient of a universally catenary ring $A$ is universally catenary, because a finitely generated $A/I$-algebra is a finitely generated $A$-algebra. A localization $S^{-1}A$ of a universally catenary ring $A$ is universally catenary. Indeed, a finitely generated $S^{-1}A$-algebra has the form $C = S^{-1}A[X_1, \dots, X_m]/J$. Let $J_0$ be the inverse image of $J$ in $A[X_1, \dots, X_m]$ and let $C_0 = A[X_1, \dots, X_m]/J_0$. Then $S^{-1}J_0 = J$, because each element of $J$ is a fraction $g/s$ with $g \in J_0$ and $s \in S$. Localization is exact, so $C = S^{-1}C_0$. The ring $C_0$ is a finitely generated $A$-algebra, so it is catenary, and its localization $C$ is catenary.

**Theorem 5.2.5.** A Cohen-Macaulay ring is universally catenary. In particular, a regular ring is universally catenary.

Lean: none yet

This is [Mat, Section 17]. The proof shows that a Cohen-Macaulay local ring is catenary, and that a polynomial ring over a Cohen-Macaulay ring is Cohen-Macaulay. A regular local ring is Cohen-Macaulay, because its maximal ideal is generated by a regular sequence of length equal to the dimension (Theorem 1.5.3). A field is regular, so it is universally catenary. This says that all the maximal chains of primes between two primes of an algebra of finite type over a field have the same length.

**Proposition 5.2.6.** A complete Noetherian local ring is universally catenary.

Lean: none yet

*Proof.* Let $(R, \mathfrak{m}, \kappa)$ be a complete Noetherian local ring. If $R$ contains a field, then $R$ is a quotient of $\kappa[[X_1, \dots, X_e]]$ by Corollary 4.4.8. Otherwise $R$ is a quotient of $C[[X_1, \dots, X_n]]$, where $C$ is a Cohen ring, by Theorem 4.4.9. In both cases $R$ is a quotient of a regular local ring $S$: the ring $\kappa[[X_1, \dots, X_e]]$ is regular by Corollary 4.3.4, and $C[[X_1, \dots, X_n]]$ is regular because its maximal ideal $(p, X_1, \dots, X_n)$ has $n + 1$ generators and its dimension is $n + 1$. By Theorem 5.2.5, $S$ is universally catenary, so its quotient $R$ is universally catenary. $\square$

A Noetherian local ring need not be catenary. Nagata constructed Noetherian local domains that are not catenary, and Noetherian local domains that are catenary but not universally catenary [Nag62, Appendix]. Such a ring is not a quotient of a regular local ring, by Theorem 5.2.5.

### Excellent rings

**Definition 5.2.7.** A Noetherian ring $A$ is *quasi-excellent* if it is a G-ring and it is J-2. It is *excellent* if, in addition, it is universally catenary.

Node: SG00000030 · Lean: none yet

The conditions are local in a weak sense. A G-ring is defined by its local rings, and the regular locus and the chains of primes are defined by the primes. The next theorem gives the main examples. It is due to Grothendieck.

**Theorem 5.2.8 (Grothendieck).**

1. A field, a complete Noetherian local ring, and a Dedekind domain whose field of fractions has characteristic $0$ are excellent. In particular, $\mathbb{Z}$ is excellent.
2. Each localization and each finitely generated algebra of an excellent ring is excellent. The same holds for quasi-excellent rings.

Lean: none yet

For the proof see [EGA4-7, Section 7.8] and [Mat, Section 32]. Part 1 combines Theorem 5.1.7, Proposition 5.1.6, Theorem 5.2.3, Theorem 5.2.5 and Proposition 5.2.6. For example, a Dedekind domain is regular, so it is universally catenary by Theorem 5.2.5. Part 2 for the G-ring property is Theorem 5.1.7. For the J-2 property it follows from the definition, since a finitely generated algebra over a finitely generated $A$-algebra is a finitely generated $A$-algebra, and the same holds for the universally catenary property. A localization $S^{-1}A$ is not a finitely generated $A$-algebra in general, so for localizations one needs more work.

**Corollary 5.2.9.** Let $A$ be a ring of finite type over a field, over $\mathbb{Z}$ or over a complete Noetherian local ring, or a localization of such a ring. Then $A$ is excellent. In particular, the local ring of a variety at a point is excellent.

Lean: none yet

*Proof.* This follows from Theorem 5.2.8. $\square$

The part of Corollary 5.2.9 that this volume proves is the following. Let $k$ be a field and let $A$ be of finite type over $k$. Then $A$ is J-2 by Theorem 1.9.5, because a finitely generated $A$-algebra is of finite type over $k$. If $k$ has characteristic $0$, then the formal fibres of the local rings of $A$ are geometrically regular as soon as they are regular, by Proposition 5.1.2. The regularity of these formal fibres is the content of Theorem 5.1.7, part 2, and we do not prove it.

### Notes and references

Excellent rings are due to Grothendieck [EGA4-7, Section 7.8], who called them "anneaux excellents". Matsumura gives an account in [Mat, Section 32]. The J-2 property is the name of Matsumura; Grothendieck states it in the form that the regular locus is open for each finitely generated algebra. Nagata studied the same class of rings under another name, and he gave the examples of bad Noetherian rings in [Nag62, Appendix]. Matsumura treats the chain conditions on prime ideals, with the theorems of Ratliff on universally catenary rings, in [Mat, Section 31].
