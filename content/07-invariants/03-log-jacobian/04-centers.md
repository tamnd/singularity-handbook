## The log-Jacobian ideal under a blowup in a regular center

A resolution algorithm does not blow up only points. It blows up regular centers of each dimension, and the center is usually the locus where an invariant has its largest value. In this section we extend Section 3.2 to a center $C$ of positive dimension. The transformation law has the same form, with an ideal $\Theta_C$ in the place of $\Theta_0$. The non-increase of $\rho$ is different. It holds under two conditions on $C$, and Example 3.4.6 shows that the first condition alone is not sufficient.

### The setting

We keep the setting of Section 3.2: $K$ is an algebraically closed field of characteristic $p$, $g \in K[x]$ is clean for the divisor $E$ of the set $\Delta$, and $\mathfrak{m} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$. Let $2 \le c \le n$, let
$$C = V(x_1, \dots, x_c), \qquad I_C = (x_1, \dots, x_c),$$
and assume that $g \in I_C^p$. Then the hypersurface $f = z^p + g$ has order $p$ along the regular center $V(z, x_1, \dots, x_c)$, by Corollary 4.2.2 with $h = 0$. The center $C$ has normal crossings with $E$, because both are given by coordinates. For $c = n$ the center is the origin, and this is the case of Section 3.2.

The *order along $C$* of an element or an ideal $I$ of $K[x]$ is
$$\operatorname{ord}_C I = \max \{ s : I \subseteq I_C^s \}.$$
It is the order at the generic point of $C$. We write $\operatorname{ord}_0 = \operatorname{ord}$ for the order at the origin. Since $I_C \subseteq \mathfrak{m}$, we have $\operatorname{ord}_C I \le \operatorname{ord}_0 I$.

After a change of the numbering of $x_1, \dots, x_c$, a point of the exceptional divisor over the origin is the origin of the chart $\pi = \pi_{C,1,t}$, with
$$\pi(x_1) = x_1, \qquad \pi(x_i) = x_1 (x_i + t_i) \ (2 \le i \le c), \qquad \pi(x_i) = x_i \ (i > c).$$
For a monomial $x^\alpha$ with $\alpha_i = 0$ for $i > c$, we have $\pi(x^\alpha) = x_1^{|\alpha|} \prod_{i = 2}^{c} (x_i + t_i)^{\alpha_i}$. So, as in Proposition 2.1.1, each element $h$ of $I_C^s$ has the form $\pi(h) = x_1^s h'$ for exactly one $h' \in K[x]$. We put $g' = x_1^{-p} \pi(g)$. The new divisor is
$$\Delta' = \{1\} \cup \{ i \in \Delta : i \ne 1, \ i > c \text{ or } t_i = 0 \}.$$
A component $x_i = 0$ of $E$ with $i > c$ contains $C$ only if $C \subseteq V(x_i)$, which is false. Its strict transform is $x_i = 0$ again, and it passes through the origin of the chart. As in Section 3.2, we compare ideals in $K[[x]]$, and $\Theta'$ is the log-Jacobian ideal for $\Delta'$.

### The transformation law

**Definition 3.4.1.** The *log-Jacobian ideal tangent to $C$* is
$$\Theta_C(g) = \bigl( x_i \partial_i g \ (i \in \Delta), \ \partial_j g \ (j \notin \Delta, \ j > c) \bigr) + I_C \bigl( \partial_j g \ (j \notin \Delta, \ j \le c) \bigr).$$

The logarithmic derivations that map $I_C$ into $I_C$ are the $K[x]$-combinations of $x_i \partial_i$ for $i \in \Delta$, of $\partial_j$ for $j \notin \Delta$ with $j > c$, and of $x_k \partial_j$ for $j \notin \Delta$ with $j \le c$ and $k \le c$. The ideal $\Theta_C(g)$ is the image of $g$ under these derivations, and $I_C \Theta(g) \subseteq \Theta_C(g) \subseteq \Theta(g)$. For $c = n$ it is the ideal $\Theta_0(g)$ of Definition 3.2.1.

**Lemma 3.4.2.** For each $h \in K[x]$,
$$\partial_i \pi(h) = x_1 \, \pi(\partial_i h) \ (2 \le i \le c), \qquad \partial_i \pi(h) = \pi(\partial_i h) \ (i > c), \qquad x_1 \partial_1 \pi(h) = \pi\Bigl( \sum_{i=1}^{c} x_i \partial_i h \Bigr).$$

*Proof.* The proof is the proof of Lemma 3.2.2. The variable $x_i$ with $2 \le i \le c$ occurs in $\pi(h)$ only in the place $i$, with the factor $x_1$. The variable $x_i$ with $i > c$ occurs only in the place $i$, without a factor. For $x_1$, the chain rule gives $\partial_1 \pi(h) = \pi(\partial_1 h) + \sum_{i = 2}^{c} (x_i + t_i) \pi(\partial_i h)$, and we multiply by $x_1$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.4.3.** In $K[[x]]$,
$$\Theta'(g') = x_1^{-p} \, \pi\bigl(\Theta_C(g)\bigr) \, K[[x]].$$

*Proof.* As in the proof of Theorem 3.2.3, $\Theta'(g') = x_1^{-p} J$, where $J$ is the ideal generated by $D \pi(g)$ for the basis elements $D$ of the logarithmic derivations for $\Delta'$. By Lemma 3.4.2, $J$ is generated by:

1. $\pi\bigl(\sum_{i \le c} x_i \partial_i g\bigr)$, from $D = x_1 \partial_1$;
2. $\pi(x_i \partial_i g)$ for $i \in \Delta'$, $i \ne 1$, from $D = x_i \partial_i$. If $i \le c$, then $t_i = 0$ and $x_i \cdot x_1 \pi(\partial_i g) = \pi(x_i) \pi(\partial_i g)$. If $i > c$, then $x_i \pi(\partial_i g) = \pi(x_i \partial_i g)$;
3. $x_1 \pi(\partial_j g)$ for $2 \le j \le c$ and $j \notin \Delta'$, from $D = \partial_j$;
4. $\pi(\partial_j g)$ for $j > c$ and $j \notin \Delta'$, from $D = \partial_j$. For $j > c$, the condition $j \notin \Delta'$ is the condition $j \notin \Delta$.

Since $\pi(I_C) K[[x]] = (x_1)$, the ideal $\pi(\Theta_C(g)) K[[x]]$ is generated by $\pi(x_i \partial_i g)$ for $i \in \Delta$, by $\pi(\partial_j g)$ for $j \notin \Delta$ with $j > c$, and by $x_1 \pi(\partial_j g)$ for $j \notin \Delta$ with $j \le c$.

First we show that $J$ contains these generators. Let $i \in \Delta$ with $i \ne 1$. If $i > c$ or $t_i = 0$, then $\pi(x_i \partial_i g)$ is in item 2. If $i \le c$ and $t_i \ne 0$, then $i \notin \Delta'$ and $\pi(x_i \partial_i g) = (x_i + t_i) \, x_1 \pi(\partial_i g)$ is a multiple of an element of item 3. Let $j \notin \Delta$ with $j \ne 1$. If $j > c$, then $\pi(\partial_j g)$ is in item 4. If $j \le c$, then $x_1 \pi(\partial_j g)$ is in item 3. The last generator has the index $1$, and it is $x_1 \pi(\partial_1 g)$ in both cases $1 \in \Delta$ and $1 \notin \Delta$. It is the element of item 1 minus the elements $\pi(x_i \partial_i g) = x_1 (x_i + t_i) \pi(\partial_i g)$ for $2 \le i \le c$. For $i \in \Delta$ these elements are in $J$ by the first part of the argument. For $i \notin \Delta$ they are multiples of the elements $x_1 \pi(\partial_i g)$ of item 3.

Next we show that $\pi(\Theta_C(g)) K[[x]]$ contains the generators of $J$. Item 1 is the image of $\sum_{i \le c} x_i \partial_i g$, and each term of this sum is in $\Theta_C(g)$. Item 2 is the image of a generator. Item 4 is the image of a generator. In item 3, if $j \notin \Delta$, the element is a generator. If $j \in \Delta$ and $t_j \ne 0$, then $x_1 \pi(\partial_j g) = (x_j + t_j)^{-1} \pi(x_j \partial_j g)$, and $x_j + t_j$ is a unit in $K[[x]]$. $\square$

For $c = n$ there is no index $j > c$, and the theorem is Theorem 3.2.3.

### The order of the weak transform

The proof of Theorem 3.2.4 uses Theorem 2.2.2: the weak transform under a point blowup does not have a larger order. For a center of positive dimension, we divide by the power of $x_1$ that the order along $C$ gives, and the weak transform can have a larger order. For example, let $c = 2$, $n = 3$ and $h = x_2 x_3$. Then $\operatorname{ord}_C h = 1$, and for $t_2 = 0$ the weak transform $x_1^{-1} \pi(h) = x_2 x_3$ has order $2$. The bound holds when the element is equimultiple, that is, when its order along $C$ and its order at the origin are equal.

**Lemma 3.4.4.** Let $h \in I_C^s$ with $\operatorname{ord}_0 h = s$, and let $h' = x_1^{-s} \pi(h)$. Then $\operatorname{ord}_0 h' \le s$, for each choice of the translations $t$.

*Proof.* The ideal $I_C^s$ is generated by the monomials $x^\alpha$ of degree $s$ in $x_1, \dots, x_c$. So $h = \sum_\alpha a_\alpha x^\alpha$, a finite sum over these $\alpha$, with $a_\alpha \in K[x]$. The homogeneous part of degree $s$ of $a_\alpha x^\alpha$ is $a_\alpha(0) x^\alpha$. So the homogeneous part of degree $s$ of $h$ is $H = \sum_\alpha a_\alpha(0) x^\alpha$, and $H \ne 0$ because $\operatorname{ord}_0 h = s$. The form $H$ involves only $x_1, \dots, x_c$, and its coefficients are in $K$.

We have $h' = \sum_\alpha \pi(a_\alpha) \prod_{i = 2}^{c} (x_i + t_i)^{\alpha_i}$. Let $\varphi \colon K[x] \to K[x_2, \dots, x_c]$ be the homomorphism with $\varphi(x_1) = 0$ and $\varphi(x_i) = 0$ for $i > c$, and $\varphi(x_i) = x_i$ for $2 \le i \le c$. Then $\varphi(\pi(x_i)) = 0$ for each $i$, because $\pi(x_i)$ is a multiple of $x_1$ for $i \le c$ and $\pi(x_i) = x_i$ for $i > c$. So $\varphi(\pi(a_\alpha)) = a_\alpha(0)$, and
$$\varphi(h') = \sum_\alpha a_\alpha(0) \prod_{i = 2}^{c} (x_i + t_i)^{\alpha_i} = H(1, x_2 + t_2, \dots, x_c + t_c).$$
The polynomial $H(1, y_2, \dots, y_c)$ is not $0$, because $H = x_1^s H(1, x_2 / x_1, \dots, x_c / x_1)$, and it has degree at most $s$. A translation of the variables does not change the degree, and it maps a nonzero polynomial to a nonzero polynomial. So $\varphi(h') \ne 0$ has degree at most $s$, and $\operatorname{ord}_0 \varphi(h') \le s$. The map $\varphi$ sends $\mathfrak{m}^k$ into the $k$-th power of the maximal ideal of $K[x_2, \dots, x_c]$, so $\operatorname{ord}_0 h' \le \operatorname{ord}_0 \varphi(h') \le s$. $\square$

For $c = n$ the condition $h \in I_C^s$ follows from $\operatorname{ord}_0 h = s$, and the lemma is Theorem 2.2.2. The polynomial $H(1, x_2 + t_2, \dots, x_c + t_c)$ is the restriction of $h'$ to the fiber $V(x_1, x_{c+1}, \dots, x_n)$ of the exceptional divisor over the origin.

### The residual order under two conditions

Write $g = x^r G$ and $d = \operatorname{ord} G$ as in Theorem 3.1.4, and let $\rho = \operatorname{ord}_0 \widetilde\Theta(G)$. Since $r_j = 0$ for $j \notin \Delta$, we have $\Theta_C(g) = x^r \widetilde\Theta_C(G)$, with
$$\widetilde\Theta_C(G) = \bigl( r_i G + x_i \partial_i G \ (i \in \Delta), \ \partial_j G \ (j \notin \Delta, \ j > c) \bigr) + I_C \bigl( \partial_j G \ (j \notin \Delta, \ j \le c) \bigr),$$
and $I_C \widetilde\Theta(G) \subseteq \widetilde\Theta_C(G) \subseteq \widetilde\Theta(G)$. The two conditions on the center are:

- (H1) $\operatorname{ord}_C \widetilde\Theta(G) = \rho$.
- (H2) $\operatorname{ord}_C \widetilde\Theta_C(G) = \rho + 1$, or $\operatorname{ord}_0 \widetilde\Theta_C(G) = \rho$.

Condition (H1) says that $\widetilde\Theta(G)$ is equimultiple along $C$: its order at the generic point of $C$ is its order at the origin. Condition (H2) says the same for $\widetilde\Theta_C(G)$ in the case where its order along $C$ is $\rho$. For $c = n$ both conditions hold, because $\operatorname{ord}_C = \operatorname{ord}_0$ and the order of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ is $\rho$ or $\rho + 1$.

**Theorem 3.4.5.** Let $g \ne 0$ be clean with $g \in I_C^p$, and assume (H1) and (H2). Let $g'_c$ be the clean part of $g'$ for the divisor $\Delta'$. Then $g'_c \ne 0$ and $\rho(g'_c) \le \rho$.

*Proof.* By part 2 of Theorem 3.1.4, $\Theta(g) \ne 0$, so $\Theta_C(g) \supseteq I_C \Theta(g)$ is not $0$. The map $\pi$ is injective, so $\Theta'(g') \ne 0$ by Theorem 3.4.3. If $g'_c = 0$, then $g'$ is a $p$-th power, and $\Theta'(g') = 0$ by Lemma 3.1.2. So $g'_c \ne 0$.

Let $\sigma = \operatorname{ord}_C \widetilde\Theta_C(G)$. From $I_C \widetilde\Theta(G) \subseteq \widetilde\Theta_C(G) \subseteq \widetilde\Theta(G)$ and (H1), $\sigma$ is $\rho$ or $\rho + 1$. Each element $h$ of $\widetilde\Theta_C(G)$ is in $I_C^\sigma$, so $\pi(h)$ is divisible by $x_1^\sigma$. Let $W$ be the ideal of $K[[x]]$ generated by the elements $x_1^{-\sigma} \pi(h)$ with $h \in \widetilde\Theta_C(G)$.

We show that $\operatorname{ord}_0 W \le \rho$. If $\sigma = \rho + 1$, take $h \in \widetilde\Theta(G)$ with $\operatorname{ord}_0 h = \rho$. By (H1), $h \in I_C^\rho$. Then $x_1 h \in \widetilde\Theta_C(G)$, so $x_1^{-\rho - 1} \pi(x_1 h) = x_1^{-\rho} \pi(h)$ is in $W$, and it has order at most $\rho$ by Lemma 3.4.4. If $\sigma = \rho$, then (H2) gives $\operatorname{ord}_0 \widetilde\Theta_C(G) = \rho$. Take $h \in \widetilde\Theta_C(G)$ with $\operatorname{ord}_0 h = \rho$. Then $h \in I_C^\rho$, so $x_1^{-\rho} \pi(h) \in W$ has order at most $\rho$ by Lemma 3.4.4.

Now we apply Theorem 3.4.3. Write $|r|_C = r_1 + \dots + r_c$. Then
$$\pi(x^r) = x_1^{|r|_C} \prod_{i = 2}^{c} (x_i + t_i)^{r_i} \prod_{i > c} x_i^{r_i},$$
so
$$\Theta'(g') = x_1^{-p} \pi(x^r) \, x_1^\sigma \, W = u \cdot x_1^{|r|_C + \sigma - p} \prod_{i \in \Delta' \setminus \{1\}} x_i^{r_i} \cdot W,$$
where $u$ is the product of the units $(x_i + t_i)^{r_i}$ with $i \le c$ and $t_i \ne 0$. The exponent of $x_1$ is not negative. Indeed, the derivations $x_i \partial_i$ for $i \in \Delta$ and $\partial_j$ for $j > c$ map $I_C^s$ into $I_C^s$, and $\partial_j$ for $j \le c$ maps $I_C^s$ into $I_C^{s-1}$. So each generator of $\widetilde\Theta_C(G)$ is in $I_C^{s}$ for $s = \operatorname{ord}_C G$, and $\sigma \ge \operatorname{ord}_C G$. Then $|r|_C + \sigma - p \ge |r|_C + \operatorname{ord}_C G - p = \operatorname{ord}_C g - p \ge 0$, because $g \in I_C^p$.

So $\Theta'(g') = x^b W$ for a monomial $x^b$ in the variables of $\Delta'$. The rest of the proof is the end of the proof of Theorem 3.2.4. Let $x^s$ be the largest monomial in the variables of $\Delta'$ that divides each element of $W$, and write $W = x^s W_0$. By Lemma 3.1.2 and Theorem 3.1.4, part 1, applied to the clean $g'_c$, we get $\widetilde\Theta(G') = W_0$. So
$$\rho(g'_c) = \operatorname{ord}_0 W_0 = \operatorname{ord}_0 W - |s| \le \operatorname{ord}_0 W \le \rho. \qquad \square$$

The proof does not use the number of variables. Condition (H1) gives an element of order $\rho$ in $\widetilde\Theta(G)$ that is equimultiple along $C$, and condition (H2) gives one in $\widetilde\Theta_C(G)$ when the factor $x_1$ of the first case is not available. Without these conditions, the elements of the smallest order at the origin can have a smaller order along $C$, and then Lemma 3.4.4 does not apply.

### Condition (H2) cannot be omitted

**Example 3.4.6.** Let $p = 2$, $n = 3$ with the coordinates $(x, y, w)$, $\Delta = \{3\}$, so $E = V(w)$, and let $g = y^2 w + y^3$. Let $C = V(x, y)$, so $c = 2$ with $x_1 = x$ and $x_2 = y$.

The polynomial $g$ is clean, because neither $y^2 w$ nor $y^3$ is a square. The exponent of $w$ in $y^3$ is $0$, so $r = 0$ and $G = g$, with $d = 3$. Also $g = y^2 (w + y) \in I_C^2$. In characteristic $2$,
$$w \partial_w g = y^2 w, \qquad \partial_x g = 0, \qquad \partial_y g = 2 y w + 3 y^2 = y^2.$$
So $\widetilde\Theta(G) = (y^2 w, 0, y^2) = (y^2)$, and $\rho = 2$. The order of $(y^2)$ along $C$ is $2$, so (H1) holds. The ideal tangent to $C$ is
$$\widetilde\Theta_C(G) = (y^2 w) + (x, y) \cdot (\partial_x g, \partial_y g) = (y^2 w, x y^2, y^3).$$
Its order along $C$ is $2$, from $y^2 w$, and its order at the origin is $3$. So (H2) fails.

Take the $x$-chart without translation: $\pi(x) = x$, $\pi(y) = x y$ and $\pi(w) = w$. Then $\pi(g) = x^2 y^2 w + x^3 y^3$, so $g' = y^2 w + x y^3$, and $\Delta' = \{1, 3\}$, so $E' = V(x w)$. The polynomial $g'$ is clean, and no variable of $\Delta'$ divides both of its monomials, so $r' = 0$ and $d' = 3$. Then
$$x \partial_x g' = x y^3, \qquad w \partial_w g' = y^2 w, \qquad \partial_y g' = 2 y w + 3 x y^2 = x y^2.$$
So $\Theta'(g') = (x y^3, y^2 w, x y^2) = (y^2 w, x y^2)$. Neither $x$ nor $w$ divides both generators, so $\widetilde\Theta(G') = (y^2 w, x y^2)$, and $\rho(g'_c) = 3$. Thus $\rho$ goes up from $2$ to $3$.

We can check Theorem 3.4.3 in this example. Here $\Theta_C(g) = (y^2 w, x y^2, y^3)$, and $x^{-2} \pi(\Theta_C(g)) = (y^2 w, x y^2, x y^3) = (y^2 w, x y^2)$, which is $\Theta'(g')$. The proof of Theorem 3.4.5 fails at one place. Here $\sigma = 2 = \rho$, but each element of $\widetilde\Theta_C(G)$ has order at least $3$ at the origin, so Lemma 3.4.4 gives no element of order $2$ in $W$. The element $y^2$ of order $\rho = 2$ in $\widetilde\Theta(G)$ gives only $x y^2 \in \widetilde\Theta_C(G)$, and $x^{-2} \pi(x y^2) = x y^2$ has order $3$.

The center $C$ is too large here. The order of $\widetilde\Theta_C(G)$ is $2$ at the generic point of $C$ and $3$ at the origin, so the origin is a special point of $C$. A center rule must avoid this case. A natural candidate is to blow up the locus where the pair $(\rho, \operatorname{ord}_0 \widetilde\Theta_C(G))$ is maximal. Whether such a rule ends with $\rho = 0$ for a base of dimension $4$ is the open problem of this chapter.

### Notes and references

For a base of dimension $3$, Cossart gives a condition of permissibility for the centers of positive dimension in his thesis [Cos87], and Posva gives a modern account of the transformation law [Pos24, Appendix A]. The conditions (H1) and (H2) and Theorem 3.4.5 are written here for each number of variables. Lemma 3.4.4 is the equimultiple case of the classical bound for the order of a weak transform [Hau03]. The computation in Example 3.4.6 uses only the definitions, and the reader can check each step by hand.
