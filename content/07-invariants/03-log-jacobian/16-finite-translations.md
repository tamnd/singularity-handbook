## Finiteness of the translations

This section continues Section 3.15. There, Theorem 3.15.17 shows that a corner run with free translations has only a finite number of nonzero translations if $\deg_x g < p$, and Theorem 3.15.16 shows that no such run of $\mu x + B_0$ keeps $\rho \ge 1$. By Proposition 3.15.10 and Corollary 3.15.11, the general case with $g_x \ne 0$ reduces to the points $\mu x + Q(x^p, y, z)$, and the proof of Theorem 3.15.16 stops there, since the coefficients of the powers $x_m^{pi}$ change with $m$. Here we close this gap. The main result is Theorem 3.16.14: for each clean $g \in K[[y, z]][x]$, each corner run with free translations has only a finite number of nonzero translations. After the last one, Theorem 3.13.20 applies, so Question 3.13.18 has a positive answer for each such run.

The proof has four parts. First, we turn the transcendental type of Proposition 3.15.7 into a bound on the values: for a power series $G$, the value of $G$ along the translations is less than the values of all the terms of its Taylor expansion (Lemma 3.16.3). This is the inequality of Kaplansky, in a form that allows power series. Second, we take the limits of the values of the coefficients of $g = \mu x + F(x^p)$ at $\varphi_\infty$. They define numbers $W_k$, and the set $\mathcal{T}$ of the indices where $W_k$ is least. A short argument with Lucas's theorem shows that $\mathcal{T}$ contains only $1$ and powers of $p$ (Lemma 3.16.4). Third, an identity in the Hahn field compares the finite sum $(V_m)_{< W}$ with an infinite sum of monomials (Proposition 3.16.7). In each of the three cases $\mathcal{T} = \{1\}$, $1 \notin \mathcal{T}$, and $1 \in \mathcal{T} \ne \{1\}$, this gives a contradiction if the run has an infinite number of nonzero translations (Theorems 3.16.8, 3.16.9 and 3.16.10). Fourth, a change of the free coordinate keeps an infinite number of nonzero translations (Lemma 3.16.12), so the normal form of Corollary 3.15.11 reduces the general case to the class (Theorem 3.16.13).

The result does not say that $\rho$ drops. Example 3.15.18 is a run with $\rho = 1$ at each point and no translations. The conjecture V of Section 3.15 also stays open.

### The setting and the power series in x

We use the setting and the notation of Section 3.15. So $n = 3$, $V(y_m)$ and $V(z_m)$ are in $\Delta$ and $V(x_m)$ is not, step $m$ is the $y$-chart or the $z$-chart followed by a translation $t_m$ in $x$, each chart occurs an infinite number of times, and $\rho \ge 1$ is the same at each point. Also $x_N = \varphi_m + M_m x_m$, $v(M_m)$ increases to $1 + \theta$, and $M_m y_m z_m = y_N z_N$ by Lemma 3.13.24.

The normal form of Corollary 3.15.11 is a power series in $x''$, and not a polynomial. So we need Lemma 3.15.1 and Proposition 3.15.5 for power series in $x$.

**Lemma 3.16.1.** Let $g \in K[[x, y, z]]$ be clean at $P_N$. Then Lemma 3.15.1 holds for $g$, with $P_m(X) = g(\varphi_m + M_m X) = \sum_{k \ge 0} A_k X^k$ a power series, $A_k = M_m^k g^{[k]}(\varphi_m) \in K[[y_m, z_m]]$ and $g^{[k]}$ the Hasse derivatives of $g$ in $x$. Proposition 3.15.5 also holds for $g$. In particular (★) fails at each point of a corner run with free translations of $g$.

*Proof.* By Lemma 3.13.24, part 3, $\varphi_m$ and $M_m$ are polynomials in $y_m, z_m$, and their monomials have positive values, so they are not constants. Also $y_N$ and $z_N$ are monomials in $y_m, z_m$ with nonnegative exponents, and they are not constants. So the substitution $x_N = \varphi_m + M_m x_m$, $y_N = y_N(y_m, z_m)$, $z_N = z_N(y_m, z_m)$ sends the maximal ideal into the maximal ideal, and it defines a map $K[[x_N, y_N, z_N]] \to K[[x_m, y_m, z_m]]$. Taylor's formula $g(a + b) = \sum_k g^{[k]}(a) b^k$ holds for each power series $g$ and $a, b$ in the maximal ideal, since it holds for polynomials and both sides are continuous for the topology of the maximal ideal. This gives $A_k = M_m^k g^{[k]}(\varphi_m)$, with $g^{[k]}(\varphi_m) \in K[[y_m, z_m]]$. The proof of Lemma 3.15.1 uses only these facts, the rule $\pi(h^p) = \pi(h)^p$ and the chain rule, and all of them hold for power series. So Lemma 3.15.1 holds.

In the proof of Proposition 3.15.5, $P^{\mathrm{cl}}$ is now a power series in $x_m$. The set $S$ of the exponents of its terms can be infinite, and Lemma 3.15.4 allows this. As before, the exponent $q_*$ of $\mu_0$ is the only point of $S$ with the least value, since each term with $x_m^k$, $k \ge 1$, has a value at least $w_k > v(\mu_0)$. For $m' \ge m_0$, each term $Y x_m^k$ with $Y \ne \mu_0$ goes to a series whose monomials are $\pi(Y) \eta \, x_{m'}^l$, with $\eta$ of nonnegative exponents and both exponents of $\pi(Y)$ larger than those of $\mu_0' = \pi(\mu_0)$. The sum of these series converges in $K[[x_{m'}, y_{m'}, z_{m'}]]$, since $\pi$ is a map of power series rings. So $\pi(P^{\mathrm{cl}}) = \mu_0' (1 + f)$ with $f$ in the maximal ideal, and the rest of the proof is the same. $\square$

Lemma 3.15.2 and Lemma 3.15.4 do not use that $g$ is a polynomial in $x$. So all the results of Section 3.15 up to Proposition 3.15.7 hold for $g \in K[[x, y, z]]$.

### Values along the translations

Assume that an infinite number of the $t_j$ are not $0$. In this section the letter $i$ is the index of the Hasse derivatives, so we write $a_\ell$ and $\gamma_\ell$ for the $a_i$ and $\gamma_i$ of Section 3.15. So $a_\ell = \varphi_{j_\ell + 1}$, $\gamma_\ell = v(M_{j_{\ell+1} + 1})$, and $v(a_k - a_\ell) = \gamma_\ell$ for $k > \ell \ge 1$. Also $v(a_\ell) = \gamma_0$ for each $\ell \ge 1$. We call $m = j_\ell + 1$ a *good* index. At a good index, $\varphi_m = a_\ell$ and $v(M_m) = \gamma_{\ell - 1}$. Between two nonzero translations, $\varphi_m$ does not change.

We use the Hahn field $\mathbb{K}$ and the embedding $\hat H \subseteq \mathbb{K}$ of Lemma 3.15.6. Put $\varphi_\infty = \sum_j t_j M_{j+1}$, $\delta_m = \varphi_\infty - \varphi_m = \sum_{j \ge m} t_j M_{j+1}$, $\Phi_m = \varphi_m^p$ and $\Phi_\infty = \varphi_\infty^p$. The terms of $\delta_m$ have the values $v(M_{j+1})$ with $j \ge m$, so $v(\delta_m) > v(M_m)$, and $v(\delta_m) \to 1 + \theta$.

Sums in $\mathbb{K}$. A family of elements of $\mathbb{K}$ is *summable* if the union of their supports is well ordered and each exponent is in the support of only a finite number of them. Then the sum is defined exponent by exponent, and a summable family can be grouped in any way. Let $A \subseteq \Gamma$ be a well ordered set of nonnegative values, and let $B \subseteq \Gamma$ be a well ordered set of positive values. By the lemma of Neumann (B. H. Neumann, On ordered division rings, Trans. Amer. Math. Soc. 66 (1949)), the set of the sums $\alpha + \beta_1 + \dots + \beta_r$ with $\alpha \in A$, $r \ge 0$ and $\beta_s \in B$ is well ordered, and each element of it is such a sum in only a finite number of ways. So if $f = \sum_k c_k X^k$ has coefficients $c_k \in K[[y_N, z_N]]$, and $a, b \in \mathbb{K}$ have positive values, then $f(a)$ is defined in $\mathbb{K}$ and Taylor's formula $f(a + b) = \sum_k f^{[k]}(a) b^k$ holds in $\mathbb{K}$. Here $A$ is the set of the values of the monomials in $y_N, z_N$ with nonnegative exponents, and $B$ is the union of the supports of $a$ and $b$. If $a \in \hat H$, then $f(a)$ is also the limit in $\hat H$ of the partial sums, and the two agree, since the embedding of Lemma 3.15.6 keeps $v$. The map $\operatorname{cl}$ commutes with the sum of a summable family, since it acts on each exponent.

The first lemma says that a power series has a constant value along the translations. For a polynomial this is the transcendental type. For a power series we reduce to a polynomial by the Weierstrass preparation theorem.

**Lemma 3.16.2.** Let $f = \sum_k c_k X^k \in \hat H[[X]]$ be nonzero with $v(c_k) \ge c$ for each $k$ and a constant $c$. Then $f(a_\ell)$ converges in $\hat H$ for each $\ell$, and $v(f(a_\ell))$ is finite and constant for $\ell$ large.

*Proof.* The idea is to scale $X$ so that the $a_\ell$ lie on the unit circle, and then to split $f$ into a polynomial and a unit. Let $\pi$ be a Laurent monomial in $y_N, z_N$ with $v(\pi) = \gamma_0$, and put $s_\ell = a_\ell / \pi$. Then $v(s_\ell) = 0$. Since $v(c_k \pi^k) \ge c + k \gamma_0 \to \infty$, the series $h_0(S) = f(\pi S)$ is an element of the Tate algebra $\hat H \langle S \rangle$ of the power series whose coefficients tend to $0$. The field $\hat H$ is complete for the absolute value $|u| = e^{-v(u)}$, and this absolute value is not trivial and not archimedean. Also $f(a_\ell) = h_0(s_\ell)$ converges.

The coefficients of $h_0$ tend to $0$ and are not all $0$, so one of them has the largest absolute value. Call it $c'$, and put $h = h_0 / c'$. Then the Gauss norm of $h$ is $1$, and the reduction of $h$ modulo the elements of norm less than $1$ is a nonzero polynomial in $K[S]$. Let $s$ be its degree. Then $h$ is distinguished of degree $s$ in the sense of [Bosch, Güntzer, Remmert, Non-Archimedean Analysis, Springer, 1984, Section 5.2.1]. By the Weierstrass preparation theorem [ibid., Section 5.2.2, Theorem 1], $h = P E$ with $P \in \hat H[S]$ monic of degree $s$ and $E$ a unit of $\hat H \langle S \rangle$. The Gauss norm is multiplicative, and $|h| = |P| = 1$. So $|E| = 1$ and $|E^{-1}| = 1$. For $|u| \le 1$ this gives $|E(u)| \le 1$ and $|E(u)^{-1}| \le 1$, so $|E(u)| = 1$.

Thus $v(f(a_\ell)) = v(c') + v(P(s_\ell)) = v(c') + v(\tilde P(a_\ell))$ with $\tilde P(X) = P(X / \pi) \in \hat H[X]$, $\tilde P \ne 0$. By Proposition 3.15.7, $v(\tilde P(a_\ell))$ is constant for $\ell$ large. The $a_\ell$ are distinct and $\tilde P$ has only a finite number of roots, so this constant is finite. $\square$

The next lemma is the inequality of Kaplansky for the series in $X^p$ that occur in our class. Its part 2 is the main tool of this section. It says that the value of $G$ along the translations is less than each term of the Taylor expansion of $G$ at the next point.

**Lemma 3.16.3.** Let $G(Y) = C + \sum_{k \ge 1} b_k Y^k$ with $C \in H$ and $b_k \in K[[y_N, z_N]]$, and assume that $G$ is not constant. For $i \ge 0$ put $\beta^G_i = \lim_m v(G^{[i]}(\Phi_m))$, with $\beta^G_i = \infty$ if $G^{[i]} = 0$, and put $\lambda_G(\gamma) = \min_{i \ge 1} (\beta^G_i + p i \gamma)$.

1. The limits exist, and $\beta^G_i$ is finite if $G^{[i]} \ne 0$. For each bound, only a finite number of $i$ have $\beta^G_i + p i \gamma_0$ below it. The function $\lambda_G$ is finite, continuous and strictly increasing on $[\gamma_0, 1 + \theta]$, and there is $c_G < 1 + \theta$ such that for $\gamma \in (c_G, 1 + \theta)$ the minimum is at a unique $i$.
2. There is $m_1$ such that for each $m \ge m_1$ and each $i \ge 1$, $$\beta^G_0 < \lambda_G(v(M_m)) \le v(G^{[i]}(\Phi_m)) + p i \, v(M_m).$$
3. $v(G(\Phi_\infty)) = \beta^G_0$, and $G(\Phi_\infty)$ and $G(\Phi_m)$ have the same leading term for $m$ large.

*Proof.* Part 1. For $i \ge 1$ with $G^{[i]} \ne 0$, apply Lemma 3.16.2 to $f(X) = G^{[i]}(X^p)$. Its coefficients are $C$ or are in $K[[y_N, z_N]]$, so their values are bounded below. This gives the limit along the $a_\ell$. Since $\varphi_m$ is constant between two nonzero translations, the limit is also the limit in $m$. The same holds for $i = 0$. For $i \ge 1$ the constant $C$ does not occur in $G^{[i]}$, so $\beta^G_i \ge 0$. Thus $\beta^G_i + p i \gamma \ge p i \gamma_0$ for $\gamma \ge \gamma_0$, and only a finite number of $i$ give values below a bound. Some $b_k \ne 0$ with $k \ge 1$, so $G^{[k]} \ne 0$, and $\lambda_G$ is finite. On $[\gamma_0, 1 + \theta]$, $\lambda_G$ is the minimum of a finite number of lines with positive slopes. So it is continuous and strictly increasing. Two distinct lines meet at most once, so near $1 + \theta$ and to the left of it no two of these lines meet, and one of them is below the others.

Part 2. Let $B = \lambda_G(1 + \theta) + 1$, and let $I$ be the finite set of the $i \ge 1$ with $p i \gamma_0 \le B$. Choose $\ell_0$ such that for $\ell \ge \ell_0$, $\gamma_\ell > c_G$, $v(G(a_\ell^p)) = \beta^G_0$, and $v(G^{[i]}(a_\ell^p)) = \beta^G_i$ for each $i \in I$. Let $\sigma > \ell \ge \ell_0$. Since $a_\sigma^p - a_\ell^p = (a_\sigma - a_\ell)^p$, Taylor's formula gives $$G(a_\sigma^p) - G(a_\ell^p) = \sum_{i \ge 1} G^{[i]}(a_\ell^p) (a_\sigma - a_\ell)^{p i},$$ and the term $i$ has the value $v(G^{[i]}(a_\ell^p)) + p i \gamma_\ell$. For $i \in I$ this is $\beta^G_i + p i \gamma_\ell$. For $i \notin I$ it is more than $B > \lambda_G(\gamma_\ell)$. By part 1 one term has the least value $\lambda_G(\gamma_\ell)$, so the difference has the value $\lambda_G(\gamma_\ell)$. Both $G(a_\sigma^p)$ and $G(a_\ell^p)$ have the value $\beta^G_0$, so $\lambda_G(\gamma_\ell) \ge \beta^G_0$. Since $\lambda_G$ is strictly increasing, $\lambda_G(\gamma_{\ell + 1}) > \beta^G_0$.

Now let $m$ be large, with $j_\ell < m \le j_{\ell + 1}$ and $\ell > \ell_0$. Then $v(M_m) \ge v(M_{j_\ell + 1}) = \gamma_{\ell - 1}$, so $\lambda_G(v(M_m)) \ge \lambda_G(\gamma_{\ell - 1}) > \beta^G_0$. For $i \in I$, $v(G^{[i]}(\Phi_m)) + p i \, v(M_m) = \beta^G_i + p i \, v(M_m) \ge \lambda_G(v(M_m))$. For $i \notin I$, $v(G^{[i]}(\Phi_m)) + p i \, v(M_m) \ge p i \gamma_0 > B > \lambda_G(v(M_m))$.

Part 3. In $\mathbb{K}$, $G(\Phi_\infty) - G(\Phi_m) = \sum_{i \ge 1} G^{[i]}(\Phi_m) \delta_m^{p i}$, by Taylor's formula and $\Phi_\infty - \Phi_m = \delta_m^p$. Since $v(\delta_m) > v(M_m)$, part 2 shows that each term has a value larger than $\beta^G_0$, and the terms with $i \notin I$ have values larger than $B$. So the difference has a value larger than $\beta^G_0 = v(G(\Phi_m))$, and the leading terms are the same. $\square$

### The class and its limit data

In this part and the next two, $g = \mu x + F(x^p)$ is clean at $P_N$, with $\mu$ zero or a nonzero constant times a monomial in $y, z$, and $F(Y) = \sum_{k \ge 0} B_k Y^k$ with $B_k \in K[[y, z]]$. So $g \in K[[x, y, z]]$, and Lemma 3.16.1 applies. Since $g$ is clean, no monomial of a $B_k$ is a $p$-th power.

The expansion. By Lucas's theorem, $\binom{p k}{l} \equiv 0 \bmod p$ if $p \nmid l$, and $\binom{p k}{p i} \equiv \binom{k}{i}$. So $g^{[1]} = \mu$, $g^{[p i]}(x) = F^{[i]}(x^p)$ for $i \ge 1$, and the other Hasse derivatives of order at least $1$ are $0$. Thus in Lemma 3.16.1 $$A_0 = \mu \varphi_m + F(\Phi_m), \qquad A_1 = \mu M_m, \qquad A_{p i} = M_m^{p i} F^{[i]}(\Phi_m),$$ and $A_k = 0$ for the other $k$. Each monomial of $F^{[i]}(\Phi_m)$ is a monomial of some $B_k$ times the $p$-th power of a Laurent monomial. So no monomial of $F^{[i]}(\Phi_m)$ is a $p$-th power, $\operatorname{cl} A_{p i} = A_{p i}$, and $w_{p i} = v(A_{p i}) = p i \, v(M_m) + v(F^{[i]}(\Phi_m))$. Also $w_1 = v(\mu M_m)$, with $w_1 = \infty$ if $\mu = 0$. The same argument applies to $F^{[i]}(\Phi_\infty)$ in $\mathbb{K}$.

A nonzero translation $t_j$ is *twisted* if $\mu M_{j+1}$ is not a $p$-th power, and *free* if it is. If $\mu = 0$, each nonzero translation is free. Let $\varphi_m^{\mathrm{tw}}$ and $\delta_m^{\mathrm{tw}}$ be the sums of the twisted terms of $\varphi_m$ and of $\delta_m$. The terms $\mu t_j M_{j+1}$ are distinct monomials, so $\operatorname{cl}(\mu \varphi_m) = \mu \varphi_m^{\mathrm{tw}}$ and $\operatorname{cl}(\mu \delta_m) = \mu \delta_m^{\mathrm{tw}}$. Thus $$V_m = \operatorname{cl} A_0 = \mu \varphi_m^{\mathrm{tw}} + F(\Phi_m), \qquad g_m = \mu M_m x_m + V_m + \sum_{i \ge 1} A_{p i} x_m^{p i}.$$ By Lemma 3.16.1, $M_m^{-p} g_m$ is the function of $P_m$, so $M_m^{-p} V_m \in K[[y_m, z_m]]$. By Lemma 3.13.24, part 2, $V_m$ has only a finite number of monomials below each value. For $f \in \mathbb{K}$ let $f_{< c}$ be the sum of the terms of $f$ with a value less than $c$. So $(V_m)_{< c}$ is a finite sum, and it is an element of $H$.

The limit data. Assume that an infinite number of the $t_j$ are not $0$, and that $F$ is not constant if $\mu = 0$. Put $$\beta_1 = v(\mu), \qquad \beta_{p i} = v(F^{[i]}(\Phi_\infty)), \qquad W_k = \beta_k + k (1 + \theta), \qquad W = \min_k W_k, \qquad \mathcal{T} = \{k : W_k = W\},$$ for $k = 1$ and $k = p i$ with $i \ge 1$, with $v(0) = \infty$. By Lemma 3.16.3, part 3, for $G = F^{[i]}$, and by Lemma 3.16.2, $\beta_{p i}$ is the limit of $v(F^{[i]}(\Phi_m))$, and it is finite if $F^{[i]} \ne 0$. So $W_1$ and $W_{p i}$ are the limits of $w_1$ and of $w_{p i}$ at the good indices. Since $\beta_{p i} \ge 0$, $W_{p i} \ge p i (1 + \theta)$, so $W$ is a minimum over a finite set. By the hypothesis, $W < \infty$. So $\mathcal{T}$ is finite and not empty. Put $\lambda = \lambda_F$ if $F$ is not constant. Then $\lambda(1 + \theta) = \min_{i \ge 1} W_{p i}$, $\lambda(\gamma) < \min_{i \ge 1} W_{p i}$ for $\gamma < 1 + \theta$, and $\lambda(v(M_m)) \to \min_{i \ge 1} W_{p i}$.

For $k \in \mathcal{T}$ let $e_k$ be the leading term of $g^{[k]}(\varphi_\infty)$. So $e_1 = \mu$ and $e_{p i}$ is the leading term of $F^{[i]}(\Phi_\infty)$. The term $e_k (y_N z_N)^k$ has the value $\beta_k + k (1 + \theta) = W$. Since $\theta$ is irrational, two monomials with the same value differ by a constant factor. So there are a monomial $E$ and constants $c_k \in K^\times$ with $$e_k = c_k E (y_N z_N)^{-k}, \qquad k \in \mathcal{T}.$$ The class of $e_{p i}$ modulo $p$ is not $0$, since $F^{[i]}(\Phi_\infty)$ has no monomial that is a $p$-th power.

The next lemma is the main new step. It says that the tie set has no index $p i$ with $i$ not a power of $p$. The idea: if $i = p^a s$ with $s > 1$, then $G = F^{[p^a]}$ has the Hasse derivative $s F^{[i]}$, so Lemma 3.16.3 for $G$ compares the lines of $p^{a+1}$ and $p i$. The two lines are affine in $\gamma$, the line of $p i$ is steeper, and it is above at the points $v(M_m) < 1 + \theta$. So it is also above at $1 + \theta$.

**Lemma 3.16.4.** Assume that an infinite number of the $t_j$ are not $0$. Let $i = p^a s$ with $s > 1$ and $p \nmid s$, and assume $F^{[i]} \ne 0$. Then $W_{p^{a+1}} < W_{p i}$. So $\mathcal{T} \setminus \{1\} \subseteq \{p, p^2, p^3, \dots\}$.

*Proof.* Put $G = F^{[p^a]}$. By the identity $(F^{[u]})^{[l]} = \binom{u + l}{u} F^{[u + l]}$ for the Hasse derivatives, $G^{[i - p^a]} = \binom{i}{p^a} F^{[i]}$. The digits of $p^a$ in base $p$ are a $1$ at the place $a$ and zeros, and the digit of $i$ at the place $a$ is the last digit of $s$. So Lucas's theorem gives $\binom{p^a s}{p^a} \equiv s \not\equiv 0 \bmod p$, and $G^{[i - p^a]} = s F^{[i]} \ne 0$. Thus $G$ is not constant, $\beta^G_0 = \beta_{p^{a+1}}$ and $\beta^G_{i - p^a} = \beta_{p i}$, both finite. Lemma 3.16.3, part 2, for $G$ gives, for $m$ large, $$\beta_{p^{a+1}} < \lambda_G(v(M_m)) \le \beta_{p i} + p (i - p^a) \, v(M_m).$$ Put $D(\gamma) = \beta_{p i} + p (i - p^a) \gamma - \beta_{p^{a+1}}$. This is an affine function with the slope $p (i - p^a) > 0$, and $D(v(M_m)) > 0$ with $v(M_m) < 1 + \theta$. So $D(1 + \theta) > 0$. Add $p^{a+1} (1 + \theta)$ to both sides of $\beta_{p^{a+1}} < \beta_{p i} + p (i - p^a)(1 + \theta)$. This gives $W_{p^{a+1}} < W_{p i}$. Then $W \le W_{p^{a+1}} < W_{p i}$, so $p i \notin \mathcal{T}$. Each $k \in \mathcal{T}$ with $k \ne 1$ is $p i$ with $F^{[i]} \ne 0$, so $i$ is a power of $p$. $\square$

The proof needs the strict inequality at one point to the left of $1 + \theta$ and the slope. The limit of the strict inequalities alone gives only $W_{p^{a+1}} \le W_{p i}$, and this is not sufficient for Theorem 3.16.10.

**Example 3.16.5.** Let $p$ be odd and $F = B_1 Y + B_2 Y^2$ with $B_2 \ne 0$. Then $F^{[1]} = B_1 + 2 B_2 Y$, $F^{[2]} = B_2$, and Lemma 3.16.4 with $i = 2$, $a = 0$ and $s = 2$ says $W_p < W_{2p}$, that is, $$\beta_p < v(B_2) + p (1 + \theta), \qquad \beta_p = v(B_1 + 2 B_2 \Phi_\infty).$$ We check this by hand where we can. The element $B_1 + 2 B_2 \Phi_\infty$ is not $0$, since $\beta_p$ is finite by Lemma 3.16.2. If the leading terms of $B_1$ and $2 B_2 \Phi_\infty$ do not cancel, then $\beta_p \le v(B_2) + p \, v(\varphi_\infty) = v(B_2) + p \gamma_0 < v(B_2) + p (1 + \theta)$, as the lemma says. If they cancel, then $\beta_p$ can be larger than both values, and the elementary bound fails. Here the lemma uses the run: by Lemma 3.16.3 for $G = F^{[1]}$, the value of $B_1 + 2 B_2 \Phi_m$ stays below $v(B_2) + p \, v(M_m)$ at each late point, and the limit of $v(M_m)$ is $1 + \theta$. So $W_{2p}$ is never in the tie set of a run with an infinite number of nonzero translations.

### The identity below W

The next two results compare $V_m$, which has only a finite number of terms below $W$, with the Taylor expansion of $g$ at $\varphi_\infty$. The first is the analogue of Lemma 3.15.9, part 2.

**Lemma 3.16.6.** Assume that an infinite number of the $t_j$ are not $0$, and that $F$ is not constant if $\mu = 0$. Then $\operatorname{cl}(g(\varphi_\infty))$ has no term with a value less than $W$.

*Proof.* Put $\omega(m) = \min_{k \ge 1} w_k(m)$. By Lemma 3.16.3, part 2, for $G = F$, $w_{p i}(m) \ge \lambda(v(M_m))$ for each $i \ge 1$ and $m$ large, with $\lambda = \infty$ if $F$ is constant. So $\omega(m) \ge \min(v(\mu M_m), \lambda(v(M_m)))$, and the right side tends to $\min(W_1, \min_i W_{p i}) = W$. By Lemma 3.16.1, (★) fails at $P_m$, so $V_m = 0$ or $v(V_m) \ge \omega(m)$. **Proposition 3.16.7.** Assume that an infinite number of the $t_j$ are not $0$, and that $F$ is not constant if $\mu = 0$. Put $[1 \in \mathcal{T}] = 1$ if $1 \in \mathcal{T}$ and $0$ if not. The right side of (1) has, a priori, an infinite number of terms below $W$, one or more for each $j \ge m$ with $t_j \ne 0$. The left side has a finite number. The three theorems below find a contradiction in each case of $\mathcal{T}$. If $\mathcal{T}$ contains a $k = p i$ with $i$ not a power of $p$, the terms of $\delta_m^k$ are products of distinct monomials, and they can cancel. Lemma 3.16.4 excludes this case, and Example 3.16.11 below shows that the cancellation is a real danger.

Let $g_x \ne 0$. By Proposition 3.15.10 and Corollary 3.15.11 there are an $m$ and a free coordinate $x''$ at $P_m$ with $g_m = \mu_m x'' + Q(x''^p, y_m, z_m)$, where $\mu_m$ is a nonzero constant times a monomial. By Lemma 3.15.1, the function of $P_m$ is $f = M_m^{-p} g_m = \mu' x'' + M_m^{-p} Q$ with $\mu' = M_m^{-p} \mu_m$. As a series in $x'', y_m, z_m$, the coefficient of $x''$ in $f$ is $\mu'$, since $Q$ has only powers of $x''^p$. So $\mu'$ is a nonzero constant times a monomial with nonnegative exponents. Let $f''$ be the cleaning of $f$ in the coordinates $(x'', y_m, z_m)$. It keeps $\mu' x''$, and it removes monomials of $M_m^{-p} Q$ only, so $f'' = \mu' x'' + F''(x''^p)$ with $F'' \in K[[y_m, z_m]][[Y]]$, and $f''$ is clean. Also $f - f''$ is a $p$-th power, since $K$ is perfect. By Lemma 3.15.2, parts 3 and 5, the run from $P_m$ is a corner run with free translations of $f''$ in the coordinates $(x'', y_m, z_m)$, with the same $\rho$. By Lemma 3.16.12, it has an infinite number of nonzero translations. This contradicts Theorem 3.16.13, applied at $P_m$ in these coordinates.

So only a finite number of the $t_m$ are not $0$. From $P_{J+1}$ on, each step is at the origin of the $y$-chart or of the $z$-chart, and each chart occurs an infinite number of times. The point $g_{J+1}$ is clean, $V(x_{J+1})$ is not in $\Delta$, and the run keeps $\rho$. These are the hypotheses of Theorem 3.13.20. $\square$

The theorem contains Theorems 3.13.27, 3.15.16 and 3.15.17 as far as the finiteness of the translations is concerned. It says nothing about the drop of $\rho$: Example 3.15.18 is a run of this kind with $\rho = 1$ at each point. It does not prove the conjecture V of Section 3.15 either, since the polynomial $\varphi_{J+1}$ need not be a polynomial in $y_N, z_N$. Example 3.16.11 shows that the monomials $M_{j+1}$ of a run can have negative exponents in $y_N, z_N$.

### Remarks

**Remark 3.16.15 (a second proof by transcendence).** The consequence $\mathcal{T} \setminus \{1\} \subseteq \{p, p^2, \dots\}$ of Lemma 3.16.4 has a second proof, which uses the transcendence of $\varphi_\infty$ and not the inequality of Kaplansky. Since the method can be useful in other regimes, we give it.

The first step is that $\varphi_\infty$ is transcendental over $\hat H$, where $\hat H \subseteq \mathbb{K}$ by Lemma 3.15.6. It is a pseudo limit of $(a_\ell)$, that is, $v(\varphi_\infty - a_\ell) = \gamma_\ell$, but it is not a limit in $\hat H$. If $P \in \hat H[X]$ is nonzero with $P(\varphi_\infty) = 0$, write $P = c \prod_s (X - \alpha_s)$ in an algebraic closure of $\mathbb{K}$, with $\alpha_1 = \varphi_\infty$. As in Step 2 of the proof of Proposition 3.15.7, each value $v(a_\ell - \alpha_s)$ is constant for $\ell$ large or equal to $\gamma_\ell$, and for $\alpha_1$ it is $\gamma_\ell$. So $v(P(a_\ell))$ increases strictly for $\ell$ large, and this contradicts the transcendental type. Since $\varphi_m \in H$, it follows that $\delta_m$ and $\delta_m^p$ are transcendental over $H$ for each $m$.

The second step is identity (1) at two points $m_0 \le m < m'$ with a nonzero translation between them. Put $\sigma = \varphi_{m'} - \varphi_m = \delta_m - \delta_{m'}$, a nonzero Laurent polynomial, and $\sigma' = \sigma / (y_N z_N)$. Then $\delta_m^{\mathrm{tw}} = \delta_{m'}^{\mathrm{tw}} + \sigma^{\mathrm{tw}}$. Subtract (1) at $m'$ from (1) at $m$. The twisted parts cancel up to the finite sum $\sigma^{\mathrm{tw}}$, and since $(\delta_{m'} + \sigma)^p = \delta_{m'}^p + \sigma^p$, the result is $S(\delta_{m'}^p) \in H$, with $$S(U) = \sum_{i \in I} (-1)^{p i} e_{p i} \bigl[(U + \sigma^p)^i - U^i\bigr] \in H[U], \qquad I = \{i : p i \in \mathcal{T}\}.$$ The coefficient of $U^l$ is $E (y_N z_N)^{-p l} \sum_{i \in I, \, i > l} (-1)^{p i} c_{p i} \binom{i}{l} Z^{i - l}$ with $Z = \sigma'^p$. The terms of $\sigma'$ have negative values, so $Z \notin K$, and $Z$ is transcendental over $K$. The third step is a lemma of Lucas type: for $c_i \in K^\times$ and $Z$ transcendental over $K$, the polynomial $\sum_{i \in I} c_i [(U + Z)^i - U^i]$ is not constant if and only if some $i \in I$ is not a power of $p$, and if $i_0 = p^a s \in I$ with $s \ge 2$ and $p \nmid s$, then its coefficient of $U^{p^a}$ is not $0$. Indeed, the powers $Z^{i - l}$ are distinct, $\binom{p^e}{l} \equiv 0$ for $0 < l < p^e$, and $\binom{p^a s}{p^a} \equiv s$. So if $I$ contains an $i$ that is not a power of $p$, then $S(U) - S(\delta_{m'}^p)$ is a nonzero polynomial over $H$ with the root $\delta_{m'}^p$, and this contradicts the first step. This is Theorem D of note 36.

The first instance is $p = 3$ and $\mathcal{T} \supseteq \{1, 3, 6, 9\}$, so $I \supseteq \{1, 2, 3\}$. For $I = \{1, 2, 3\}$ the coefficient of $U$ is $$E (y_N z_N)^{-3} (2 c_6 \sigma'^3 - 3 c_9 \sigma'^6) = 2 c_6 E (y_N z_N)^{-3} \sigma'^3,$$ since $3 = 0$ in $K$, and the coefficient of $U^2$ is $3 c_9 E (y_N z_N)^{-6} \sigma'^3 = 0$. So (1) at two points forces $\delta_{m'}^3 \in H$. In words: the cross terms $2 e_6 \delta_{m'}^3 \sigma^3$ of $\delta_m^6$ are linear in $\delta_{m'}^3$, and a linear relation over $H$ is not possible. These are the cross terms of Example 3.16.11.

**Remark 3.16.16 (one point of the identity).** The identity (1) at one point and the transcendence of $\delta_m$ also give the following, under the hypothesis that an infinite number of the $t_j$ are not $0$. First, $1 \in \mathcal{T}$: if not, (1) says that a nonzero polynomial $\sum_{k \in \mathcal{T}} e_k (-X)^k$ takes a value in $H$ at $\delta_m$. This is a second proof of Theorem 3.16.9, also for $\mu = 0$. Second, an infinite number of the nonzero translations are free: if not, then $\delta_m^{\mathrm{tw}} = \delta_m$ for $m$ large, and the coefficient of $X$ in the polynomial is $-\mu \ne 0$. Third, if $\mathcal{T} \ne \{1\}$, only a finite number of the twisted translations have $M_{j+1}$ of a class other than $(1, 1)$ modulo $p$ in $y_N, z_N$, since all $e_k$ with $k \ge p$ have the class of $E = \mu y_N z_N$ up to a constant. The transcendence does not exclude the case of an infinite number of free translations, since $\mu \delta_m^{\mathrm{fr}}$ is a sum of $p$-th powers and is not a polynomial in $\delta_m$. This is the case where Theorems 3.16.8 and 3.16.10 need the dominance lemma or the primitive vectors.

**Remark 3.16.17.** Computer tests over $\mathbb{F}_2$, $\mathbb{F}_3$, $\mathbb{F}_5$ and $\mathbb{F}_7$ agree with the results of this section. Two independent sets of scripts did the tests, with no shared code. The translations of the tests are in $\mathbb{F}_p$ and not in $K$, the words of charts are periodic, and the searches have a cap on the depth. A test can see only a finite part of a run, so it cannot see the limit set $\mathcal{T}$, and the tests are not a proof.

1. The class $\mu x + F(x^p)$. A depth first search over all translations that keep $\rho$, along the words $yz$, $yyz$, $yzz$, $yyzyz$ and $yzzz$, from random clean starts of four kinds for $p = 2, 3, 5$, did $152500$ runs and found $28272$ branches of the depth $40$ or $60$. No branch had more than $3$ nonzero translations, and no branch had a nonzero translation in its second half. Designed starts with $\mu (x - \varphi_0) + B_1 (x - \varphi_0)^p$ took the designed translations and then no more.
2. The closed form of Lemma 3.16.1. The transform step by step agreed with $\operatorname{cl}(g(\varphi_m + M_m x_m))$ in $24000$ checks for $p = 2, 3, 5$ in the first set of scripts, and in $1176$, $1188$, $2340$, $2400$, $2400$ and $2400$ checks in the second set. A version without the cleaning in the closed form gave $278$ mismatches of $500$, and $521$ of $1188$, so the test can see errors.
3. Lemmas 3.16.3 and 3.16.4. For random $F$ and random sequences with $6$ to $9$ nonzero translations, part 2 of Lemma 3.16.3 had no failure in $4216$ checks, and the inequalities of Lemma 3.16.4 had no failure in $4440$ checks of the weak form and $1878$ checks of the strong form. This test is weak, since the leading terms are often stable from the start.
4. Searches near a tie. Starts with an exact tie of the start data, $W_1 = W_p = W_{2p}$ and others, in the coordinate $x - \varphi_0$, took the designed translations and then no more: $13500$ starts with $2886$ deep branches and $4400$ designed starts with $3056$ deep branches in the first set, and $112971$ runs with $47968$ deep branches of the depth $60$ or $80$ for $p = 2, 3, 5, 7$ in the second set. No deep branch had a late nonzero translation. By Lemma 3.16.4 such a tie is never the limit data of a run with an infinite number of nonzero translations, so these tests only show the behavior near a tie.
5. The Lucas lemma of Remark 3.16.15. For all sets $I \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}$ with at most $K$ elements and random $c_i$, "not constant" agreed with "some $i$ is not a power of $p$": $61100$ sets in the first set, and $2625$, $19650$, $7875$, $7875$, $31930$ and $222050$ cases in the second set. The identity $S(\delta_{m'}^p)$ of the two points agreed exactly in $200$ random trials for each $p = 2, 3, 5, 7$.
6. Example 3.16.11. A script checked the table, the relation $M_m y_m z_m = y_N z_N$, the primitivity of the vectors $n_{N+s}$ and the four relations $n_{N+s} + n_{N+s+4} = 3 n_{N+s+2}$.

### Notes and references

The results of this section are a result of the milestone S7.18 of this project (tamnd/singularity#245). They come from notes 34 to 37 of the research notes. Note 34 proved Lemmas 3.16.2, 3.16.3 and 3.16.12 and Theorems 3.16.8 and 3.16.9, and proved the case of Theorem 3.16.10 with $\mathcal{T} \setminus \{1\}$ in the powers of $p$ by a chain argument. Note 35 is the referee report on note 34. It added the Weierstrass details of Lemma 3.16.2, the case $\mu = 0$ of Theorem 3.16.9, and the strong form of Lemma 3.16.4, which closes the case that note 34 left open. Note 36 gave the second proof of Remark 3.16.15 and the results of Remark 3.16.16, and note 37 is the referee report on note 36. Note 37 also checked the strong form of Lemma 3.16.4 a second time. We state the strong form as the main proof, since it is shorter, and the method of note 36 as a remark. The proof of Theorem 3.16.10 by the primitive vectors $n_{j+1}$ is new in this section. It replaces the chain argument of note 34, which bounds the depth of the chains by the $p$-adic valuation of the vectors. Since the vectors are primitive, this depth is $0$, and no chain occurs. Lemma 3.16.1 writes out the extension of Lemma 3.15.1 and Proposition 3.15.5 to power series in $x$, which notes 35 and 37 state with the remark that the proofs do not change.

The outside results are the following. The pseudo Cauchy sequences, the two types and the inequality behind Lemma 3.16.3 are from the paper of Kaplansky, Maximal fields with valuations, Duke Math. J. 9 (1942), and the step from algebraic type to an immediate algebraic extension is its Theorem 3. The generalized stability theorem is [Kuh10, Theorem 1.1], and the facts that a henselization of a defectless field is algebraically maximal and that a henselization of a field with a valuation of rank $1$ lies in its completion are in [Kuh-book]. The Weierstrass preparation theorem in the Tate algebra over a complete field with a nontrivial absolute value that is not archimedean is in S. Bosch, U. Güntzer and R. Remmert, Non-Archimedean Analysis, Springer, 1984, Section 5.2.2, Theorem 1, with the definition of a distinguished series in Section 5.2.1. The summability in the Hahn field is the lemma of B. H. Neumann, On ordered division rings, Trans. Amer. Math. Soc. 66 (1949). The use of the history of the divisor is from [CJS20]. None of the results has a Lean proof yet.

Several questions are open.

1. The conjecture V of Section 3.15 is open. Theorem 3.16.14 gives a last nonzero translation $t_J$, but it does not say that $\varphi_{J+1}$ is a polynomial in $y_N, z_N$ with nonnegative exponents.
2. The results are for the corner runs with free translations in three variables. The other regimes of note 30, for example the runs that leave a component of $\Delta$ an infinite number of times, are not treated.
3. For $n \ge 4$ the value group has a rational rank larger than $2$, and Lemma 3.15.6 and Proposition 3.15.7 must be proved again before the method applies.
4. Proposition 3.15.7 rests on the cited theorems of Kuhlmann and Kaplansky and on the facts on the henselization. The exact statements and numbers of these sources were not checked again against the sources for this section.
5. Theorem 3.16.14 gives the finiteness of the translations and not the end of the run. A run with only a finite number of translations need not end, as Example 3.15.18 shows, and its centers must come from the corner curves of Theorem 3.13.20.
