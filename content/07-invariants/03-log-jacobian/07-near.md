## The points where the log-Jacobian order stays

Theorem 3.2.4 says that the residual order $\rho$ of the log-Jacobian ideal does not go up under a point blowup. A resolution invariant must also go down, and the first question is where it can stay. For the order of a hypersurface, Theorem 2.2.5 gives the answer of Hironaka: the points where the order stays lie in the projective space of the directrix of the initial form. In this section we prove the analogue for $\rho$. The points where $\rho$ stays lie in the directrix of the initial forms of the elements of order $\rho$ of a residual ideal. The ideal is $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ or $\widetilde\Theta(G)$, and the choice depends on the order of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$. An example shows that the ideal $\widetilde\Theta(G)$ is too large in the first case.

We use the setting of Section 3.2. So $K$ is algebraically closed, $g \in K[x]$ is clean for $\Delta$, $\operatorname{ord} g \ge p$, and $\pi = \pi_{1,t}$ is the chart at a point of the exceptional divisor, with the new divisor $\Delta'$. Write $g = x^r G$ with $d = \operatorname{ord} G$, let $\rho = \rho(g)$, and let $\sigma = \operatorname{ord} \widetilde\Theta_0(G)$, where $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ is the ideal of the proof of Theorem 3.2.4. We saw there that $\sigma = \rho$ or $\sigma = \rho + 1$. The vector of the chart is $v = (1, t_2, \dots, t_n)$, as in Theorem 2.2.5. For an element $h$ of order $s$ we write $\operatorname{in}(h)$ for its homogeneous part of degree $s$.

### The theorem

**Theorem 3.7.1.** Let $g'_c$ be the clean part of $g'$ for $\Delta'$, and assume that $\rho(g'_c) = \rho$.

1. If $\sigma = \rho$, then $v \in T(\operatorname{in}(h))$ for each $h \in \widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ with $\operatorname{ord} h = \rho$.
2. If $\sigma = \rho + 1$, then $v \in T(\operatorname{in}(h))$ for each $h \in \widetilde\Theta(G)$ with $\operatorname{ord} h = \rho$.

*Proof.* Let $h$ be as in part 1 or part 2. Put $h_0 = h$ in part 1 and $h_0 = x_1 h$ in part 2. In both cases $h_0 \in \widetilde\Theta_0(G)$, because $\mathfrak{m} \widetilde\Theta(G) \subseteq \widetilde\Theta_0(G)$, and $\operatorname{ord} h_0 = \sigma$. Let $W$ be the ideal of the proof of Theorem 3.2.4, generated by the elements $x_1^{-\sigma} \pi(k)$ with $k \in \widetilde\Theta_0(G)$. Since $\pi(x_1) = x_1$, in both cases
$$x_1^{-\sigma} \pi(h_0) = x_1^{-\rho} \pi(h) = h',$$
where $h'$ is the weak transform of $h$ in the chart. So $h' \in W$. The proof of Theorem 3.2.4 gives $\rho(g'_c) = \operatorname{ord} W - |s| \le \operatorname{ord} W$. With the assumption,
$$\rho = \rho(g'_c) \le \operatorname{ord} W \le \operatorname{ord} h' \le \rho,$$
where the last inequality is Theorem 2.2.2 for $h$. So $\operatorname{ord} h' = \rho = \operatorname{ord} h$, and Theorem 2.2.5 for $h$ gives $v \in T(\operatorname{in}(h))$. $\square$

The proof is the proof of Theorem 3.2.4, read backwards. That proof finds one element of $W$ of order at most $\rho$. If $\rho$ stays, then each element of this kind must have order exactly $\rho$, and Theorem 2.2.5 turns this into a condition on the vector of the chart. The theorem does not use the number of variables, and the point of the chart can be translated.

Let $T_\rho(g)$ be the intersection of the subspaces $T(\operatorname{in}(h))$ for the elements $h$ of Theorem 3.7.1, that is, for $h \in \widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ of order $\rho$ if $\sigma = \rho$, and for $h \in \widetilde\Theta(G)$ of order $\rho$ if $\sigma = \rho + 1$. The theorem says that the points of the exceptional divisor where $\rho$ stays lie in the projective space $\mathbb{P}(T_\rho(g))$. Each vector of a chart has an entry $1$, so it is not $0$. This gives the first corollary.

**Corollary 3.7.2.** If $T_\rho(g) = 0$, then $\rho(g'_c) < \rho$ at each point of the exceptional divisor.

The two cases of the theorem are related to the type $d - \rho$ of Definition 3.1.5. Each generator of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ has order at least $d$, so $\sigma \ge d$. If $\rho = d - 1$, then $\sigma = \rho + 1$, and we are in part 2. If $\rho = d$, then $\sigma = d = \rho$ by Lemma 3.10.1 below, and we are in part 1. So part 1 is the type $0$, and part 2 is the type $1$. The next corollary gives elements of order $\rho$ in terms of $G$.

**Corollary 3.7.3.** In the situation of Theorem 3.7.1:

1. if $\rho = d$ and $p$ does not divide $|r| + d$, then $v \in T(\operatorname{in}(G))$;
2. if $\rho = d - 1$, then $v \in T(\partial_j \operatorname{in}(G))$ for each $j \notin \Delta$ with $\partial_j \operatorname{in}(G) \ne 0$.

*Proof.* Part 1. For $j \notin \Delta$, the element $x_j \partial_j G$ is in $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$, and $r_j = 0$. So $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ contains
$$e = \sum_{i \in \Delta} (r_i G + x_i \partial_i G) + \sum_{j \notin \Delta} x_j \partial_j G = |r| \, G + \sum_{i=1}^n x_i \partial_i G.$$
By the Euler identity for the homogeneous polynomial $\operatorname{in}(G)$ of degree $d$, the homogeneous part of degree $d$ of $e$ is $(|r| + d) \operatorname{in}(G)$, and this is not $0$. So $\operatorname{ord} e = d = \rho$, hence $\sigma = \rho$, and part 1 of the theorem for $h = e$ gives $v \in T((|r| + d) \operatorname{in}(G)) = T(\operatorname{in}(G))$.

Part 2. Here $\sigma = \rho + 1$, as we saw above. For $j \notin \Delta$, the element $\partial_j G$ is in $\widetilde\Theta(G)$. Its homogeneous part of degree $d - 1$ is $\partial_j \operatorname{in}(G)$, so if this is not $0$, then $\operatorname{ord} \partial_j G = d - 1 = \rho$ and $\operatorname{in}(\partial_j G) = \partial_j \operatorname{in}(G)$. Part 2 of the theorem gives the claim. $\square$

Part 1 is the closest analogue of Theorem 2.2.5. If $\rho = d$ and $p \nmid |r| + d$, the points where $\rho$ stays lie in the directrix of the initial form of the residual factor $G$. For $\Delta = \emptyset$ and $p \nmid d$, the conclusion is the conclusion of Theorem 2.2.5 for $G = g$, with the assumption that $\rho$ stays in the place of the assumption that the order stays. In part 2 the subspace is in general larger than $T(\operatorname{in}(G))$. A random test with random clean $g$ and random charts, for $(p, n) = (2, 3), (3, 3), (2, 4), (3, 4)$ and $(5, 3)$, found 1,878 points where $\rho = d - 1$ stays. At 732 of them the vector $v$ is not in $T(\operatorname{in}(G))$. Corollary 3.7.3 held at each point of the test. The same test found no point where $\rho = d$ stays, $p$ divides $|r| + d$ and $v \notin T(\operatorname{in}(G))$, but we have no proof of this.

### The ideal cannot be enlarged in part 1

In part 1, $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ is a proper subideal of $\widetilde\Theta(G)$ in general, and the theorem is false for $\widetilde\Theta(G)$.

**Example 3.7.4.** Let $p = 3$, $n = 2$ with the variables $x, y$, $\Delta = \{1\}$ and $g = x^3 y^2 + x^4 = x^3 (y^2 + x)$. This $g$ is clean, because the exponents $(3, 2)$ and $(4, 0)$ are not both divisible by $3$. Here $r = (3, 0)$, $G = y^2 + x$, $d = 1$ and $\operatorname{in}(G) = x$. Since $3 G = 0$, the generators of $\widetilde\Theta(G)$ are $r_1 G + x \partial_x G = x$ and $\partial_y G = 2 y$. So $\widetilde\Theta(G) = (x, y)$ and $\rho = 1 = d$. The generators of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ are $x$, $2 x y$ and $2 y^2$, so $\sigma = 1 = \rho$, and we are in part 1. Also $|r| + d = 4$ is not divisible by $3$.

Take the $y$-chart $\pi(x) = x y$, $\pi(y) = y$ at its origin, with the vector $v = (0, 1)$. Then $\pi(g) = x^3 y^5 + x^4 y^4 = y^3 (x^3 y^2 + x^4 y)$, so $g' = x^3 y^2 + x^4 y$, which is clean, and $\Delta' = \{1, 2\}$. Here $r' = (3, 1)$ and $G' = x + y$. The generators of $\widetilde\Theta(G')$ are $3 G' + x \partial_x G' = x$ and $G' + y \partial_y G' = x + 2 y$, so $\rho(g'_c) = 1$, and $\rho$ stays. As Corollary 3.7.3 says, $v \in T(x) = T(\operatorname{in}(G))$. But the element $h = 2 y$ of $\widetilde\Theta(G)$ has order $1 = \rho$, and $2 (y + s) \ne 2 y$, so $v \notin T(\operatorname{in}(h))$.

In the $x$-chart, $\pi(g) = x^4 (1 + x (y + t)^2)$ and $g' = x + x^2 (y + t)^2$, with $\Delta' = \{1\}$. Here $r' = (1, 0)$ and $G' = 1 + x (y + t)^2$ has order $0$, so $\rho(g'_c) = 0$ at each point of this chart. So the point of the $y$-chart is the only point where $\rho$ stays. This agrees with Theorem 3.7.1: $T_\rho(g) = T(x)$ is the line of $v = (0, 1)$.

The example shows what the two cases mean. The ideal $\Theta_0(g)$ comes from the logarithmic derivations that map $\mathfrak{m}$ into $\mathfrak{m}$. The derivation $\partial_y$ is not of this kind, so it can give an element $\partial_y G$ of order $\rho$ whose initial form does not control the new points. Only $x \partial_y G$ and $y \partial_y G$ are in $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$, and they have order $2$. In part 2 no element of order $\rho$ comes from $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$, and $x_1 h$ for $h \in \widetilde\Theta(G)$ plays its role in the proof.

### The Lean proof

The Lean library proves Theorem 3.7.1, Corollary 3.7.2 and Example 3.7.4 for polynomials over a field of characteristic $p$, in the file `Singularity/Invariant/LogJacobianNearPoint.lean`. The Lean statements use the element $\psi = x^r h$ of $\Theta_0(g)$ or of $\Theta(g)$ in the place of $h$, because the library does not define the ideal $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$. Part 1 of the theorem is `Singularity.chartVector_mem_directrix_of_logJacobianOrder_eq`. Part 2 is `Singularity.chartVector_mem_directrix_of_logJacobianOrder_eq_of_idealOrd`, with the condition $\sigma = \rho + 1$ in the form $\operatorname{ord} \Theta_0(g) = |r| + \rho + 1$. The Lean proof does not use the ideal $W$. As in the Lean proof of Theorem 3.2.4, it uses the inclusion $\pi(\Theta_0(g)) \subseteq x_1^p \Theta'(g')$ and the monomial $x^b$ that divides each element of $\Theta'(g')$. The element $\pi(x^r h_0)$ is $x_1^p \varphi$ with $\varphi \in \Theta'(g')$ and $\operatorname{ord} \varphi = |b| + \operatorname{ord} h'$, so $\rho(g'_c) \le \operatorname{ord} h'$. Corollary 3.7.2 is `Singularity.logJacobianOrder_cleanPart_lt_of_directrix_eq_bot` for the case $\sigma = \rho$ and `Singularity.logJacobianOrder_cleanPart_lt_of_directrix_eq_bot_of_idealOrd` for the case $\sigma = \rho + 1$. Part 1 of Corollary 3.7.3 is `Singularity.chartVector_mem_directrix_initialForm_of_logJacobianOrder_eq`, and its proof puts the Euler element $e$ in the ideal with the lemma `Singularity.sum_X_mul_pderiv_monomial_mul`. Part 2 is `Singularity.chartVector_mem_directrix_pderiv_initialForm_of_logJacobianOrder_eq`. Its assumption is $\rho(g'_c) = d - 1$ in the place of $\rho = d - 1$. This is sufficient, because $\partial_j \operatorname{in}(G) \ne 0$ gives $\rho \le d - 1$, and Theorem 3.2.4 gives $\rho(g'_c) \le \rho$.

### Notes and references

Theorem 2.2.5 is from [Hir67], and Theorem 3.7.1 is its analogue for the invariant $\rho$. For a base of dimension $3$, the log-Jacobian ideal is in [Cos87]. We do not know a published statement of Theorem 3.7.1 in each dimension. The theorem gives a place for the points where $\rho$ stays, but it does not say that $\rho$ goes down after finitely many blowups. That needs a second invariant at these points, and this is the open step of route R1.
