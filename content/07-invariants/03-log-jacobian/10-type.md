## The case is the type

Sections 3.7 to 3.9 divide the points into two cases. A point is in the first case if $\sigma = \rho$, and in the second case if $\sigma = \rho + 1$, where $\sigma$ is the order of the ideal $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$. The codimension $\tau_\rho$ of Section 3.8 uses a different ideal in each case, and Example 3.8.3 shows that $\tau_\rho$ can go down at a step from the second case to the first case. The full codimension $\widetilde\tau$ of Section 3.8 does not go down at such a step, but it can go down at a step from the first case. So neither number is a secondary invariant alone. In this section we show that the case is not a new datum. It is the type $d - \rho$ of Definition 3.1.5, because $\sigma = d$ for each clean $g$. The proof is the proof of part 2 of Theorem 3.1.4, with the derivation $x_k \partial_k$ in the place of $\partial_k$. So the results of Sections 3.8 and 3.9 are statements about the triple $(\rho, d, \tau_\rho)$, where $d$ is the residual order of Chapter 1. Theorem 3.10.3 says that this triple does not get worse at a step where $\rho$ stays and no component of the divisor leaves. In particular, $d$ goes down by one at each step from the second case to the first case, and so at each drop of $\tau_\rho$ of Example 3.8.3. At the end of the section we describe the steps where the triple can get worse. All of them have a leaving component.

We use the setting and the notation of Sections 3.8 and 3.9. So $g = x^r G$ is clean, $d = \operatorname{ord} G$ is the residual order, $\rho = \rho(g) = \operatorname{ord} \widetilde\Theta(G)$, and
$$\widetilde\Theta_0(G) = \bigl( e_i = r_i G + x_i \partial_i G \ (i \in \Delta) \bigr) + \mathfrak{m} \bigl( \partial_l G \ (l \notin \Delta) \bigr), \qquad \sigma = \operatorname{ord} \widetilde\Theta_0(G).$$
The proof of Theorem 3.2.4 gives $\sigma = \rho$ or $\sigma = \rho + 1$. At the new point we write $g'_c = x^{r'} G'$, $d' = \operatorname{ord} G'$, $\rho' = \rho(g'_c)$ and $\sigma' = \operatorname{ord} \widetilde\Theta_0(G')$. As in Section 3.9, a component $i \in \Delta$ with $i \ne 1$ *leaves* in the chart if $t_i \ne 0$. We write the type as $d - \rho$. We do not use the letter $\tau$ of Definition 3.1.5 for it, because $\tau_\rho$ is the codimension of Section 3.8.

### The order of the tangent ideal

The ideal $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ is generated by the elements $e_i$ for $i \in \Delta$ and the elements $x_k \partial_l G$ for $l \notin \Delta$ and $1 \le k \le n$. Each of these generators has order at least $d$. The operator $x_i \partial_i$ maps a monomial to a multiple of itself, so it does not lower the degree. The operator $\partial_l$ lowers the degree by one, and the factor $x_k$ raises it again. So $\sigma \ge d$. The lemma says that this bound is an equality.

**Lemma 3.10.1.** Let $g \ne 0$ be clean, with $g = x^r G$ and $d = \operatorname{ord} G$. Then $\sigma = d$.

*Proof.* First we show that $\sigma \ge d$. An element of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ is a sum $\sum_j u_j h_j$, where the $h_j$ are generators. Its order is at least the smallest order of the $h_j$. By the remark above, each generator has order at least $d$. So $\sigma \ge d$.

Next we show that $\sigma \le d$. Write $G = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha x^\alpha$, and take $\alpha$ with $c_\alpha \ne 0$ and $|\alpha| = d$. The monomial $x^{r + \alpha}$ of $g$ has the coefficient $c_\alpha$. Since $g$ is clean, not all exponents of this monomial are divisible by $p$. So there is an index $k$ such that $p$ does not divide $r_k + \alpha_k$. We use the identity $x_k \partial_k (x^\beta) = \beta_k x^\beta$.

1. Let $k \in \Delta$. The coefficient of $x^\alpha$ in $e_k = r_k G + x_k \partial_k G$ is $(r_k + \alpha_k) c_\alpha$. This is not $0$ in $K$.
2. Let $k \notin \Delta$. Then $r_k = 0$, so $p$ does not divide $\alpha_k$. The element $x_k \partial_k G$ is a generator of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$, with $l = k$. The coefficient of $x^\alpha$ in it is $\alpha_k c_\alpha$. This is not $0$ in $K$.

In both cases a generator of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ has a term of degree $d$ with a coefficient that is not $0$. This generator has order at least $d$, so its order is $d$. Thus $\sigma \le d$. $\square$

The proof is the proof of part 2 of Theorem 3.1.4, with one change. There, an index $k \notin \Delta$ gives the derivative $\partial_k G$, which lowers the degree by one, and this is the source of the type $1$. Here the same index gives $x_k \partial_k G$, which keeps the degree. The ideal $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ comes from the logarithmic derivations that map $\mathfrak{m}$ into $\mathfrak{m}$ (Section 3.2). On the monomials these derivations do not lower the degree, so $\sigma$ is not smaller than $d$. Cleanness says that they do not kill all the terms of degree $d$, so $\sigma$ is not larger than $d$.

The lemma is stronger than the argument with the Euler element in Lemma 3.9.2. That argument needs $p \nmid |r| + d$. In Example 3.9.5 the number $|r| + d = 6$ is $0$ in $K$, and the Euler element is $0$. The lemma still applies. The term $y u^2$ of $G = (y + z) u^2$ comes from the monomial $x y z w u^2$ of $g$, and its exponent of $y$ is $1$. Since $y \in \Delta$, case 1 of the proof gives the generator $e_y = y u^2$, of order $3 = d$. So $\sigma = 3 = d$, as we found there. In Example 3.1.6 with $\Delta = \emptyset$, the type is $1$. There $\widetilde\Theta_0(G) = \mathfrak{m} (x^2, y^2)$, so $\sigma = 3 = d$ and $\rho = 2$.

**Corollary 3.10.2.** Let $g \ne 0$ be clean, with $g = x^r G$ and $d = \operatorname{ord} G$.

1. The point is in the first case if and only if the type is $0$, that is, $\rho = d$.
2. The point is in the second case if and only if the type is $1$, that is, $\rho = d - 1$.
3. The point is in the second case if and only if $\partial_l \operatorname{in}(G) \ne 0$ for some $l \notin \Delta$.

*Proof.* By Lemma 3.10.1, $\sigma - \rho = d - \rho$. The first case is $\sigma - \rho = 0$, and the second case is $\sigma - \rho = 1$. This gives parts 1 and 2. By part 3 of Theorem 3.1.4, $\rho = d - 1$ if and only if $\partial_l \operatorname{in}(G) \ne 0$ for some $l \notin \Delta$. With part 2 this gives part 3. $\square$

So the case can be read from the initial form of $G$ and the divisor. We do not need to compute the ideal $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$. The second case means that $\operatorname{in}(G)$ has a monomial in which a free variable $x_l$, with $l \notin \Delta$, has an exponent prime to $p$. The first case means that this does not happen. In the first case $H(g)$ is the set of the elements of order $d$ of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$. In the second case $H(g)$ is the set of the elements of order $d - 1$ of $\widetilde\Theta(G)$.

The corollary makes some steps of Section 3.9 shorter. Part 1 of Lemma 3.9.2 gets $\rho = d$ in the first case with the Euler element, and the facts before Theorem 3.9.10 get it with no hypothesis on $p$. The corollary also gives the converse with no hypothesis on $p$: if $\rho = d$, then the point is in the first case. So the hypothesis $\rho = d$ of the Lean statements of Section 3.9 is equivalent to the hypothesis that the old point is in the first case, for each $p$.

The corollary corrects a remark of an earlier edition. Section 3.7 said after Corollary 3.7.2 that both cases can occur if $\rho = d$. By part 1 this is false, and Section 3.7 now refers to Lemma 3.10.1.

In the setting of Proposition 3.6.1, the residual order $d$ is the invariant $\epsilon$ of Cossart and Piltant, by Volume 6, Proposition 3.2.4(4). The order $\rho$ is their invariant $\omega$, by Proposition 3.6.1. So in that setting the type is $\epsilon - \omega$. The first case is the case $\omega = \epsilon$, and the second case is the case $\omega = \epsilon - 1$.

### The triple does not get worse

Now we follow a point blowup. By Corollary 3.10.2, at the old point and at the new point the residual order is $\rho$ in the first case and $\rho + 1$ in the second case. So at a step where $\rho$ stays, the change of $d$ is the change of the case. The results of Sections 3.8 and 3.9 say how the case and $\tau_\rho$ change, and so they say how $d$ and $\tau_\rho$ change together.

**Theorem 3.10.3.** Assume that $\rho' = \rho$ and that no component of $\Delta$ leaves. Then $d' \le d$. If $d' = d$, then $\tau_\rho(g'_c) \ge \tau_\rho(g)$. More precisely:

1. if $g$ is in the first case, then $g'_c$ is in the first case, $d' = d$, and $\tau_\rho(g'_c) \ge \tau_\rho(g)$;
2. if $g$ and $g'_c$ are in the second case, then $d' = d$, and $\tau_\rho(g'_c) \ge \tau_\rho(g)$;
3. if $g$ is in the second case and $g'_c$ is in the first case, then $d' = d - 1$.

*Proof.* By Theorem 3.2.4, $g'_c \ne 0$. So Lemma 3.10.1 and Corollary 3.10.2 apply at the old point and at the new point. The old point is in the first case or in the second case, so the three parts cover all the possibilities.

Part 1. By Corollary 3.10.2, $d = \rho$. Since no component of $\Delta$ leaves, Theorem 3.9.10 says that $g'_c$ is in the first case. By Corollary 3.10.2 at the new point, $d' = \rho' = \rho = d$. Theorem 3.9.11 gives $\tau_\rho(g'_c) \ge \tau_\rho(g)$.

Part 2. By Corollary 3.10.2 at the two points, $d = \rho + 1$ and $d' = \rho' + 1 = \rho + 1 = d$. Since $g'_c$ is in the second case, Proposition 3.8.2 gives $\tau_\rho(g'_c) \ge \tau_\rho(g)$.

Part 3. By Corollary 3.10.2 at the two points, $d = \rho + 1$ and $d' = \rho' = \rho = d - 1$.

In each part $d' \le d$. The equality $d' = d$ holds only in parts 1 and 2, and there $\tau_\rho(g'_c) \ge \tau_\rho(g)$. $\square$

The hypothesis that no component leaves is used only in part 1, through Theorems 3.9.10 and 3.9.11. Parts 2 and 3 use only $\rho' = \rho$. So at a step from the second case where $\rho$ stays, the conclusion holds with no hypothesis on the divisor. In part 1 we can replace the hypothesis by the hypothesis that $p$ does not divide $|r| + d$. Then Proposition 3.9.7 and Corollary 3.9.8 give the same conclusion.

### The order on the triples

For a clean $g \ne 0$ we consider the triple $(\rho, d, \tau_\rho)$. A smaller $\rho$ is better, then a smaller $d$, and then a larger $\tau_\rho$. So we say that $(\rho', d', \tau')$ is *not worse* than $(\rho, d, \tau)$ if $\rho' < \rho$, or $\rho' = \rho$ and $d' < d$, or $\rho' = \rho$, $d' = d$ and $\tau' \ge \tau$. A point blowup does not change the number $n$ of variables. So this is the lexicographic order on the triples
$$(\rho, d, n - \tau_\rho) = (\rho, d, \dim T_\rho(g)) \in \mathbb{N}^3,$$
where the smaller triple is the better one. The lexicographic order on $\mathbb{N}^3$ is a well-order. Since $d = \rho$ or $d = \rho + 1$, the second entry has the same information as the type $d - \rho$, if the first entry is known.

Theorem 3.2.4 and Theorem 3.10.3 together give this statement. At each point of the exceptional divisor where no component of $\Delta$ leaves, the triple of $g'_c$ is not worse than the triple of $g$. Indeed, $\rho' \le \rho$ by Theorem 3.2.4. If $\rho' < \rho$, the new triple is better. If $\rho' = \rho$, Theorem 3.10.3 gives $d' \le d$, and it gives $\tau_\rho(g'_c) \ge \tau_\rho(g)$ when $d' = d$. By the remark after the proof, the same holds at each point of the exceptional divisor if the old point is in the second case.

This has the form of the first steps of the invariant of Hironaka for the order of a hypersurface [Hir67]. The order does not go up, and at a point where the order stays, the codimension of the directrix does not go down. Here the first entry $\rho$ does not go up by Theorem 3.2.4, and Theorem 3.10.3 controls the two other entries at a point where $\rho$ stays. The result means three things for the resolution problem.

1. The drops of $\tau_\rho$ from the second case to the first case are not obstructions for the triple. At each of them $d$ goes down by one, so the triple gets better. This holds for Example 3.8.3 and for the 447 steps of the test of Section 3.8 where $\tau_\rho$ goes down. The full codimension $\widetilde\tau$ is not needed for these steps.
2. The residual order $d$ alone can go up under a point blowup, by Moh's bound (Section 1.4) and Example 3.2.6. In the triple, $d$ comes after $\rho$. At a point where $\rho$ stays, $d$ goes up only at a step with a leaving component, as we show below.
3. The theorem does not say that the triple goes down. In a well-order, a sequence that never goes up can go down only finitely many times. So along a sequence of blowups with no leaving component, the triple goes down only finitely many times. But it can stay the same, and a resolution invariant must also exclude an infinite sequence of steps where it stays. This needs more information at the points where the triple stays, as in the invariant of Hironaka.

### The example of Section 3.8

**Example 3.10.4.** We compute $d$ at the two points of Example 3.8.3. There $p = 2$, $n = 3$ with the variables $x, y, z$, $\Delta = \emptyset$ and
$$g = x y z^2 + y^2 z + x z^2.$$
At the old point $r = 0$ and $G = g$. The terms $y^2 z$ and $x z^2$ have degree $3$, and $x y z^2$ has degree $4$. So $d = 3$ and $\operatorname{in}(G) = y^2 z + x z^2$. Example 3.8.3 found $\rho = 2$ and $\tau_\rho(g) = 2$. So the type is $1$, and $g$ is in the second case, as Corollary 3.10.2 says. Part 3 of the corollary sees this directly: $x \notin \Delta$ and $\partial_x \operatorname{in}(G) = z^2 \ne 0$. Lemma 3.10.1 also holds here. The term $y^2 z$ has the exponent $1$ in $z$, and $z \notin \Delta$, so case 2 of the proof gives the generator $z \partial_z G = y^2 z$ of order $3$. So $\sigma = 3 = d$. The triple is $(\rho, d, \tau_\rho) = (2, 3, 2)$.

At the origin of the $x$-chart, $\Delta' = \{x\}$, $r' = (1, 0, 0)$ and
$$G' = x y z^2 + y^2 z + z^2.$$
The only term of degree $2$ is $z^2$, so $d' = 2$ and $\operatorname{in}(G') = z^2$. Example 3.8.3 found $\rho' = 2$ and $\tau_\rho(g'_c) = 1$. So the type is $0$, and $g'_c$ is in the first case. Again part 3 of Corollary 3.10.2 sees this. The free variables are $y$ and $z$, and $\partial_y (z^2) = 0$ and $\partial_z (z^2) = 2 z = 0$. The triple is $(2, 2, 1)$.

Since $\Delta = \emptyset$, no component leaves, and part 3 of Theorem 3.10.3 applies: $d' = 2 = d - 1$. So $\tau_\rho$ goes down from $2$ to $1$, but $d$ goes down from $3$ to $2$, and the triple gets better. In the form with the dimensions of the directrices, the step is $(2, 3, 1) \to (2, 2, 2)$ in $\mathbb{N}^3$.

The degree that $d$ loses goes into the exceptional divisor. The term $x z^2$ of $g$ becomes $x^3 z^2$ under $\pi$, and $x z^2$ in $g' = x^{-2} \pi(g)$. The new divisor contains the component $x = 0$. So the factor $x$ of this term goes into the monomial $x^{r'}$, and the term gives the term $z^2$ of degree $2$ of $G'$. At the old point the same term had degree $3$ in $G$, because $x = 0$ was not a component of the divisor.

### The kangaroo point

**Remark 3.10.5.** Example 3.2.6 shows that the hypothesis on the divisor in Theorem 3.10.3 cannot be omitted. There $p = 2$, $\Delta = \{x, y\}$, $g = x y (x + y)^2$, $r = (1, 1)$, $G = x^2 + y^2$, $d = 2$ and $\rho = 2$. So the type is $0$, and $g$ is in the first case. The only form of $H(g)$ is $(x + y)^2$, up to a factor. In characteristic $2$ we have $(x + y + s(a_x + a_y))^2 = (x + y)^2 + s^2 (a_x + a_y)^2$, so $T_\rho(g) = \{a_x = a_y\}$ and $\tau_\rho(g) = 1$. The chart $\pi(x) = x$, $\pi(y) = x (y + 1)$ has $t_y = 1$, so the component $y = 0$ leaves, and $\Delta' = \{x\}$. At the new point $G' = y^3$, $d' = 3$ and $\rho' = 2$. So the type is $1$, and $g'_c$ is in the second case. The ideal $\widetilde\Theta(G')$ is generated by $2 G' + x \partial_x G' = 0$ and $\partial_y G' = y^2$, so $T_\rho(g'_c) = \{a_y = 0\}$ and $\tau_\rho(g'_c) = 1$. The triple goes from $(2, 2, 1)$ to $(2, 3, 1)$, and it gets worse.

The reason is Theorem 3.9.10. In the example $R = \{x, y\}$, since $\Phi_x = \Phi_y = x^2 + y^2$. The direction of the chart is $x$, and the only other index $y$ of $R$ leaves. So neither hypothesis of Theorem 3.9.10 holds. The sum $\Phi_x + \Phi_y$ is $(|r| + d) \operatorname{in}(G) = 0$, so the Euler element does not give an element of order $d$, and the proof of Theorem 3.9.10 has no element of order $d$ with an exact transport into $\Theta'_0(g'_c)$. At the new point the element of order $\rho$ is $\partial_y G' = y^2$, the derivative in the direction $y$ that left the divisor. It is in $\widetilde\Theta(G')$ and not in $\widetilde\Theta_0(G')$, so the new point is in the second case.

The same argument describes all the points where $d$ goes up under a point blowup. By Corollary 3.2.5, $d' \le \rho' + 1 \le \rho + 1 \le d + 1$. So $d' = d + 1$ only if $\rho' = \rho$, $d = \rho$ and $d' = \rho' + 1$. By Corollary 3.10.2, the old point is then in the first case and the new point is in the second case. By Proposition 3.9.7, $p$ divides $|r| + d$. By Theorem 3.9.10, $R \ne \{1\}$, and each index of $R \setminus \{1\}$ leaves. In particular a component of $\Delta$ leaves. So each kangaroo point of a point blowup, in the sense of [Hau10], is a point where $\rho$ stays and a component of the divisor leaves. Example 3.9.9 is a second example, for $p = 3$, where $d$ goes from $3$ to $4$.

### What a random test found

Random tests checked the lemma and the theorem. A test computed $\sigma$, $\rho$ and $d$ for 97,989 random clean $g$, with $(p, n) = (2, 3), (2, 4), (3, 3), (3, 4)$ and $(5, 3)$, and it found $\sigma = d$ for each of them, with the type $1$ for 74,523 of them and the type $0$ for 23,466 of them. A second test recorded the type at both points of each step where $\rho$ stays, in random sequences of point blowups. In a first run with $p = 2$, $n = 3$ and 800 sequences, the type was $1$ at each point of the second case and $0$ at each point of the first case, the type went up only at steps from the first case with $p \mid |r| + d$ to the second case with a leaving component, and the 18 drops of $\tau_\rho$ were all from the second case to the first case, with the type going from $1$ to $0$. The test of Section 3.8 had 447 steps from the second case to the first case where $\tau_\rho$ goes down, and by Theorem 3.10.3 the residual order goes down by one at each of them.

### What is left

At the points where $\rho$ stays, the triple can get worse only at a step where the old point is in the first case, $p$ divides $|r| + d$, and a component of $\Delta$ leaves. Indeed, if the old point is in the second case, the remark after Theorem 3.10.3 applies. If the old point is in the first case and $p \nmid |r| + d$, Proposition 3.9.7 and Corollary 3.9.8 apply. If no component leaves, Theorem 3.10.3 applies. There are two kinds of these steps.

1. The type goes up. The new point is in the second case, and $d$ goes from $\rho$ to $\rho + 1$. These are the kangaroo points of the remark above, as in Examples 3.2.6 and 3.9.9. By Theorem 3.9.10, the old point has $R \ne \{1\}$, and each index of $R \setminus \{1\}$ leaves.
2. The codimension goes down in the first case. Both points are in the first case, so $d' = d$, and $\tau_\rho$ goes down. Examples 3.9.5 and 3.9.6 are of this kind.

A leaving component is a component of the old divisor that does not pass through the new point. So at a leaving step the new point lies on fewer strict transforms of the components of $\Delta$ than the old point lies on components of $\Delta$. But the exceptional divisor is a new component at each step, so the number of components through the point is not a monotone invariant. The next step of route R1 is to find an entry, built from the components of the divisor and their history, that goes down at the two kinds of leaving steps and does not go up at the other steps. Section 3.11 studies the steps of the first kind together with the points of the second case after them, up to the next point of the first case.

### The Lean proof

The Lean library proves Lemma 3.10.1 and Theorem 3.10.3 for polynomials over a field of characteristic $p$, with the standard axioms only, in `Singularity/Invariant/LogJacobianType.lean`. As in Sections 3.7 to 3.9, the Lean statements use the ideal $\Theta_0(g)$ and not $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$. Lemma 3.10.1 is `Singularity.idealOrd_logJacobianTangent_eq_ord`, in the form $\operatorname{ord} \Theta_0(g) = \operatorname{ord} g$ for a clean $g \ne 0$. Since $\Theta_0(g) = x^r \widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ and $\operatorname{ord} g = |r| + d$, this is the equation $|r| + \sigma = |r| + d$. Theorem 3.10.3 has two forms. `Singularity.residualOrderAlong_cleanPart_le_of_forall_eq_zero` gives $d' \le d$, and `Singularity.logJacobianDirectrixCodim_le_cleanPart_of_residualOrderAlong_eq` gives $\tau_\rho(g'_c) \ge \tau_\rho(g)$ under the additional hypothesis $d' = d$. In both forms the hypothesis that no component leaves is $t_i = 0$ for each $i \in \Delta$ with $i \ne 1$, as in the Lean form of Theorem 3.9.11. The proofs combine Lemma 3.10.1 with the Lean forms of Theorem 3.9.10 and Theorem 3.9.11 in `Singularity/Invariant/LogJacobianNoLeave.lean` and of Proposition 3.8.2 in `Singularity/Invariant/LogJacobianDirectrix.lean`.
