## The codimension from the first case to the first case

Proposition 3.8.2 says that $\tau_\rho$ does not go down at a point where $\rho$ stays, if the new point is in the second case. The proof sends each element $h$ of $H(g)$ to its weak transform $h'$, and it uses that $h'$ is an element of $H(g'_c)$. If the new point is in the first case, this step can fail, also when the old point is in the first case. Example 3.9.4 below shows this for $p = 2$. In this section we prove that $\tau_\rho$ still does not go down from the first case to the first case, if $p$ does not divide $|r| + d$. The proof does not transport all the elements of $H(g)$. In the first case the initial forms of $H(g)$ come from the elements $r_i G + x_i \partial_i G$, one for each $i \in \Delta$. We transport only those elements whose weak transform is exactly an element of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G')$, and we add one more element, the Euler element of Corollary 3.7.3. A short computation with derivatives shows that these elements are sufficient. Examples 3.9.5 and 3.9.6 show that the theorem is false if $p$ divides $|r| + d$.

We use the setting and the notation of Section 3.8. So $g = x^r G$ with $d = \operatorname{ord} G$, the chart is $\pi = \pi_{1,t}$ with the vector $v = (1, t_2, \dots, t_n)$, and the new divisor is
$$\Delta' = \{1\} \cup \{ i \in \Delta : i \ne 1, \ t_i = 0 \}.$$
So for $i \in \Delta$, we have $v_i = 0$ if and only if $i \ne 1$ and $i \in \Delta'$. Let $F = \operatorname{in}(G)$, the homogeneous part of degree $d$ of $G$. For $i \in \Delta$ we write
$$e_i = r_i G + x_i \partial_i G, \qquad \Phi_i = r_i F + x_i \partial_i F.$$
So $\Phi_i$ is the homogeneous part of degree $d$ of $e_i$. As in Corollary 3.7.3, the *Euler element* is
$$e = |r| \, G + \sum_{k=1}^n x_k \partial_k G = \sum_{i \in \Delta} e_i + \sum_{l \notin \Delta} x_l \partial_l G.$$
It is an element of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$, and by the Euler identity its homogeneous part of degree $d$ is $(|r| + d) F$. For an element $h$ of order $d$ we write $h' = x_1^{-d} \pi(h)$ for its weak transform.

### The theorem

**Theorem 3.9.1.** Assume that $\rho' = \rho$, that $g$ and $g'_c$ are both in the first case, and that $|r| + d$ is not $0$ in $K$. Then $\tau_\rho(g'_c) \ge \tau_\rho(g)$.

The proof uses two lemmas. The first one describes $T_\rho(g)$ in the first case, and the second one gives the elements whose weak transforms are exact.

**Lemma 3.9.2.** Assume that $g$ is in the first case and that $|r| + d$ is not $0$ in $K$.

1. Then $\rho = d$, and $T_\rho(g)$ is the intersection of the subspaces $T(\Phi_i)$ for the $i \in \Delta$ with $\Phi_i \ne 0$. Also $T_\rho(g) \subseteq T(F)$.
2. Assume also that $\rho' = \rho$. If $i \in \Delta$ and $v_i \ne 0$, then $\partial_i F = 0$ and $\Phi_i = r_i F$.

*Proof.* Part 1. Each generator of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ has order at least $d$, so $\sigma \ge d$. In the first case $\sigma = \rho$, so $\rho \ge d$. The Euler element is in $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$, and its homogeneous part of degree $d$ is $(|r| + d) F \ne 0$. So $\sigma \le d$, and $\rho = \sigma = d$.

For $l \notin \Delta$, the element $\partial_l G$ is in $\widetilde\Theta(G)$, so its order is at least $\rho = d$. So each element $x_k \partial_l G$ has order at least $d + 1$, and $\partial_l F = 0$. The ideal $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ is generated by the elements $e_i$ for $i \in \Delta$ and $x_k \partial_l G$ for $l \notin \Delta$. The homogeneous parts of degree $d$ of these generators are the $\Phi_i$ and $0$. By the remark after Definition 3.8.1, $T_\rho(g)$ is the intersection of the $T(\Phi_i)$ with $\Phi_i \ne 0$. Since $\partial_l F = 0$ for $l \notin \Delta$, the homogeneous part of degree $d$ of the Euler element is $\sum_{i \in \Delta} \Phi_i$. So $F$ is a linear combination of the $\Phi_i$, and $T_\rho(g) \subseteq T(F)$.

Part 2. By Theorem 3.7.1, $v \in T_\rho(g)$. By part 1, $v \in T(F)$, and $v \in T(\Phi_i)$, also if $\Phi_i = 0$. For a form $\Phi$ with $v \in T(\Phi)$ we have $\Phi(x + s v) = \Phi(x)$ in $K[x, s]$. The coefficient of $s$ on the left side is $\partial_v \Phi = \sum_k v_k \partial_k \Phi$, so $\partial_v \Phi = 0$. The operator $\partial_v$ commutes with $\partial_i$, and $\partial_v(x_i) = v_i$. So
$$\partial_v \Phi_i = r_i \, \partial_v F + v_i \, \partial_i F + x_i \, \partial_i (\partial_v F) = v_i \, \partial_i F.$$
Both $\partial_v F$ and $\partial_v \Phi_i$ are $0$, so $v_i \partial_i F = 0$. Since $v_i \ne 0$, we get $\partial_i F = 0$, and $\Phi_i = r_i F$. $\square$

**Lemma 3.9.3.** Assume that $\rho' = \rho = d$ and that $g$ and $g'_c$ are both in the first case. Let $h$ be the Euler element $e$, or the element $e_i$ for an $i \in \Delta$ with $v_i = 0$. If $\operatorname{ord} h = d$, then $h' \in H(g'_c)$.

*Proof.* First we compute $\pi(x^r h)$. Let $i \in \Delta$ with $v_i = 0$, so $i \ne 1$ and $t_i = 0$. Then $\pi(x_i) = x_1 x_i$, and Lemma 3.2.2 gives $\pi(x_i \partial_i k) = x_1 x_i \, \pi(\partial_i k) = x_i \partial_i \pi(k)$ for each $k$. With $k = g$ and $\pi(g) = x_1^p g'$, and since $x^r e_i = x_i \partial_i g$,
$$\pi(x^r e_i) = x_i \partial_i (x_1^p g') = x_1^p \, x_i \partial_i g'.$$
For the Euler element, $x^r e = \sum_k x_k \partial_k g$. Lemma 3.2.2 gives, since $p = 0$ in $K$,
$$\pi(x^r e) = x_1 \partial_1 \pi(g) = x_1 \partial_1 (x_1^p g') = x_1^p \, x_1 \partial_1 g'.$$
In both cases $\pi(x^r h) = x_1^p \, D g'$, with $D = x_i \partial_i$ or $D = x_1 \partial_1$. The indices $i$ and $1$ are in $\Delta'$, so $D g'$ is a generator of $\Theta'_0(g')$, the ideal $\Theta_0$ for $\Delta'$.

Next we compute $\pi(x^r h)$ in a second way. As in the proof of Theorem 3.2.4, $\pi(x^r) = u \, x_1^{|r|} x^e$, with the unit $u = \prod (x_i + t_i)^{r_i}$ over the $i \ne 1$ with $t_i \ne 0$, and $x^e = \prod_{i \in \Delta' \setminus \{1\}} x_i^{r_i}$. Since $\operatorname{ord} h = d$, we have $\pi(h) = x_1^d h'$. So
$$u \, x^b h' = D g', \qquad x^b = x_1^{|r| + d - p} x^e.$$
This $x^b$ is the monomial of the proof of Theorem 3.2.4, with $\sigma = d$. In that proof $r' = b + s$, and the proof of Proposition 3.8.2 shows that $s = 0$ when $\rho' = \rho$. So $x^b = x^{r'}$. By Lemma 3.1.2, applied to the derivations of $\Theta'_0$, we have $\Theta'_0(g') = \Theta'_0(g'_c) = x^{r'} \widetilde\Theta_0(G')$. We cancel $x^{r'}$ in $u \, x^{r'} h' = D g'$, and we get $u h' \in \widetilde\Theta_0(G')$. Since $u$ is a unit, $h' \in \widetilde\Theta_0(G')$. The proof of Theorem 3.7.1 gives $\operatorname{ord} h' = \rho = \rho'$. Since $g'_c$ is in the first case, $h' \in H(g'_c)$. $\square$

For the other indices the weak transform is not exact. Let $i \in \Delta$ with $i \ne 1$ and $t_i \ne 0$. Then $\pi(x_i) = x_1 (x_i + t_i)$, and $\pi(x_i \partial_i g) = x_1^p (x_i + t_i) \partial_i g'$. The term $t_i \partial_i g'$ is in $\Theta'(g')$, but in general it is not in $\Theta'_0(g')$, because $i \notin \Delta'$. If $1 \in \Delta$, the element $\pi(x_1 \partial_1 g)$ is the Euler term minus the terms of the other indices, and it has the same problem.

*Proof of Theorem 3.9.1.* By Lemma 3.9.2, $\rho = d$, and $T_\rho(g)$ is the intersection of the $T(\Phi_i)$ with $\Phi_i \ne 0$. By Theorem 3.7.1, $v \in T_\rho(g)$.

First we show a general fact. Let $h \in H(g)$ with $h' \in H(g'_c)$, and let $w \in T_\rho(g'_c)$ with $w_1 = 0$. Then $w \in T(\operatorname{in}(h))$. The proof is the last part of the proof of Proposition 3.8.2. The second and the third fact of that proof use only $v \in T(\operatorname{in}(h))$, and the final step uses $w \in T(\operatorname{in}(h'))$, which holds since $h' \in H(g'_c)$.

Now let $w \in T_\rho(g'_c)$ with $w_1 = 0$, and let $i \in \Delta$ with $\Phi_i \ne 0$. We show that $w \in T(\Phi_i)$. Since $\Phi_i \ne 0$, we have $\operatorname{ord} e_i = d$ and $e_i \in H(g)$.

1. If $v_i = 0$, then $e'_i \in H(g'_c)$ by Lemma 3.9.3. The general fact gives $w \in T(\operatorname{in}(e_i)) = T(\Phi_i)$.
2. If $v_i \ne 0$, then $\Phi_i = r_i F$ by Lemma 3.9.2. The Euler element has order $d$ and the initial form $(|r| + d) F$, so it is in $H(g)$, and $e' \in H(g'_c)$ by Lemma 3.9.3. The general fact gives $w \in T((|r| + d) F) = T(F)$. Since $T(F) \subseteq T(r_i F)$, we get $w \in T(\Phi_i)$.

So $w \in T_\rho(g)$. We showed that $T_\rho(g'_c) \cap L \subseteq T_\rho(g) \cap L$ for the hyperplane $L$ given by $w_1 = 0$. Since $v \in T_\rho(g)$ and $v_1 = 1$, the count of dimensions at the end of the proof of Proposition 3.8.2 gives $\dim T_\rho(g'_c) \le \dim T_\rho(g)$, so $\tau_\rho(g'_c) \ge \tau_\rho(g)$. $\square$

The assumption that $|r| + d$ is not $0$ in $K$ is used twice. It gives $\rho = d$, and it makes the Euler element an element of $H(g)$ with the initial form $(|r| + d) F$. Without this element, the proof has no control of the forms $\Phi_i$ with $v_i \ne 0$, and Example 3.9.5 shows that the theorem then fails.

### An example

**Example 3.9.4.** Let $p = 2$, $n = 3$ with the variables $x, y, z$, $\Delta = \{x\}$ and
$$g = x z^2 + x^2 y^3.$$
The exponents $(1, 0, 2)$ and $(2, 3, 0)$ are not both even, so $g$ is clean, and $\operatorname{ord} g = 3 \ge p$. Here $r = (1, 0, 0)$, $G = z^2 + x y^3$, $d = 2$ and $F = z^2$. In characteristic $2$,
$$e_x = G + x \partial_x G = z^2 + 2 x y^3 = z^2, \qquad \partial_y G = 3 x y^2 = x y^2, \qquad \partial_z G = 2 z = 0.$$
So $\rho = 2 = d$. The ideal $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ is generated by $z^2$ and the elements $x_k \cdot x y^2$, so $\sigma = 2$ and $g$ is in the first case. Here $|r| + d = 3$ is not $0$ in $K$. The only form is $\Phi_x = z^2$, so $T_\rho(g) = T(z^2) = \{w_3 = 0\}$ and $\tau_\rho(g) = 1$.

Take the $y$-chart $\pi(x) = y (x + 1)$, $\pi(y) = y$, $\pi(z) = y z$ at the point with $t_x = 1$, with the vector $v = (1, 1, 0) \in T_\rho(g)$. Then $\pi(g) = y^3 (x + 1) z^2 + y^5 (x + 1)^2$, so
$$g' = y \bigl( (x + 1) z^2 + y^2 (x^2 + 1) \bigr),$$
which is clean. Since $t_x \ne 0$, the component $x = 0$ leaves the divisor, and $\Delta' = \{y\}$. Here $r' = (0, 1, 0)$ and $G' = (x + 1) z^2 + y^2 (x^2 + 1)$. In characteristic $2$,
$$G' + y \partial_y G' = G', \qquad \partial_x G' = z^2, \qquad \partial_z G' = 0.$$
So $\rho' = 2 = \rho$. The element $G'$ is in $\widetilde\Theta_0(G')$ and has order $2$, so $g'_c$ is in the first case. The other generators of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G')$ are the elements $x_k z^2$, of order $3$. So each element of $H(g'_c)$ has an initial form $c (z^2 + y^2) = c (z + y)^2$ with $c \ne 0$, and $T_\rho(g'_c) = \{w_2 = w_3\}$. So $\tau_\rho(g'_c) = 1 = \tau_\rho(g)$, as Theorem 3.9.1 says.

The weak transform of $e_x = z^2$ is $z^2$. It is in $\widetilde\Theta(G')$, because $\partial_x G' = z^2$, but its initial form $z^2$ is not a multiple of $z^2 + y^2$. So $e'_x \notin H(g'_c)$, and the proof of Proposition 3.8.2 does not apply. Here $v_x = 1 \ne 0$, and $\Phi_x = z^2 = r_x F$, as Lemma 3.9.2 says. The Euler element is $e = G + x \partial_x G + y \partial_y G + z \partial_z G = z^2 + 3 x y^3 = G$. Its weak transform is
$$y^{-2} \pi(G) = z^2 + y^2 (x + 1) = (x + 1)^{-1} G',$$
which is in $\widetilde\Theta_0(G')$, as Lemma 3.9.3 says. A vector $w$ with $w_2 = 0$ and $w_2 = w_3$ has $w_3 = 0$, so it is in $T(F)$.

### What a random test found

A random test checked the combination from the first case to the first case. It used random clean $g$, random $\Delta$ and random charts for $(p, n) = (2, 3), (2, 4), (3, 3)$ and $(3, 4)$, and it computed the forms $\Phi_i$ and the directrices by a search over $\mathbb{F}_p^n$. It found 3,776 points where $\rho$ stays and both points are in the first case. At each of them $\rho = d$. At 3,103 of them $|r| + d$ is not $0$ in $K$, and at 1,493 of these some $\Phi_i \ne 0$ has $v_i \ne 0$. At these points the weak transform of $e_i$ is not exact, and the proof needs part 2 of Lemma 3.9.2. At each of the 3,103 points each $\Phi_i$ is a linear combination of $F$ and the forms $\Phi_k$ with $v_k = 0$, as the proof says. A second test with 2,583 points from the first case to the first case found 17 points where the initial form of a weak transform is not in the span of the initial forms of $H(g'_c)$, and no point where $\tau_\rho$ goes down.

### The case where $p$ divides $|r| + d$

Then the Euler element has order larger than $d$, and the proof has no element of $H(g)$ with the initial form $F$. The next example shows that the theorem is then false.

**Example 3.9.5.** Let $p = 2$, $n = 5$ with the variables $x, y, z, w, u$, $\Delta = \{x, y, z, w\}$ and
$$g = x z^2 w u^2 + x y z w u^2.$$
No monomial of $g$ is a square, so $g$ is clean, and $\operatorname{ord} g = 6 \ge p$. Here $r = (1, 0, 1, 1, 0)$, so $x^r = x z w$, and
$$G = z u^2 + y u^2 = (y + z) u^2, \qquad d = 3, \qquad F = G.$$
So $|r| + d = 6$, and this is $0$ in $K$. In characteristic $2$,
$$e_x = G, \qquad e_y = y u^2, \qquad e_z = G + z u^2 = y u^2, \qquad e_w = G, \qquad \partial_u G = 0.$$
So $\widetilde\Theta(G) = \widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ is generated by $G$ and $y u^2$, and $\rho = \sigma = 3$. The point is in the first case, with the forms $(y + z) u^2$ and $y u^2$. We write a vector of $K^5$ as $a = (a_x, a_y, a_z, a_w, a_u)$. The directrix of $y u^2$ is $\{a_y = a_u = 0\}$, since $(y + s a_y)(u + s a_u)^2 = y u^2$ in $K[x, s]$ only for $a_y = a_u = 0$. In the same way the directrix of $(y + z) u^2$ is $\{a_y + a_z = 0, \ a_u = 0\}$. So
$$T_\rho(g) = \{ a_y = a_z = a_u = 0 \}, \qquad \tau_\rho(g) = 3.$$
Since $G$ is homogeneous of degree $d$, the Euler element is $e = |r| G + \sum_k x_k \partial_k G = (|r| + d) G = 6 G = 0$.

Take the $w$-chart $\pi(x) = w (x + 1)$, $\pi(y) = w y$, $\pi(z) = w z$, $\pi(w) = w$, $\pi(u) = w u$ at the point with $t_x = 1$ and $t_y = t_z = t_u = 0$, with the vector $v = (1, 0, 0, 1, 0)$. This vector is in $T_\rho(g)$, as Theorem 3.7.1 says. Then $\pi(g) = w^6 (x + 1) (z^2 + y z) u^2$, so
$$g' = w^4 (x + 1) (z^2 + y z) u^2 = w^4 \bigl( x z^2 u^2 + z^2 u^2 + (x + 1) y z u^2 \bigr).$$
The monomial $w^4 z^2 u^2$ is a square, so the clean part is
$$g'_c = w^4 \bigl( x z^2 u^2 + (x + 1) y z u^2 \bigr).$$
Since $t_x \ne 0$, the component $x = 0$ leaves the divisor, and $\Delta' = \{y, z, w\}$. Here $r' = (0, 0, 1, 4, 0)$ and
$$G' = x z u^2 + (x + 1) y u^2.$$
In characteristic $2$,
$$e'_y = (x + 1) y u^2, \qquad e'_z = G' + x z u^2 = (x + 1) y u^2, \qquad e'_w = 4 G' = 0, \qquad \partial_u G' = 0,$$
and the derivative in the direction that left the divisor is
$$\partial_x G' = z u^2 + y u^2 = (y + z) u^2.$$
The ideal $\widetilde\Theta(G')$ contains $\partial_x G'$, so $\rho' = 3 = \rho$. The ideal $\widetilde\Theta_0(G')$ is generated by $(x + 1) y u^2$ and the elements $x_k (y + z) u^2$, of order $4$. So $\sigma' = 3$, the new point is in the first case, and the only form of order $3$ of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G')$ is $y u^2$, up to a factor. So
$$T_\rho(g'_c) = \{ a_y = a_u = 0 \}, \qquad \tau_\rho(g'_c) = 2 < \tau_\rho(g).$$

The example shows where the proof of Theorem 3.9.1 fails. The old forms are $F$ and $y u^2$. The form $y u^2$ comes from $e_y$, with $v_y = 0$, and its weak transform is exact. The form $F$ comes from $e_x$ and $e_w$, with $v_x = v_w = 1$, and the Euler element is $0$. So no element of $H(g)$ with an exact weak transform has the initial form $F$. At the new point the form $(y + z) u^2$ is still there, as the initial form of $\partial_x G'$, but this element is in $\widetilde\Theta(G')$ and not in $\widetilde\Theta_0(G')$. In the first case the definition of $T_\rho(g'_c)$ uses only $\widetilde\Theta_0(G')$, so it does not see this form.

This suggests to define $T_\rho$ with $\widetilde\Theta(G)$ also in the first case. Then in Example 3.9.5 both values are $3$. But this form of $\tau$ is worse. A random test for $(p, n) = (2, 3), (2, 4), (2, 5), (3, 3)$ and $(3, 4)$ found many points where it goes down from the first case to the first case, also when $p$ does not divide $|r| + d$. The reason is the same. An element $\partial_l G$ with $l \notin \Delta$ has no exact transport, since $\partial_l$ is not a logarithmic derivation. So Theorem 3.9.1 needs the ideal $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ in the first case, and the hypothesis on $|r| + d$ is necessary.

The drop is rare. For $p = 2$ and $n = 3$, a random test found 6,290 points where $\rho$ stays, $p$ divides $|r| + d$ and both points are in the first case, and $\tau_\rho$ does not go down at any of them. For $n = 4$ the test found the next example. It has three monomials, and the mechanism is the same as in Example 3.9.5.

**Example 3.9.6.** Let $p = 2$, $n = 4$ with the variables $x, y, z, w$, $\Delta = \{x, y, w\}$ and
$$g = x y z^2 w^2 + x^3 y w^2 + x^3 y^2 z^3 w.$$
Here $r = (1, 1, 0, 1)$, and
$$G = (x + z)^2 w + x^2 y z^3, \qquad d = 3, \qquad F = (x + z)^2 w.$$
So $|r| + d = 6$, and this is $0$ in $K$. In characteristic $2$,
$$e_x = G, \qquad e_y = (x + z)^2 w, \qquad e_w = x^2 y z^3, \qquad \partial_z G = x^2 y z^2.$$
So $\rho = \sigma = 3$, the point is in the first case, and the only form of order $3$ of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ is $F$, up to a factor. With $a = (a_x, a_y, a_z, a_w)$,
$$T_\rho(g) = \{ a_x + a_z = 0, \ a_w = 0 \}, \qquad \tau_\rho(g) = 2.$$
Take the $y$-chart $\pi(x) = y (x + 1)$, $\pi(y) = y$, $\pi(z) = y (z + 1)$, $\pi(w) = y w$, with the vector $v = (1, 1, 1, 0)$ in $T_\rho(g)$. Since $(z + 1)^2 + (x + 1)^2 = (x + z)^2$ in characteristic $2$,
$$g' = y^4 (x + 1) (x + z)^2 w^2 + y^7 (x + 1)^3 (z + 1)^3 w.$$
The square monomials of $g'$ are $y^4 x^2 w^2$ and $y^4 z^2 w^2$, so
$$g'_c = y^4 x (x + z)^2 w^2 + y^7 (x + 1)^3 (z + 1)^3 w.$$
Since $t_x \ne 0$, the component $x = 0$ leaves the divisor, and $\Delta' = \{y, w\}$. Here $r' = (0, 4, 0, 1)$ and
$$G' = x (x + z)^2 w + y^3 (x + 1)^3 (z + 1)^3.$$
In characteristic $2$,
$$e'_y = e'_w = y^3 (x + 1)^3 (z + 1)^3, \qquad \partial_x G' = (x + z)^2 w + y^3 (x + 1)^2 (z + 1)^3, \qquad \partial_z G' = y^3 (x + 1)^3 (z + 1)^2.$$
So $\rho' = 3 = \rho$. The ideal $\widetilde\Theta_0(G')$ is generated by $e'_y$ and the elements $x_k \partial_x G'$ and $x_k \partial_z G'$, of order $4$. So $\sigma' = 3$, the new point is in the first case, and the only form of order $3$ of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G')$ is $y^3$. Its directrix is $\{a_y = 0\}$, so
$$\tau_\rho(g'_c) = 1 < \tau_\rho(g).$$

As in Example 3.9.5, the form $F$ comes only from $e_x$ and $e_y$, with $v_x = v_y = 1$, and the Euler element is $0$. At the new point the form $(x + z)^2 w + y^3$ is the initial form of $\partial_x G'$, which is in $\widetilde\Theta(G')$ and not in $\widetilde\Theta_0(G')$. We do not know if there is an example with $n = 3$.

### The Lean proof

The Lean library proves Theorem 3.9.1, Lemma 3.9.2, Lemma 3.9.3 and Example 3.9.4 for polynomials over a field of characteristic $p$, with the standard axioms only, in the file `Singularity/Invariant/LogJacobianDirectrixCaseA.lean`. Theorem 3.9.1 is `Singularity.finrank_logJacobianDirectrix_cleanPart_le_of_caseA` in the form $\dim T_\rho(g'_c) \le \dim T_\rho(g)$, and `Singularity.logJacobianDirectrixCodim_le_cleanPart_of_caseA` in the form with $\tau_\rho$.

The Lean theorem has the hypothesis $\rho = d$ in the place of the hypothesis that $g$ is in the first case. If $|r| + d$ is not $0$ in $K$, the two hypotheses are equivalent. Part 1 of Lemma 3.9.2 gives $\rho = d$ in the first case. Conversely, if $\rho = d$, then the Euler element gives $\sigma \le d = \rho$, and $\sigma \ge \rho$ always, so $g$ is in the first case. The hypothesis that $g'_c$ is in the first case is the equation $\operatorname{ord} \Theta'_0(g'_c) = |r'| + d$.

The ideal $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ is `Singularity.residualLogJacobianTangent`, and the equation $\Theta_0(x^r G) = x^r \widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ of Lemma 3.1.2 is `Singularity.logJacobianTangent_monomial_mul`. The proof of part 2 of Lemma 3.9.2 uses the identity $\Phi(x + s v) = \Phi(x)$ through the chain rule for the translation by $v$, `Singularity.pderiv_some_translate`. In Lemma 3.9.3, the Lean proof gets $x^b = x^{r'}$ in a different way. It shows that $b \le r'$, and the equation $\operatorname{ord} \Theta'_0(g'_c) = |r'| + d$ gives $|r'| \le |b|$, because $D g' = u \, x^b h'$ is an element of $\Theta'_0(g'_c)$ of order $|b| + d$. The proof then does not cancel the unit $u$. It shows that $u h'$ is in the definition of $T_\rho(g'_c)$, and it uses that the constant term of $u$ is not $0$, so $u h'$ and $h'$ have the same initial form up to a nonzero factor. In Example 3.9.4, `Singularity.tauRhoCaseAExample_not_mem` shows that $g'$ is clean and that the transforms $y z^2$ and $y (x + 1) z^2$ of $x e_x$ are not in $\Theta'_0(g'_c)$.

In the file `Singularity/Invariant/LogJacobianDirectrixDrop.lean`, the Lean library proves Example 3.9.5 over $\mathbb{F}_2$, with the polynomials written out. `Singularity.tauRhoDropExample_hyp` gives the data: $g$ is clean of order at least $2$, the chart gives $\pi(g) = w^2 g'$, the new divisor is $\{y, z, w\}$, the clean part is $g'_c$, and $|r| + d$ is $0$ in $\mathbb{F}_2$. `Singularity.tauRhoDropExample_cases` shows that both points are in the first case, in the form $\operatorname{ord} \Theta_0 = |r| + \rho$. `Singularity.tauRhoDropExample_finrank` gives $\rho = \rho' = 3$ and the dimensions $2$ and $3$ of the two directrices, and `Singularity.tauRhoDropExample_codim` gives $\tau_\rho(g) = 3$ and $\tau_\rho(g'_c) = 2$. The library does not prove Example 3.9.6.
