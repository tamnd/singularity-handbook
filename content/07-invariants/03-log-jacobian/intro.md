# The log-Jacobian ideal

The residual order of Chapter 1 is a number computed from the coefficients of $F$ in the equation $z^p + F$. It depends on the clean representative of $F$, and Moh's bound shows that it can go up under a blowup. This chapter studies a second object, an ideal built from the derivatives of $F$. It does not see the choice of $z$, and its residual order differs from the residual order of $F$ by at most one.

Section 3.1 defines the log-Jacobian ideal, proves that it does not change when a $p$-th power is added to $F$, and proves the dichotomy: for a clean $F$, the residual order $\rho$ of the ideal is $d - 1$ or $d$, where $d$ is the residual order of $F$. Section 3.2 gives the transformation law of the ideal under a point blowup, and it proves that $\rho$ does not go up. The residual order can go up, but by at most one, and the kangaroo points of Hauser are the points where it does. Section 3.3 uses the derivatives of $F$ in a different way. It gives the Newton criterion for the equation $z^p + F$: the equation is Newton nondegenerate if and only if no initial form of $F$ has a critical point in the torus. In characteristic $p$ a critical point need not lie on the zero set, and a mod $p$ Euler identity tells where this can happen. Section 3.4 returns to the blowups. For a regular center of positive dimension, the transformation law has the same form, and $\rho$ does not go up when the center satisfies two conditions of equimultiplicity. An example shows that the second condition cannot be omitted. Section 3.5 finds the singularities at a point with $\rho = 0$. After a change of the coordinates the equation is $z^p + x^r$ or $z^p + x^r x_j$, a binomial equation, so the end of the route is not the difficult part. Section 3.6 compares $\rho$ with the invariant $\omega$ of Cossart and Piltant. For the equations $z^p + F$ over a perfect field the two invariants are equal, so $\omega$ does not depend on the coordinates and does not go up under the blowups of Sections 3.2 and 3.4, in each dimension. Section 3.7 finds the points where $\rho$ stays under a point blowup. They lie in the projective space of a directrix, as for the order in Section 2.2, but the directrix comes from the ideal $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$ or from $\widetilde\Theta(G)$, and the choice depends on the order of $\widetilde\Theta_0(G)$. Section 3.8 proves that the codimension of this directrix does not go down at a point where $\rho$ stays if the new point is in the second case, and an example for $p = 2$ shows that it can go down when the old point is in the second case and the new point is in the first case. The section also defines a full form of the codimension with all of $\widetilde\Theta(G)$, and proves that it does not go down at a step from the second case. Section 3.9 proves that it does not go down when both points are in the first case, if $p$ does not divide $|r| + d$. The proof transports only some of the elements of the ideal, and it uses the Euler element of Section 3.7. Two examples for $p = 2$ show that the hypothesis on $|r| + d$ is necessary. Section 3.10 shows that the case of a point is the type of Section 3.1: the first case is the type $0$, and the second case is the type $1$. So the triple of $\rho$, the residual order $d$ and the codimension does not get worse at a point where $\rho$ stays and no component of the divisor leaves. In particular, $d$ goes down by one at each step from the second case to the first case, and the remaining steps where the triple can get worse all have a leaving component. Section 3.11 follows a kangaroo path, a sequence of steps that leaves the first case at a kangaroo point and comes back to it. It proves that $\tau_\rho$ does not go down along the points of the second case, it gives an example where $\tau_\rho$ goes down at the step back to the first case, and it states the conjecture that $\tau_\rho$ at the end is at least $\tau_\rho$ at the start. The conjecture is true if $\tau_\rho$ at the start is at most $1$, but it is false in general: Example 3.11.6 is a kangaroo path for $p = 2$ in three variables where $\tau_\rho$ goes down from $2$ to $1$. Section 3.12 asks a weaker question: is each run of point blowups where $\rho$ stays finite? Example 3.12.2 is a run for $p = 2$ in three variables that does not end. It follows a curve along which the order is $\rho$, so a point blowup does not separate the curve from the locus of this order, and a resolution must blow up the curve. Example 3.12.4 is an infinite run that starts at an isolated point of the locus where $\rho$ is at least its value, and along its curve the order of $G$ is $\rho$, but the order of $\widetilde\Theta(G)$ is smaller. Hypothesis 3.12.5 says that each infinite run follows a formal curve along which the order of $G$ is at least $\rho$. It is an open question, and the section reports the computer tests. So the centers of a resolution with this invariant must be in the locus of the order of $G$, with $\rho$ as a second entry. Section 3.13 proves the second half of the hypothesis in each dimension: along an infinite run that follows a regular formal curve, the order of $G$ along the curve is $\rho$ or $\rho + 1$, with an order that does not depend on the coordinates. It proves the whole hypothesis for $n = 2$. In three variables the first half is false: a run that takes the corners of the divisor for ever follows no curve, and it lies on a surface. In the example of Cossart and Piltant in four variables the run follows a transcendental curve, and its Zariski closure is a plane. So Hypothesis 3.13.12 asks for a subvariety of positive dimension, and the centers of a resolution can be surfaces.

Throughout the chapter, $K$ is a field of characteristic $p > 0$, $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$, and $\partial_i$ is the partial derivative with respect to $x_i$. The divisor is $E = V(\prod_{i \in \Delta} x_i)$ for a subset $\Delta \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$, as in Section 1.3. The results hold for $K[x]$ and for $K[[x]]$. The Lean library proves them for polynomials.
