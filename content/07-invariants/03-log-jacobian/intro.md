# The log-Jacobian ideal

The residual order of Chapter 1 is a number computed from the coefficients of $F$ in the equation $z^p + F$. It depends on the clean representative of $F$, and Moh's bound shows that it can go up under a blowup. This chapter studies a second object, an ideal built from the derivatives of $F$. It does not see the choice of $z$, and its residual order differs from the residual order of $F$ by at most one.

Section 3.1 defines the log-Jacobian ideal, proves that it does not change when a $p$-th power is added to $F$, and proves the dichotomy: for a clean $F$, the residual order $\rho$ of the ideal is $d - 1$ or $d$, where $d$ is the residual order of $F$. Section 3.2 gives the transformation law of the ideal under a point blowup, and it proves that $\rho$ does not go up. The residual order can go up, but by at most one, and the kangaroo points of Hauser are the points where it does. Section 3.3 uses the derivatives of $F$ in a different way. It gives the Newton criterion for the equation $z^p + F$: the equation is Newton nondegenerate if and only if no initial form of $F$ has a critical point in the torus. In characteristic $p$ a critical point need not lie on the zero set, and a mod $p$ Euler identity tells where this can happen. Section 3.4 returns to the blowups. For a regular center of positive dimension, the transformation law has the same form, and $\rho$ does not go up when the center satisfies two conditions of equimultiplicity. An example shows that the second condition cannot be omitted. Section 3.5 finds the singularities at a point with $\rho = 0$. After a change of the coordinates the equation is $z^p + x^r$ or $z^p + x^r x_j$, a binomial equation, so the end of the route is not the difficult part. Section 3.6 compares $\rho$ with the invariant $\omega$ of Cossart and Piltant. For the equations $z^p + F$ over a perfect field the two invariants are equal, so $\omega$ does not depend on the coordinates and does not go up under the blowups of Sections 3.2 and 3.4, in each dimension.

Throughout the chapter, $K$ is a field of characteristic $p > 0$, $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$, and $\partial_i$ is the partial derivative with respect to $x_i$. The divisor is $E = V(\prod_{i \in \Delta} x_i)$ for a subset $\Delta \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$, as in Section 1.3. The results hold for $K[x]$ and for $K[[x]]$. The Lean library proves them for polynomials.
