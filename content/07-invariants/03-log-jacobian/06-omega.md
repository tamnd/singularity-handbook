## The invariant omega of Cossart and Piltant

Volume 6 follows the proof of Cossart and Piltant for threefolds. Their main invariant at a point of multiplicity $p$ is $\omega$ (Volume 6, Definition 3.2.5). It is defined from the initial form of the equation in well adapted coordinates, and its independence of these coordinates needs a proof [CP19, Remark 2.69]. In this section we show that, for the purely inseparable equations $Z^p + f$ over a perfect field, $\omega$ is the residual order $\rho$ of the log-Jacobian ideal. This gives a definition of $\omega$ without coordinates, and the theorems of Sections 3.2 and 3.4 then say that $\omega$ does not go up, in each dimension.

We use the notation of Volume 6, Section 3.2. Let $k$ be a perfect field of characteristic $p$, let $S = k[[u_1, \dots, u_n]]$ and $E = \operatorname{div}(u_1 \cdots u_e)$. We apply the results of Section 3.1 with $K = k$, $x = u$ and $\Delta = \{1, \dots, e\}$. Let $h = Z^p + f$ with $f \in S$ clean and $f \ne 0$. Then $f$ is its own clean part, so $(u; Z)$ is well adapted by Volume 6, Proposition 3.2.4(1). Let $x$ be the closed point, and assume that $m(x) = p$, that is, $\operatorname{ord} f \ge p$. Write $f = u^r G$ as in Section 3.1, with $d = \operatorname{ord} G$, and let $F$ be the initial form of $f$.

### The two invariants agree

**Proposition 3.6.1.** In this setting, $\omega(x) = \rho(f)$.

*Proof.* By Volume 6, Proposition 3.2.4(4), $\epsilon(x) = \operatorname{ord} G = d$. The initial form of $f = u^r G$ is $F = U^r \operatorname{in}(G)$, because the graded ring $k[U_1, \dots, U_n]$ is a domain. For $j > e$ we have $r_j = 0$, so $U^r$ does not contain $U_j$, and
$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial U_j} = U^r \, \frac{\partial \operatorname{in}(G)}{\partial U_j}.$$
So $\partial F / \partial U_j \ne 0$ for some $j > e$ if and only if $\partial_j \operatorname{in}(G) \ne 0$ for some $j \notin \Delta$. If this holds, then $\omega(x) = d - 1$ by Definition 3.2.5 of Volume 6, and $\rho(f) = d - 1$ by Theorem 3.1.4(3). If it does not hold, then $\omega(x) = d$, and $\rho(f) = d$ by Theorem 3.1.4(2) and (3). $\square$

The proof is short, but the two definitions look different. Cossart and Piltant use the initial form of $h$ and its derivatives in the directions outside $E$. The ideal $\Theta(f)$ uses all the logarithmic derivations, and its order is the smallest order of an element. Theorem 3.1.4 shows that only the free directions can lower the order, and only by one.

### Independence of the coordinates

**Corollary 3.6.2.** In the setting of Proposition 3.6.1, let $u'$ be a regular system of parameters of $S$ with $\operatorname{div}(u'_i) = \operatorname{div}(u_i)$ for $i \le e$, and let $Z'$ be such that $h = Z'^p + f'$ with $f'$ clean in the expansion in $u'$. Then $\omega(x)$ computed in $(u'; Z')$ is equal to $\omega(x)$ computed in $(u; Z)$.

Node: SG0000004T

*Proof.* First we describe $\Theta(f)$ without coordinates. Each derivation $D$ of $S$ kills each perfect subfield of $S$, because $D(a^p) = p a^{p-1} D(a) = 0$. By the Leibniz rule, $D(\mathfrak{m}^s) \subseteq \mathfrak{m}^{s-1}$, so $D$ is continuous. So $D = \sum_i D(u_i) \, \partial / \partial u_i$, and in the same way $D = \sum_i D(u'_i) \, \partial / \partial u'_i$ in an expansion $S = k'[[u']]$ with a coefficient field $k'$, as in Volume 6, Proposition 3.2.4(5). A derivation $D$ is logarithmic for $E$ if $D(u_i) \in (u_i)$ for each $i \le e$. This condition depends only on the ideals $(u_i)$, so the module of the logarithmic derivations depends only on $S$ and $E$. It has the basis of Section 3.1 in the coordinates $u$, and also in the coordinates $u'$. So the ideal $\Theta(f)$, generated by $D(f)$ for all logarithmic $D$, is the same in the two systems of coordinates.

Both $Z$ and $Z'$ are translations of the variable of the monic polynomial $h$, so $Z = Z' + \varphi$ for some $\varphi \in S$. Then $h = Z'^p + f + \varphi^p$, so $f' = f + \varphi^p$, and $\Theta(f') = \Theta(f)$ by Lemma 3.1.2. Now we read the exponents from this ideal. For $i \le e$ let $\operatorname{ord}_{u_i}$ be the valuation of the prime ideal $(u_i)$. We have $\Theta(f) = u^r \widetilde\Theta(G)$, and by Theorem 3.1.4(1) the ideal $\widetilde\Theta(G)$ is not contained in $(u_i)$. So $r_i$ is the smallest value of $\operatorname{ord}_{u_i}$ on $\Theta(f)$. The same argument in the coordinates $u'$ gives the same number, because $(u'_i) = (u_i)$. So $u'^{r'} = c \, u^r$ for a unit $c$ of $S$, and the ideal $u^{-r} \Theta(f)$ is the same in the two systems of coordinates. Its order at $x$ is $\rho(f)$ in the coordinates $u$ and $\rho(f')$ in the coordinates $u'$, so $\rho(f) = \rho(f')$. By Proposition 3.6.1 in the two systems of coordinates, the two values of $\omega(x)$ are equal. $\square$

Cossart and Piltant prove the independence for each equation of degree $p$, also in the Artin-Schreier case and over a residue field that is not perfect [CP19, Remark 2.69]. Corollary 3.6.2 is the purely inseparable case over a perfect field, with a proof that uses only the ideal $\Theta(f)$. Volume 6, Proposition 3.2.4(5), gives the same result for $\epsilon(x)$.

### Omega does not go up

**Corollary 3.6.3.** In the setting of Proposition 3.6.1, let $k$ be algebraically closed and let $f \in k[u_1, \dots, u_n]$ be a polynomial. Let $x'$ be a closed point over $x$ of one of these blowups:

1. the blowup of the point $x$;
2. the blowup along $Y = V(Z, u_1, \dots, u_c)$, where $C = V(u_1, \dots, u_c)$ with $2 \le c \le n$, $f \in I_C^p$, and $f$ satisfies the conditions (H1) and (H2) of Section 3.4 for $C$.

Let $\omega(x')$ be computed for the strict transform of $h$ and the new divisor $E'$ in the completion of the local ring at $x'$. Then $\omega(x') \le \omega(x)$.

Node: SG0000004T

*Proof.* If $m(x') < p$, then $\omega(x') = 0$ by Volume 6, Definition 3.2.5. So assume $m(x') = p$. Since $k$ is algebraically closed, after a change of the numbering the point $x'$ is the origin of a chart $\pi$ of Section 3.2 in case 1, or of Section 3.4 in case 2. With $Z = u_1 Z'$, the strict transform is $h' = Z'^p + f'$ with $f' = u_1^{-p} \pi(f)$. Let $f'_c$ be the clean part of $f'$ for the new divisor, and let $\psi$ be such that $f'_c = f' + \psi^p$. Then $h' = (Z' - \psi)^p + f'_c$, and the coordinates $(u; Z' - \psi)$ of the chart are well adapted at $x'$, by Volume 6, Proposition 3.2.4(1). Proposition 3.6.1 at $x$ and at $x'$ gives $\omega(x) = \rho(f)$ and $\omega(x') = \rho(f'_c)$. By Theorem 3.2.4 in case 1, and by Theorem 3.4.5 in case 2, $\rho(f'_c) \le \rho(f)$. $\square$

Compare this with Theorem 3.2.6 of Volume 6, the theorem of Cossart and Piltant [CP19, Theorem 3.13]. That theorem holds for each equation of degree $p$ and for each center of the first or the second kind, and it gives more: the invariant $\iota = (m, \omega, \kappa)$ does not go up. But it assumes condition (E). Corollary 3.6.3 holds only for the purely inseparable equations, and only for point blowups and for the centers of Section 3.4. It does not assume condition (E), and its proof holds in each dimension. We do not know a published statement of it without condition (E). The experiment E4 of Volume 6, Section 4.3, finds that $\omega$ never goes up on random walks of point blowups for $n = 3$ and $n = 4$, also at the steps that Theorem 3.2.6 does not cover. Case 1 of the corollary proves this observation. The walks use points with coordinates in $\mathbb{F}_p$, and the value of $\omega$ does not change when we extend $\mathbb{F}_p$ to its algebraic closure, because Definition 3.2.5 of Volume 6 uses only the coefficients of $F$.

### An increase of omega

**Example 3.6.4.** Take the data of Example 3.4.6: $p = 2$, $S = k[[x, y, w]]$, $E = \operatorname{div}(w)$, $h = Z^2 + y^2 w + y^3$ and the center $Y = V(Z, x, y)$. The center is Hironaka-permissible, because it is regular, it has normal crossings with $E$, and $h \in (Z, x, y)^2$. We compute $\omega$ with Definition 3.2.5 of Volume 6, and the free variables are $x$ and $y$. At the origin, $F = Y^2 W + Y^3$ and $\epsilon = 3$. Since $\partial F / \partial Y = Y^2 \ne 0$, we get $\omega = 2$. At the origin of the $x$-chart, $h' = Z'^2 + y^2 w + x y^3$ and $E' = \operatorname{div}(x w)$, so the only free variable is $y$. Here $F' = Y^2 W$ and $\epsilon' = 3$. Since $\partial F' / \partial Y = 2 Y W = 0$, we get $\omega' = 3$. So $\omega$ goes up from $2$ to $3$. These are the values $\rho = 2$ and $\rho(g'_c) = 3$ of Example 3.4.6, as Proposition 3.6.1 says.

This does not contradict Theorem 3.2.6 of Volume 6, because condition (E) fails here. Since $h = Z^2 + y^2 (w + y) \in (Z, y)^2$, each point of the surface $V(Z, y)$ has multiplicity $2$. So the locus $\operatorname{Sing}_p X$ contains this surface, and its image $V(y)$ in $\operatorname{Spec} S$ is not contained in $E = V(w)$. The center is also not of the first kind. Let $\xi$ be the generic point of $Y$. The residue field of $S$ at the image of $\xi$ is $k(w)$, and $w$ is not a square in it. So the term $y^2 w$ of $f$ cannot be removed by a translation of $Z$, and $\delta(\xi) = 1$ and $\epsilon(\xi) = 2$, which is one less than the value $\epsilon = 3$ at the origin. We have not checked the two other conditions of a center of the second kind. The example shows that a condition of this kind is necessary for the non-increase of $\omega$ under the blowup of a curve.

### The Lean proof

The Lean library does not yet prove the results of this section. For polynomials, Proposition 3.6.1 follows from part 3 of Theorem 3.1.4, which is `Singularity.logJacobianOrder_add_one_eq_iff`, and Corollary 3.6.3 follows from `Singularity.logJacobianOrder_cleanPart_le` and `Singularity.logJacobianOrder_cleanPart_le_center`. Corollary 3.6.2 needs the module of the logarithmic derivations of $K[[x]]$ and the changes of the coordinates, which are not in the library.

### Notes and references

The invariant $\omega$ is [CP19, Definition 2.68], and Cossart's thesis uses the same kind of invariant for a base of dimension $3$ [Cos87]. The identification with the log-Jacobian ideal uses only Theorem 3.1.4. The ideal $\Theta(f)$ is also the ideal that Cossart uses to state the transformation law [Cos87].
