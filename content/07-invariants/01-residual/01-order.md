## The order of a power series

The order of a hypersurface at a point is the first and simplest measure of its singularity. A point of order $1$ is a regular point, and the points of maximal order are the worst points. In this section we define the order of a power series, relate it to the order of Section 3.2 of Volume 1, and prove its laws.

### The order and the initial form

**Definition 1.1.1.** Let $f = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha x^\alpha$ be a nonzero element of $R = K[[x]]$. The *order* of $f$ is
$$\operatorname{ord} f = \min \{ |\alpha| : c_\alpha \ne 0 \},$$
and $\operatorname{ord} 0 = \infty$. The *initial form* of $f \ne 0$ is the homogeneous polynomial
$$\operatorname{in}(f) = \sum_{|\alpha| = \operatorname{ord} f} c_\alpha x^\alpha.$$

Lean: Singularity.ord (for polynomials, through the power series ring)

For example, $\operatorname{ord}(x^2 - y^3) = 2$ and $\operatorname{in}(x^2 - y^3) = x^2$. A power series has order $0$ if and only if it is a unit of $R$, because a power series with a nonzero constant term is invertible.

**Proposition 1.1.2.** Let $f \in R$ and $d \in \mathbb{N}$. Then $f \in \mathfrak{m}^d$ if and only if $\operatorname{ord} f \ge d$. Thus $\operatorname{ord} f = \operatorname{ord}_\mathfrak{m}(f)$, the order of Definition 3.2.1 of Volume 1.

Lean: Singularity.le_ord_iff_mem_pow, Singularity.ord_eq_order_idealOfVars (for polynomials)

*Proof.* The ideal $\mathfrak{m}^d$ is generated by the monomials $x^\beta$ with $|\beta| = d$. Each monomial of a product $g_1 \cdots g_d$ with $g_i \in \mathfrak{m}$ has degree at least $d$, and so has each monomial of a sum of such products. Thus $\operatorname{ord} f \ge d$ for each $f \in \mathfrak{m}^d$. Conversely, assume that $\operatorname{ord} f \ge d$. For each $\alpha$ with $|\alpha| \ge d$, choose $\beta(\alpha) \le \alpha$ with $|\beta(\alpha)| = d$. For each $\beta$ with $|\beta| = d$, let $g_\beta = \sum_{\beta(\alpha) = \beta} c_\alpha x^{\alpha - \beta}$. This is a power series, and $f = \sum_{|\beta| = d} x^\beta g_\beta$ is a finite sum. Thus $f \in \mathfrak{m}^d$. The second statement follows from Lemma 3.2.2 of Volume 1. $\square$

**Proposition 1.1.3.** Let $f, g \in R$.

1. $\operatorname{ord}(f + g) \ge \min(\operatorname{ord} f, \operatorname{ord} g)$, with equality if $\operatorname{ord} f \ne \operatorname{ord} g$.
2. $\operatorname{ord}(fg) = \operatorname{ord} f + \operatorname{ord} g$, and $\operatorname{in}(fg) = \operatorname{in}(f) \operatorname{in}(g)$ if $f, g \ne 0$.
3. Let $\varphi$ be an automorphism of the $K$-algebra $R$. Then $\operatorname{ord} \varphi(f) = \operatorname{ord} f$.

Lean: Singularity.min_ord_le_ord_add, Singularity.ord_add_of_lt, Singularity.ord_mul, Singularity.ord_algEquiv (for polynomials; for part (2) on the initial form, see Singularity.initialForm_mul with the weight $(1, \dots, 1)$)

*Proof.* (1) Each monomial of $f + g$ is a monomial of $f$ or of $g$. If $\operatorname{ord} f < \operatorname{ord} g$, then the monomials of $\operatorname{in}(f)$ do not cancel in $f + g$.

(2) We can assume that $f, g \ne 0$. Let $a = \operatorname{ord} f$ and $b = \operatorname{ord} g$. Each monomial of $fg$ has degree at least $a + b$, and the homogeneous part of degree $a + b$ of $fg$ is $\operatorname{in}(f) \operatorname{in}(g)$. This product is not $0$, because the polynomial ring $K[x]$ is a domain.

(3) The ring $R$ is local with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$. An automorphism of a local ring maps the maximal ideal onto itself, so $\varphi(\mathfrak{m}^d) = \mathfrak{m}^d$ for each $d$. The statement follows from Proposition 1.1.2. $\square$

Part (3) says that the order does not depend on the choice of the regular system of parameters. The initial form depends on it, but only through a linear change of the variables: the automorphism $\varphi$ induces a linear automorphism of $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$, and $\operatorname{in}(\varphi(f))$ is the image of $\operatorname{in}(f)$ under it.

The *order of an ideal* $I \subseteq R$ is $\operatorname{ord} I = \min \{ \operatorname{ord} f : f \in I \}$. By Proposition 1.1.2, it is the largest $d$ with $I \subseteq \mathfrak{m}^d$. For ideals $I$ and $J$, part (2) of Proposition 1.1.3 gives $\operatorname{ord}(IJ) = \operatorname{ord} I + \operatorname{ord} J$, because $IJ$ is generated by the products $fg$ and the order of a sum is at least the minimum of the orders.

### The order at a point

Let $f \in K[x]$ be a polynomial and let $a \in K^n$. The *order of $f$ at $a$* is $\operatorname{ord}_a f = \operatorname{ord} f(x + a)$, the order of the Taylor expansion of $f$ at $a$. It is the order of $f$ in the local ring of $\mathbb{A}^n$ at $a$, and it is the *multiplicity* of the hypersurface $X = V(f)$ at $a$. The point $a$ is in $X$ if and only if $\operatorname{ord}_a f \ge 1$, and a point of $X$ is a regular point if and only if $\operatorname{ord}_a f = 1$, by Proposition 1.1.2 and the definition of a regular local ring.

**Proposition 1.1.4.** Let $f \in K[x]$ and $d \in \mathbb{N}$. Then the set $\{ a \in K^n : \operatorname{ord}_a f \ge d \}$ is the common zero set of the polynomials $\partial^{(\beta)} f$ with $|\beta| < d$. In particular, it is closed in the Zariski topology.

Lean: none yet

*Proof.* By the definition of the operators $\partial^{(\beta)}$ in Section 6.3 of Volume 1, we have $f(a + t) = \sum_\beta (\partial^{(\beta)} f)(a) \, t^\beta$. So the coefficient of $t^\beta$ in the Taylor expansion of $f$ at $a$ is $(\partial^{(\beta)} f)(a)$, and $\operatorname{ord}_a f \ge d$ if and only if these coefficients vanish for $|\beta| < d$. $\square$

Thus the order is upper semicontinuous, and the points of maximal order of a hypersurface are a closed set. This set, the *top locus*, is where a resolution algorithm makes its first blowup. In characteristic $0$ we can use the usual derivatives $\partial^\beta$ in Proposition 1.1.4, because $\partial^{(\beta)} = \partial^\beta/\beta!$. In characteristic $p$ we cannot. For example, all the usual derivatives of $x^p$ vanish, but $\operatorname{ord}_a x^p = p$ only at the points with $a_1 = 0$. The Hasse derivatives detect this: $\partial^{(p)} x^p = 1$.

### Maximal contact in characteristic zero

Let $f$ have order $d$ at $0$, and assume that $K$ has characteristic $0$. Then some derivative $\partial^{\beta} f$ with $|\beta| = d - 1$ has order $1$. Its zero set is a smooth hypersurface $V$ that contains the points of order $d$ of $f$ near $0$, and this property stays true after each permissible blowup [Hau03]. Such a $V$ is a *hypersurface of maximal contact*. The induction on the dimension in the proofs of resolution in characteristic $0$ uses it.

In characteristic $p$ this fails. Let $f = z^p + F(x)$ with $\operatorname{ord} F \ge p$. Each derivative $\partial^\beta f$ with $|\beta| = p - 1$ is a derivative of $F$, and it does not involve $z$. Thus the hypersurface $z = 0$, the natural candidate, is not given by a derivative, and a hypersurface of maximal contact need not exist [Hau10]. For these equations, the second component of the invariant is the residual order of Section 1.3.

### Notes and references

The order is the multiplicity of Section 3.3 of Volume 1 for a hypersurface: if $f \ne 0$ has order $d$, then the local ring of $V(f)$ at $0$ has multiplicity $d$ [Mat, Section 14]. Proposition 1.1.2 is the reason that the order is intrinsic. The survey [Hau03] explains the role of the order and of maximal contact in characteristic $0$, and [Hau10] explains why maximal contact fails in characteristic $p$.
