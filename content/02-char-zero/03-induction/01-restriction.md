## Restriction to a smooth hypersurface

Let $S \subseteq X$ be a smooth hypersurface, and let $(I, m)$ be a marked ideal with $m \ge 1$. We consider blowup sequences of order at least $m$ whose centers lie in the birational transforms of $S$. In this section the divisor $E$ plays no role, and we take $E = \emptyset$. The aim is Theorem 3.1.3: the restriction of $I_i$ to $S_i$ alone does not see the points of order at least $m$, but the restrictions of the transforms of all the derivative ideals do.

The idea of the section is simple. A function $f$ on $X$ has order at least $m$ at a point of $S$ if and only if its Taylor expansion in the direction normal to $S$ has coefficients of the right orders: in coordinates with $S = (x_1 = 0)$, write $f = \sum_k f_k x_1^k$ with $f_k$ independent of $x_1$. Then $\operatorname{ord} f \ge m$ at a point of $S$ if and only if $\operatorname{ord} f_k \ge m - k$ there for each $k < m$. The coefficient $f_k$ is $(k!)^{-1} (\partial_1^k f)|_S$. So the derivative ideals restricted to $S$, with the marks $m - j$, see the points of order $m$ on $S$, and the theorem of this section says that they keep doing so after blowups with centers in $S$. The division by $k!$ is the place where the characteristic enters.

### Logarithmic derivations

**Definition 3.1.1 (logarithmic derivations).** A derivation $\delta$ of $\mathcal{O}_X$ is *tangent to $S$* if $\delta(\mathcal{O}_X(-S)) \subseteq \mathcal{O}_X(-S)$. For an ideal sheaf $B$, let $D(-\log S)(B)$ be the ideal generated by $B$ and the $\delta b$, with $b \in B$ and $\delta$ tangent to $S$. Let $D_S$ denote the derivative ideal on $S$, as in Definition 2.1.3, and write $B|_S = B \cdot \mathcal{O}_S$.

If $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are local coordinates with $S = (x_1 = 0)$, then a derivation $\delta = \sum a_i \partial_i$ is tangent to $S$ if and only if $a_1 = \delta(x_1) \in (x_1)$. So the tangent derivations are generated by $x_1 \partial_1, \partial_2, \dots, \partial_n$. A derivation tangent to $S$ induces a derivation $\delta_S$ of $\mathcal{O}_S$ with $(\delta b)|_S = \delta_S(b|_S)$. Hence
$$D(-\log S)(B)\big|_S \subseteq D_S(B|_S). \tag{3.1.1}$$
In the coordinates above, $\partial_2, \dots, \partial_n$ restrict to the derivations dual to the coordinates $x_2|_S, \dots, x_n|_S$ of $S$.

**Lemma 3.1.2.** Let $\pi \colon X' \to X$ be the blowup of a smooth center $Z \subseteq S$, let $S'$ be the birational transform of $S$ and let $\pi_S \colon S' \to S$ be the induced morphism.

1. $\pi_S$ is the blowup of $S$ along $Z$, and $F \cdot \mathcal{O}_{S'}$ is its exceptional divisor $F_S$.
2. Let $(A, a)$ be a marked ideal on $X$ with $Z \subseteq \operatorname{cosupp}(A, a)$. Then $Z \subseteq \operatorname{cosupp}(A|_S, a)$, and the restriction to $S'$ of the transform of $(A, a)$ is the transform of $(A|_S, a)$ under $\pi_S$.
3. For each ideal $B$ on $S$ and each closed point $x$, $\operatorname{ord}_x D_S^l(B) \ge \operatorname{ord}_x B - l$.

*Proof.* (1) The birational transform of a closed subscheme is the blowup of the subscheme along the restricted ideal [Har, Section II.7], and $I_Z \cdot \mathcal{O}_S$ is the ideal of $Z$ in $S$. So $I_Z \mathcal{O}_{S'} = \mathcal{O}_{X'}(-F) \mathcal{O}_{S'}$ is the ideal of the exceptional divisor of $\pi_S$. (2) Restriction to $S$ does not lower the order at points of $S$. The pullback commutes with restriction, because $S' \to X' \to X$ and $S' \to S \to X$ are the same map, and $\mathcal{O}_{X'}(aF)$ is invertible with restriction $\mathcal{O}_{S'}(aF_S)$ by (1). So $(\mathcal{O}(aF) \pi^* A)|_{S'} = \mathcal{O}(aF_S) \pi_S^*(A|_S)$. (3) A derivation maps $\mathfrak{m}_x^r$ into $\mathfrak{m}_x^{r-1}$. $\square$

The same computation shows a statement for single functions: if $\varphi \in I_Z^a$ and $g = y^{-a} \pi^* \varphi$ in a chart with $F = (y = 0)$, then $g|_{S'} = (y|_{S'})^{-a} \pi_S^*(\varphi|_S)$.

### The normal derivative

Let $x \in Z$ be a closed point. Since $Z \subseteq S$ are smooth, there are local coordinates $x_1, \dots, x_n$ at $x$ with $S = (x_1 = 0)$ and $Z = (x_1 = \dots = x_c = 0)$: take coordinates on $S$ in which $Z$ is a coordinate subspace, lift them to $X$, and add an equation $x_1$ of $S$. If $Z$ has codimension at least 2 in $X$, then $c \ge 2$. In the charts $U_j$ of Lemma 2.2.2, the transform $S'$ does not meet $U_1$, because $\pi^* x_1 = y_1$ is the equation of $F$ there. In $U_j$ with $2 \le j \le c$ we have $S' = (y_1 = 0)$, and Lemma 2.2.6 with $i = 1 < j$ gives, for $f \in I_Z^m$ and $g = y_j^{-m} \pi^* f$,
$$\partial_{y_1} g = y_j^{-(m-1)} \pi^*(\partial_1 f). \tag{3.1.2}$$
So the derivative of the transform in the direction normal to $S'$ is the transform of the normal derivative, with the mark lowered by one.

### The restriction theorem

**Theorem 3.1.3 (restriction).** Let $(X_r, I_r) \to \dots \to (X_0, I_0) = (X, I)$ be a blowup sequence of order at least $m$ for $(I, m)$ whose centers $Z_i$ lie in the birational transforms $S_i$ of $S$ and have codimension at least 2 in $X_i$. Let $J^{(j)}_i$ be as in Theorem 2.2.7, and let $T^{(j)}_i = J^{(j)}_i|_{S_i}$, with the mark $m - j$. Then for each $i$:

1. $S_{i+1} \to S_i$ is the blowup of $S_i$ along $Z_i$, and $T^{(j)}_{i+1}$ is the transform of $(T^{(j)}_i, m - j)$. So the $T^{(j)}_i$ are the successive transforms of $\big( D^j(I)|_S, \, m - j \big)$ on $S$.
2. $S_i \cap \operatorname{cosupp}(I_i, m) = \bigcap_{j=0}^{m-1} \operatorname{cosupp}(T^{(j)}_i, m - j)$.

*Proof.* (1) This is Lemma 3.1.2 (1) and (2), since $Z_i \subseteq \operatorname{cosupp}(J^{(j)}_i, m - j)$ by Theorem 2.2.7.

For (2) we first prove the following claim by induction on $i$: for $0 \le k \le m - 1$,
$$D^k(I_i)\big|_{S_i} \subseteq \sum_{j=0}^{k} D_{S_i}^{k-j}\big(T^{(j)}_i\big). \tag{3.1.3}$$
For $i = 0$ the left side is $T^{(k)}_0$. Assume (3.1.3) for $i$. Over $X_i \setminus Z_i$ the blowup is an isomorphism and both sides do not change. Let $x \in Z_i$ be a closed point, choose coordinates as above with $S_i = (x_1 = 0)$ and $Z_i = (x_1 = \dots = x_c = 0)$, $c \ge 2$, and work in the chart $U_c$, where $S_{i+1} = (y_1 = 0)$ and $y_2, \dots, y_n$ restrict to coordinates on $S_{i+1}$. The other charts $U_j$, $2 \le j < c$, are the same. Let $f$ run over generators of $I_i$ near $x$, and let $g = y_c^{-m} \pi_i^* f$. These $g$ generate $I_{i+1}$ on $U_c$, so $D^k(I_{i+1})$ is generated by the $\partial_y^\alpha g$ with $|\alpha| \le k$. Write $\alpha = (a, \alpha'')$ with $a$ the exponent of $\partial_{y_1}$. The derivations $\partial_{y_2}, \dots, \partial_{y_n}$ are tangent to $S_{i+1}$, so
$$\big( \partial_y^\alpha g \big)\big|_{S_{i+1}} = \partial_{y''}^{\alpha''} \Big( \big( \partial_{y_1}^a g \big)\big|_{S_{i+1}} \Big).$$
By (3.1.2), applied $a$ times, $\partial_{y_1}^a g = y_c^{-(m-a)} \pi_i^* \varphi$ with $\varphi = \partial_1^a f \in D^a(I_i)$. By the remark after Lemma 3.1.2, $(\partial_{y_1}^a g)|_{S_{i+1}}$ is the transform of $\varphi|_{S_i}$ with the mark $m - a$. By (3.1.3) for $i$,
$$\varphi|_{S_i} \in \sum_{j=0}^{a} D_{S_i}^{a-j}\big(T^{(j)}_i\big).$$
Each summand has order at least $(m - j) - (a - j) = m - a$ along $Z_i$, by Lemma 3.1.2 (3) and because $Z_i \subseteq \operatorname{cosupp}(T^{(j)}_i, m - j)$. So Lemma 2.2.4 and the inclusion (2.2.1) on $S_i$, applied $a - j$ times, give
$$\big( \partial_{y_1}^a g \big)\big|_{S_{i+1}} \in \sum_{j=0}^{a} D_{S_{i+1}}^{a-j}\Big( \text{transform of } \big(T^{(j)}_i, m - j\big) \Big) = \sum_{j=0}^{a} D_{S_{i+1}}^{a-j}\big(T^{(j)}_{i+1}\big),$$
where the equality is part (1). Applying $\partial_{y''}^{\alpha''}$ raises the exponent $a - j$ to $|\alpha| - j \le k - j$. This proves (3.1.3) for $i + 1$.

Now we prove (2). Let $x \in S_i \cap \operatorname{cosupp}(I_i, m)$. By Theorem 2.2.7, $J^{(j)}_i \subseteq D^j(I_i)$, which has order at least $m - j$ at $x$, and restriction does not lower the order. So $x$ lies in the right side. Conversely let $x$ be a closed point of $S_i$ with $\operatorname{ord}_x T^{(j)}_i \ge m - j$ for all $j < m$. Choose local coordinates at $x$ with $S_i = (x_1 = 0)$, and let $f \in I_i$. In $\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{X_i, x} = K[[x_1, \dots, x_n]]$ write
$$f = \sum_{k \ge 0} a_k \, x_1^k, \qquad a_k \in K[[x_2, \dots, x_n]] = \hat{\mathcal{O}}_{S_i, x}.$$
Then $k! \, a_k = (\partial_1^k f)|_{S_i}$. For $k < m$, the function $\partial_1^k f$ lies in $D^k(I_i)$, so by (3.1.3) and Lemma 3.1.2 (3) its restriction has order at least $\min_{j \le k} \big( (m - j) - (k - j) \big) = m - k$ at $x$. Since $k!$ is a unit, $\operatorname{ord}_x a_k \ge m - k$ for $k < m$. Hence $\operatorname{ord}_x f = \min_k (k + \operatorname{ord}_x a_k) \ge m$. $\square$

The chart formula (3.1.2) holds in every characteristic. The proof uses characteristic 0 in the last step, through the division by $k!$, and through Theorem 2.2.7, which uses Lemma 2.1.4.

**Remark 3.1.4.** The hypothesis on the codimension excludes centers that are components of $S_i$. A center of codimension 1 in $S_i$, which has codimension 2 in $X_i$, is allowed. Then the blowup of $S_i$ is the identity, and part (1) says that the transforms on $S_i$ are divided by the powers of the equation of the center.

**Example 3.1.5.** We check Theorem 3.1.3 on the running example $I = (y^2 - x^5)$ on $\mathbb{A}^2$ with $m = 2$, for two curves $S$. By Example 2.1.8, $D(I) = (y, x^4)$. We use the blowups of Example 2.2.9: first the origin, then the point $p_1$. Both centers are points, so they have codimension 2 in the surfaces $X_i$.

Let $S = (y = 0)$, with the coordinate $x$. Then $T^{(0)}_0 = I|_S = (x^5)$ with the mark 2, and $T^{(1)}_0 = D(I)|_S = (x^4)$ with the mark 1. Both have their points of high order only at the origin, so part (2) gives $S \cap \operatorname{cosupp}(I, 2) = \{0\}$. A point blowup of a curve is the identity, so part (1) says that the transforms on $S_1 = S$ are $x^{-2} \cdot (x^5) = (x^3)$ and $x^{-1} \cdot (x^4) = (x^3)$. We compare with $X_1$. In the chart with $y = y_1 x$, $S_1 = (y_1 = 0)$, $I_1 = (y_1^2 - x^3)$ and $J^{(1)}_1 = (y_1, x^3)$, and their restrictions to $S_1$ are $(x^3)$ and $(x^3)$. After the second blowup the transforms on $S_2$ are $(x)$ with the mark 2 and $(x^2)$ with the mark 1. The first has no point of order 2, so $S_2 \cap \operatorname{cosupp}(I_2, 2) = \emptyset$, as we found in Example 2.2.9.

Let $S' = (y = x)$, with the coordinate $x$. Then $T^{(0)}_0 = (x^2 - x^5)$ and $T^{(1)}_0 = (x, x^4) = (x)$, and again $S' \cap \operatorname{cosupp}(I, 2) = \{0\}$. After the first blowup, $S'_1 = (y_1 = 1)$ in the chart with $y = y_1 x$, and the transform of $T^{(0)}_0$ is $x^{-2} (x^2 - x^5) = (1 - x^3)$. It is a unit near $S'_1 \cap F$. So $S'_1 \cap \operatorname{cosupp}(I_1, 2) = \emptyset$, although $\operatorname{cosupp}(I_1, 2) = \{p_1\}$ is not empty. The curve $S'$ sees the points of order 2 at the start, but it loses them after one blowup. This is Example 2.3.5 again, seen from $S'$. Theorem 3.1.3 holds for every smooth hypersurface. But a smooth hypersurface that is not of maximal contact can lose the points of order $m$. By Theorem 2.3.3, a hypersurface of maximal contact keeps them.

*Example (the restriction of $I$ alone is not enough).* Let $f = y (y + x^2) = y^2 + x^2 y$ on $\mathbb{A}^2$, let $m = 2$ and let $S = (y = 0)$. Then $\partial_x f = 2xy$ and $\partial_y f = 2y + x^2$, so $D(f)$ is generated by $f$, $2xy$ and $2y + x^2$. Modulo $2y + x^2$ we have $y \equiv -x^2/2$, so $2xy \equiv -x^3$ and $f \equiv -x^4/4$, and hence
$$D(f) = (2y + x^2, \; x^3), \qquad \operatorname{cosupp}(f, 2) = V(2y + x^2, x^3) = \{0\}.$$
The restriction $T^{(0)}_0 = f|_S$ is the zero ideal, so its cosupport with the mark 2 is all of $S$. The restriction $T^{(1)}_0 = D(f)|_S = (x^2, x^3) = (x^2)$ has the cosupport $\{0\}$ with the mark 1. So both terms of part (2) of Theorem 3.1.3 are needed: the intersection is $\{0\} = S \cap \operatorname{cosupp}(f, 2)$, but the first term alone would give all of $S$. Here $S$ is a component of $V(f)$, and $S$ is not a curve of maximal contact, since $y \notin D(f)$. The curve $(2y + x^2 = 0)$ is one.

*Remark (why the centers have codimension at least 2).* Theorem 3.1.3 excludes the centers that are components of $S_i$. For such a center the transform of $S_i$ is empty, so part (1) says nothing, and part (2) would compare a set with the empty set. The hypothesis is also used in the proof: the formula (3.1.2) for the normal derivative needs a chart $U_j$ with $j \ge 2$, and for $c = 1$ there is no such chart. In the applications of Section 3.3 the centers of codimension 1 in $X$ appear, but only at the first step of Lemma 3.3.4, where they are removed from $S$ before the restriction theorem is used, and in the monomial case, Theorem 3.3.8, which does not use restriction.

*Example (the theorem for $(x^2, y^3)$).* Let $I = (x^2, y^3)$ with $m = 2$, and let $S = (x = 0)$ with the coordinate $y$. By Section 2.1, $D(I) = (x, y^2)$, so $T^{(0)}_0 = (y^3)$ with the mark 2 and $T^{(1)}_0 = (y^2)$ with the mark 1. Both have the cosupport $\{0\}$, and part (2) gives $S \cap \operatorname{cosupp}(I, 2) = \{0\}$. Blow up the origin. On $S_1 = S$ the transforms are $y^{-2} (y^3) = (y)$ with the mark 2 and $y^{-1} (y^2) = (y)$ with the mark 1. The first has no point of order 2, so $S_1 \cap \operatorname{cosupp}(I_1, 2) = \emptyset$. On $X_1$ the transform $I_1$ is $(x_1^2, y)$ in the chart $x = x_1 y$, which contains $S_1 = (x_1 = 0)$, and it has order 1 at each point. So $\operatorname{cosupp}(I_1, 2) = \emptyset$, as part (2) says.

*Remark (positive characteristic).* The proof of the claim (3.1.3) divides by $k!$ for $k < m$, and the formula $f_k = (k!)^{-1} (\partial_1^k f)|_S$ fails when $p \le k < m$. For example, in characteristic 2 let $f = y^2 + x^3$ on $\mathbb{A}^2$ with $m = 2$ and $S = (y = 0)$. The derivatives are $\partial_x f = 3x^2 = x^2$ and $\partial_y f = 0$, so $D(f)|_S = (x^3, x^2)|_S = (x^2)$, and the coefficient $y^2$ of $f$ is not visible in $\partial_y f$. In this case part (2) of the theorem still holds at the start, but the normal derivative $\partial_y$ carries no information. Volume 7 explains how the residual order replaces this information for the equations $z^p + F$.

### Notes and references

Theorem 3.1.3 is the going-up step of [Kol07, Chapter 3], Section "Restriction of derivatives and going up", in a form adapted to the coefficient ideal. Kollár proves it with D-balanced ideals. The claim (3.1.3) and its proof by the chart formula (3.1.2) are the version of his argument for the family of all derivative ideals.
