## The theorem of Abhyankar on quadratic sequences

We keep the notation of Section 1.1. Let $v$ be a valuation of $K$ that dominates $R$. Put $R_0 = R$. If $R_n$ is a regular local ring of dimension 2 that $v$ dominates, let $R_{n+1}$ be the quadratic transform of $R_n$ along $v$. If $R_n$ is a discrete valuation ring, the sequence stops, and $R_n = \mathcal{O}_v$ by Proposition 1.1.3. This gives the *quadratic sequence* $R_0 \subseteq R_1 \subseteq \dots$ along $v$. The aim of this section is the theorem of Abhyankar: the union of the sequence is $\mathcal{O}_v$. The invariant that decreases is the intersection number of two curves.

### Intersection numbers

**Definition 1.2.1.** Let $f, g \in R$ be nonzero elements with no common prime factor. The *intersection number* of $f$ and $g$ is the length
$$i_R(f, g) = \lambda_R(R/(f, g)).$$

**Lemma 1.2.2.** Let $f, g, g_1, g_2 \in R$ be nonzero.

1. If $f$ and $g$ have no common prime factor, then $i_R(f, g)$ is finite. It is 0 if and only if $f$ or $g$ is a unit.
2. If $f$ has no common prime factor with $g_1$ and with $g_2$, then $i_R(f, g_1g_2) = i_R(f, g_1) + i_R(f, g_2)$.

Lean: none yet

*Proof.* Part 1. The ring $R$ is a unique factorization domain [Mat, Theorem 20.3], so each height 1 prime of $R$ is principal, generated by a prime element. A height 1 prime that contains $f$ and $g$ would give a common prime factor. So the only prime that can contain $(f, g)$ is $\mathfrak{m}$, and $R/(f, g)$ has finite length. It is 0 if and only if $(f, g) = R$, that is, if $f$ or $g$ is a unit.

Part 2. Multiplication by $g_1$ gives an exact sequence
$$R/(f, g_2) \xrightarrow{\ g_1\ } R/(f, g_1g_2) \to R/(f, g_1) \to 0.$$
The first map is injective. Indeed, let $g_1r = sf + tg_1g_2$. Then $g_1(r - tg_2) = sf$. Since $f$ and $g_1$ have no common prime factor, $f$ divides $r - tg_2$, so $r \in (f, g_2)$. Lengths are additive in exact sequences. $\square$

### The branch formula

The next lemma computes a length on a curve from its branches. Let $C$ be a Noetherian local domain of dimension 1 with residue field $\kappa_C$, and assume that its integral closure $\bar{C}$ in $\operatorname{Frac} C$ is a finite $C$-module. Then $\bar{C}$ is a semilocal Noetherian normal domain of dimension 1. Its localizations $W_1, \dots, W_s$ at its maximal ideals are discrete valuation rings that dominate $C$. We call them the *branches* of $C$, and we write $v_i$ for the valuation of $W_i$ and $\kappa_i$ for its residue field. The degree $[\kappa_i : \kappa_C]$ is finite, because $\bar{C}$ is finite over $C$.

**Lemma 1.2.3 (branch formula).** For each $h \in C$ with $h \ne 0$,
$$\lambda_C(C/hC) = \sum_{i=1}^s [\kappa_i : \kappa_C]\, v_i(h).$$

Lean: none yet

*Proof.* Choose $c \in C$ with $c \ne 0$ and $c\bar{C} \subseteq C$, a common denominator of finitely many generators of $\bar{C}$. Then $N = \bar{C}/C$ is a finitely generated module over $C/cC$, which has finite length, so $N$ has finite length. Apply the snake lemma to multiplication by $h$ on the exact sequence $0 \to C \to \bar{C} \to N \to 0$. Multiplication by $h$ is injective on $C$ and on $\bar{C}$, so we get an exact sequence
$$0 \to \ker(h|_N) \to C/hC \to \bar{C}/h\bar{C} \to N/hN \to 0.$$
For an endomorphism of a module of finite length, the kernel and the cokernel have the same length. So $\lambda_C(C/hC) = \lambda_C(\bar{C}/h\bar{C})$. By the Chinese remainder theorem, $\bar{C}/h\bar{C}$ is the product of the rings $W_i/hW_i$. The ring $W_i/hW_i$ has a filtration by the powers of the maximal ideal with $v_i(h)$ quotients, and each quotient is isomorphic to $\kappa_i$, which has length $[\kappa_i : \kappa_C]$ as a $C$-module. $\square$

The finiteness assumption holds for the rings of this section. If $C$ is a domain that is essentially of finite type over a field, then $C$ is a localization of a finitely generated domain $A$ over the field, the integral closure of $A$ is a finite $A$-module [Eis95, Corollary 13.13], and integral closure commutes with localization.

### The multiplicity of a curve

Let $p \in \mathfrak{m}$ be a prime element of $R$ with $\operatorname{ord}(p) = b$ and initial form $P$, and let $C = R/pR$. It is a local domain of dimension 1. Let $W_1, \dots, W_s$ be its branches, and write $\bar{x}, \bar{y}$ for the images of $x, y$ in $C$.

**Lemma 1.2.4.** Assume that $p$ is not associate to $x$. Then
$$\sum_{i=1}^s [\kappa_i : \kappa]\, \min(v_i(\bar{x}), v_i(\bar{y})) = b,$$
with the convention $v_i(0) = \infty$.

Lean: none yet

*Proof.* The element $x$ is prime, because $R/xR$ is a discrete valuation ring. So $\bar{x} \ne 0$. Let $I$ be the set of $i$ with $v_i(\bar{x}) \le v_i(\bar{y})$, and $J$ its complement. If $p$ is associate to $y$, then $C = R/yR$ is a discrete valuation ring with uniformizer $\bar{x}$, so $s = 1$, both sides are 1, $I = \{1\}$ and $J$ is empty. Assume now that $p$ is associate to neither $x$ nor $y$. Then $\bar{x} \ne 0$ and $\bar{y} \ne 0$.

Consider the chart $B = R[y/x]$ and the strict transform $p' \in B$, with $p = x^bp'$. We claim that the kernel of the map $B \to \operatorname{Frac} C$ that extends $R \to C$ is $p'B$. The map exists, because $B \subseteq R_x$ and $\bar{x} \ne 0$. Its kernel is $pR_x \cap B = p'B_x \cap B$. Now $x$ is a nonzerodivisor on $B/p'B$: if $xc = p'd$ with $c, d \in B$, then $p'd \in xB$, and $p' \notin xB$ by Lemma 1.1.5, so $d = xd'$ because $xB$ is prime, and then $c = p'd'$. Thus $p'B_x \cap B = p'B$, and
$$C' = B/p'B \cong C[\bar{y}/\bar{x}] \subseteq \operatorname{Frac} C.$$
By Lemma 1.1.5, $C'/\bar{x}C' = B/(x, p')B \cong \kappa[Y]/(P(1, Y))$. This ring has dimension $\deg P(1, Y)$ over $\kappa$, so finitely many primes $\mathfrak{n}_1, \dots, \mathfrak{n}_t$ of $C'$ contain $\bar{x}$, and they are maximal.

Let $D_j = C'_{\mathfrak{n}_j}$. A branch of $D_j$ is a discrete valuation ring of $\operatorname{Frac} C$ that contains $C$, and its center on $C$ contains $\bar{x}$, so it dominates $C$. Thus it is one of the $W_i$, and $i \in I$ because $\bar{y}/\bar{x} \in W_i$. Conversely, for $i \in I$ the ring $W_i$ contains $C'$, and its center on $C'$ contains $\bar{x}$, so it is one of the $\mathfrak{n}_j$. So the branches of the rings $D_j$ are exactly the $W_i$ with $i \in I$, each for one $j$. Lemma 1.2.3 for $D_j$ and $h = \bar{x}$, multiplied by $[\kappa(\mathfrak{n}_j) : \kappa]$, gives
$$\dim_\kappa D_j/\bar{x}D_j = \sum_{W_i \text{ over } \mathfrak{n}_j} [\kappa_i : \kappa]\, v_i(\bar{x}).$$
The Artinian ring $C'/\bar{x}C'$ is the product of the rings $D_j/\bar{x}D_j$. The sum over $j$ gives
$$\sum_{i \in I} [\kappa_i : \kappa]\, v_i(\bar{x}) = \deg P(1, Y).$$

Now use the other chart $B^* = R[x/y]$ and the strict transform $p^*$ of $p$ in $B^*$. The same argument, with $x$ and $y$ exchanged, gives $C^* = B^*/p^*B^* \cong C[\bar{x}/\bar{y}]$ and $C^*/\bar{y}C^* \cong \kappa[X]/(P(X, 1))$. Let $\mathfrak{n}_0$ be the prime $(\bar{y}, \bar{x}/\bar{y})$ of $C^*$, which corresponds to the root $X = 0$. Its residue field is $\kappa$. The branches over $\mathfrak{n}_0$ are the $W_i$ with $v_i(\bar{x}/\bar{y}) > 0$, that is, the $W_i$ with $i \in J$. The local ring of $\kappa[X]/(P(X, 1))$ at $X = 0$ has dimension $\operatorname{ord}_X P(X, 1)$ over $\kappa$. If $X$ does not divide $P(X, 1)$, then $\mathfrak{n}_0$ is not a prime of $C^*$, $J$ is empty, and this order is 0. In both cases Lemma 1.2.3 gives
$$\sum_{i \in J} [\kappa_i : \kappa]\, v_i(\bar{y}) = \operatorname{ord}_X P(X, 1).$$

Write $P = \sum_{j=0}^b c_jX^{b-j}Y^j$. Then $\deg P(1, Y)$ is the largest $j$ with $c_j \ne 0$, and $\operatorname{ord}_X P(X, 1)$ is $b$ minus this $j$. So the two sums add up to $b$. For $i \in I$ the minimum is $v_i(\bar{x})$, and for $i \in J$ it is $v_i(\bar{y})$. $\square$

### The inequality of Noether

**Proposition 1.2.5 (inequality of Noether).** Assume case 2 of Proposition 1.1.3. Let $f, g \in R$ be nonzero elements with no common prime factor, with $a = \operatorname{ord}(f)$ and $b = \operatorname{ord}(g)$. Then
$$i_{R_1}(f', g') \le i_R(f, g) - ab.$$

Lean: none yet

*Proof.* By Lemma 1.1.6 the strict transforms $f'$ and $g'$ have no common prime factor, so the left side is defined. Write $g = u\,p_1 \cdots p_m$ with $u$ a unit and $p_j$ prime. Then $f$ has no common factor with each $p_j$, and $b = \sum \operatorname{ord}(p_j)$. By Lemma 1.1.5, $g' = u\,p_1' \cdots p_m'$, and by Lemma 1.1.6, $f'$ has no common factor with each $p_j'$. By Lemma 1.2.2, both sides of the inequality are additive in $g$. So we may assume that $g = p$ is a prime element of $R$ in $\mathfrak{m}$.

If $p$ is associate to $x$, then $b = 1$ and $p'$ is a unit, so the left side is 0. The right side is the length of $R/(f, x)$ minus $a$. Since $f \in \mathfrak{m}^a$, the image of $f$ in the discrete valuation ring $R/xR$ lies in the $a$-th power of its maximal ideal, so this length is at least $a$. The inequality holds.

Assume now that $p$ is not associate to $x$. Let $C = R/pR$, with branches $W_1, \dots, W_s$, and let $I$ and $J$ be as in the proof of Lemma 1.2.4. If $p$ is associate to $y$, then $J$ is empty. The description $C' = B/p'B \cong C[\bar{y}/\bar{x}]$ in that proof uses only $\bar{x} \ne 0$, so it holds in this case too. By Lemma 1.2.3,
$$i_R(f, p) = \lambda_C(C/\bar{f}C) = \sum_i [\kappa_i : \kappa]\, v_i(\bar{f}).$$
For $i \in J$ we have $v_i(\bar{y}) < v_i(\bar{x})$. Since $f$ is a sum of multiples of the monomials $x^jy^{a-j}$, we get $v_i(\bar{f}) \ge a\,v_i(\bar{y})$. For $i \in I$ the element $\bar{y}/\bar{x}$ lies in $W_i$, so $W_i$ contains $C' = C[\bar{y}/\bar{x}]$ and the image $\bar{f}'$ of $f'$. Then $v_i(\bar{f}) = a\,v_i(\bar{x}) + v_i(\bar{f}')$. With Lemma 1.2.4 we get
$$i_R(f, p) \ge ab + \sum_{i \in I} [\kappa_i : \kappa]\, v_i(\bar{f}').$$

Now let $\mathfrak{n}$ be the maximal ideal of $R_1$. If $p' \notin \mathfrak{n}$, then $i_{R_1}(f', p') = 0$, and we are done. Otherwise let $D = R_1/p'R_1$. It is the localization of $C' = B/p'B$ at the image of $\mathfrak{n}$, so it is a local domain of dimension 1, essentially of finite type over $k$. Its branches are among the $W_i$ with $i \in I$, as in the proof of Lemma 1.2.4. By Lemma 1.2.3 for $D$,
$$i_{R_1}(f', p') = \lambda_D(D/\bar{f}'D) = \sum_{W_i \text{ over } \mathfrak{n}} [\kappa_i : \kappa(\mathfrak{n})]\, v_i(\bar{f}').$$
Each term is at most the term $[\kappa_i : \kappa]\, v_i(\bar{f}')$ above, because $[\kappa_i : \kappa] = [\kappa_i : \kappa(\mathfrak{n})][\kappa(\mathfrak{n}) : \kappa]$. This proves the inequality. $\square$

Noether proved the equality $i_R(f, g) = ab + \sum i_{R'}(f', g')$, where the sum is over all points $R'$ of the first neighbourhood of $R$, weighted by the degrees of their residue fields. We need only the inequality at one point.

### The theorem

**Theorem 1.2.6 (Abhyankar).** Let $R$ be a regular local ring of dimension 2, essentially of finite type over a field, and let $v$ be a valuation of $K = \operatorname{Frac} R$ that dominates $R$. Let $(R_n)$ be the quadratic sequence along $v$. Then
$$\mathcal{O}_v = \bigcup_n R_n.$$

Node: SG0000004A · Lean: none yet

*Proof.* If the sequence stops at $R_n$, then $R_n = \mathcal{O}_v$ by Proposition 1.1.3, and there is nothing to prove. So assume that each $R_n$ has dimension 2. Let $z \in \mathcal{O}_v$ with $z \ne 0$. For each $n$ we write $z = F_n/G_n$ with $F_n, G_n \in R_n$ without common prime factor. Such a quotient exists for $n = 0$, because $R$ is a unique factorization domain with fraction field $K$.

Assume that $F_n$ and $G_n$ are both in the maximal ideal of $R_n$. Let $a = \operatorname{ord}(F_n)$ and $b = \operatorname{ord}(G_n)$, both at least 1, and let $c = \min(a, b)$. Let $x$ be the parameter of $R_n$ used in the quadratic transform. In $R_{n+1}$ we have $z = x^{a-b}F_n'/G_n'$. Put
$$F_{n+1} = x^{a-c}F_n', \qquad G_{n+1} = x^{b-c}G_n'.$$
Then $z = F_{n+1}/G_{n+1}$. These two elements have no common prime factor. Indeed, $F_n'$ and $G_n'$ have none by Lemma 1.1.6, $x$ divides neither of them by Lemma 1.1.5, and one of the exponents $a - c$ and $b - c$ is 0. By Lemma 1.2.2, Lemma 1.1.5 and Proposition 1.2.5, if $a \ge b$, then
$$i_{R_{n+1}}(F_{n+1}, G_{n+1}) = (a - b)\, i_{R_{n+1}}(x, G_n') + i_{R_{n+1}}(F_n', G_n') \le (a - b)b + i_{R_n}(F_n, G_n) - ab,$$
and the right side is $i_{R_n}(F_n, G_n) - b^2$. In the same way, if $b \ge a$, the intersection number drops by at least $a^2$. So it drops by at least $c^2 \ge 1$.

An intersection number is a nonnegative integer. So after finitely many steps one of $F_n$ and $G_n$ is a unit of $R_n$. If $G_n$ is a unit, then $z \in R_n$. If $F_n$ is a unit and $G_n$ is not, then $v(z) = -v(G_n) < 0$, because $v$ dominates $R_n$. This contradicts $z \in \mathcal{O}_v$. So $z \in R_n$ for some $n$. $\square$

Abhyankar proved the theorem for each regular local ring of dimension 2 [Abh56]. Our proof uses the finiteness of integral closure, which holds in the essentially finite type case. In higher dimension Zariski does not use the quadratic sequence alone, and his proof is much longer (Section 1.4).

### Consequences for local uniformization

**Corollary 1.2.7.** Let $k$ be a field of any characteristic, let $K/k$ be a function field of transcendence degree 2, and let $v$ be a valuation of $K/k$. Assume that there is an affine model $A \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$ of $K/k$ whose local ring at the center of $v$ is regular. Then $v$ has local uniformization.

Node: SG0000004B · Lean: none yet

*Proof.* We use Proposition 4.1.6 of Volume 3. Let $S \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$ be finite. Let $\mathfrak{p} = \mathfrak{m}_v \cap A$ and $R = A_\mathfrak{p}$. Then $\dim R \le \dim A = 2$. If $\dim R \le 1$, then $R$ is a field or a discrete valuation ring, so $R$ is a valuation ring that $\mathcal{O}_v$ dominates, and $R = \mathcal{O}_v$. Then $A' = A[S]$ satisfies $A \subseteq A' \subseteq A_\mathfrak{p}$, and Lemma 1.1.1 shows that the local ring of $A'$ at the center of $v$ is $R$, which is regular.

Let $\dim R = 2$, and let $(R_n)$ be the quadratic sequence along $v$. We show by induction that $R_n$ is the local ring at the center of $v$ of an affine model $A_n \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$. Put $A_0 = A$. Let $\mathfrak{p}_n$ be the center of $v$ on $A_n$, with $R_n = (A_n)_{\mathfrak{p}_n}$ of dimension 2. Choose the regular system of parameters $x_n, y_n$ of $R_n$ in $A_n$, which is possible after multiplication by units, with $v(x_n) \le v(y_n)$. Let $A_{n+1} = A_n[y_n/x_n] \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$. The chart $R_n[y_n/x_n]$ is the localization of $A_{n+1}$ at the multiplicative set $A_n \setminus \mathfrak{p}_n$, and this set does not meet the center $\mathfrak{p}_{n+1}$ of $v$ on $A_{n+1}$. So $R_{n+1} = (A_{n+1})_{\mathfrak{p}_{n+1}}$.

By Theorem 1.2.6, each $s \in S$ lies in some $R_n$. Since the sequence increases and $S$ is finite, $S \subseteq R_N$ for some $N$. Let $A' = A_N[S]$. Then $A_N \subseteq A' \subseteq R_N$, and by Lemma 1.1.1 the local ring of $A'$ at the center of $v$ is $R_N$, which is regular. $\square$

**Corollary 1.2.8.** Let $k$ be a field of any characteristic. Then each valuation of the field $k(x, y)$ of rational functions over $k$ has local uniformization.

*Proof.* Let $v$ be a valuation of $k(x, y)/k$. Let $u = x$ if $v(x) \ge 0$ and $u = x^{-1}$ otherwise, and choose $w \in \{y, y^{-1}\}$ in the same way. Then $u$ and $w$ are algebraically independent over $k$, and $k(u, w) = k(x, y)$. The polynomial ring $A = k[u, w]$ is an affine model contained in $\mathcal{O}_v$, and it is regular. So its local ring at the center of $v$ is regular, and Corollary 1.2.7 applies. $\square$

The corollary holds in each characteristic, also for valuations with a defect in the sense of Chapter 3. In characteristic $p$ the difficulty of surfaces is the passage from $k(x, y)$ to a finite extension $K$. The next section does this passage when the extension is étale at the valuation.

**Example 1.2.9.** We continue Example 1.1.7, with $v(x) = 1$ and $v(y) = \sqrt{2}$. The quadratic sequence is $R_n = k[x_n, y_n]_{(x_n, y_n)}$, where the pair of values $(v(x_n), v(y_n))$ follows the subtractive algorithm of Euclid. We start with $(1, \sqrt{2})$. Since $1 < \sqrt{2}$, we put $x_1 = x$ and $y_1 = y/x$, with values $(1, \sqrt{2} - 1)$. Now $v(y_1) < v(x_1)$, so $x_2 = x_1/y_1 = x^2/y$ and $y_2 = y_1$, with values $(2 - \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2} - 1)$. Again $v(y_2) < v(x_2)$, so $x_3 = x_2/y_2 = x^3/y^2$, with value $3 - 2\sqrt{2}$. The element $z = x^3/y^2$ has $v(z) > 0$, and it is not in $R_0$, $R_1$ or $R_2$, but it lies in $R_3$. Since $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational, the algorithm never stops, and each $R_n$ has dimension 2. Each element of $\mathcal{O}_v$ is in some $R_n$ by Theorem 1.2.6, although no $R_n$ is equal to $\mathcal{O}_v$. The ring $\mathcal{O}_v$ is not Noetherian, because $\Gamma_v = \mathbb{Z} + \mathbb{Z}\sqrt{2}$ is not discrete.

The proof of Theorem 1.2.6 is an algorithm. It writes $z$ as a quotient and follows the quotient up the sequence. The next example runs the algorithm on the element $z$ of Example 1.2.9 and records the intersection numbers.

**Example 1.2.10.** Keep the notation of Example 1.2.9, so $x_1 = x$, $y_1 = y/x$, $x_2 = x/y_1$, $y_2 = y_1$, $x_3 = x_2/y_2$ and $y_3 = y_2$. Let $z = x^3/y^2$.

1. Step 0. Put $F_0 = x^3$ and $G_0 = y^2$. Then $i_{R_0}(F_0, G_0) = \dim_k k[x, y]/(x^3, y^2) = 6$, with $a = 3$, $b = 2$ and $c = 2$. The parameter of the transform is $x$. The strict transforms are $F_0' = 1$ and $G_0' = y_1^2$, because $y^2 = x^2y_1^2$. So $F_1 = x$ and $G_1 = y_1^2$, and $i_{R_1}(F_1, G_1) = 2$. The drop is $4 = b^2$, so the bound in the proof is sharp here.
2. Step 1. Now $v(y_1) < v(x)$, so the parameter of the transform is $y_1$, and $x = y_1x_2$. Here $a = \operatorname{ord}(x) = 1$, $b = \operatorname{ord}(y_1^2) = 2$ and $c = 1$. The strict transforms are $F_1' = x_2$ and $G_1' = 1$. So $F_2 = x_2$ and $G_2 = y_1^{b - c} = y_2$, and $i_{R_2}(F_2, G_2) = 1$. The drop is $1 = a^2$.
3. Step 2. Again $v(y_2) < v(x_2)$, and $x_2 = y_2x_3$. Here $a = b = c = 1$, $F_2' = x_3$ and $G_2' = 1$. So $F_3 = x_3$ and $G_3 = 1$.

The intersection numbers are $6, 2, 1, 0$. The algorithm stops at $n = 3$ with $G_3$ a unit, and $z = x_3 \in R_3$. The same computation gives an equality case of Proposition 1.2.5. Let $f = y^2 - x^3$ and $g = y$ in $R_0$. Then $i_{R_0}(f, g) = \dim_k k[x, y]/(y, x^3) = 3$, with $a = 2$ and $b = 1$. The strict transforms are $f' = y_1^2 - x$ and $g' = y_1$, and $i_{R_1}(f', g') = \dim_k k[x, y_1]/(y_1, x) = 1 = 3 - ab$.

Example 1.2.9 has rank 1. The quadratic sequence of a valuation of rank 2 behaves in another way. The same parameter is used at each step, and the centers follow a curve.

**Example 1.2.11.** Let $R = k[x, y]_{(x, y)}$, and let $v$ be the valuation of rank 2 of Volume 3, Example 1.6.4, with values in $\mathbb{Z}^2$ in the lexicographic order, $v(x) = (0, 1)$ and $v(y) = (1, 0)$. Put $y_n = y/x^n$ for $n \ge 0$. We claim that $R_n = k[x, y_n]_{(x, y_n)}$ for each $n$. This holds for $n = 0$. Assume it for $n$. Then $v(y_n) = (1, -n)$, and $(1, -n) > (0, 1)$ in the lexicographic order. So $v(x) < v(y_n)$, the parameter of the transform is $x$, and $B = R_n[y_{n+1}]$. Since $v(y_{n+1}) = (1, -n-1) > 0$, the residue of $y_{n+1}$ is 0, so $h = Y$ and $R_{n+1} = k[x, y_{n+1}]_{(x, y_{n+1})}$.

1. Distinct monomials $x^iy_n^j$ have the distinct values $(j, i - nj)$. So the value of a polynomial in $x$ and $y_n$ is the least value of its monomials. An element $g \in k[x, y_n]$ outside $(x, y_n)$ has a nonzero constant term, so $v(g) = 0$.
2. Let $\mathfrak{p}$ be the prime of $\mathcal{O}_v$ strictly between 0 and $\mathfrak{m}_v$. It is the set of elements whose value has first coordinate at least 1. By part 1, $\mathfrak{p} \cap R_n = y_nR_n$. The localization of $R_n$ at $y_nR_n$ is $k(x)[y]_{(y)}$ for each $n$, because $x$ is a unit there and $y_n = y/x^n$. So $\mathfrak{p}$ has the same center on each $R_n$, the strict transform of the line $y = 0$. The closed center of $v$ on $R_n$ is the point where this curve meets the exceptional curve $x = 0$ of the last transform.
3. The proof of Theorem 1.2.6 puts $z = y/x^n$ in $R_n$. Start with $F_0 = y$ and $G_0 = x^n$, with intersection number $n$. At each step $a = 1$, the strict transform of $F_j = y_j$ is $y_{j+1}$, and $G_{j+1} = x^{n-j-1}$. The intersection number drops by $1 = a^2$ at each step. After $n$ steps $G_n = 1$. The element $y/x^n$ is not in $R_{n-1}$, because $x$ does not divide $y_{n-1}$ in the factorial ring $R_{n-1}$.

So the sequence never stops, although $\Gamma_v$ is finitely generated. A Noetherian valuation ring is a field or a discrete valuation ring, and $\dim \mathcal{O}_v = 2$. So $\mathcal{O}_v$ is not Noetherian, and no $R_n$ is equal to $\mathcal{O}_v$.

### Notes and references

Theorem 1.2.6 is due to Abhyankar [Abh56]. Zariski used quadratic transforms to prove the resolution of surfaces in characteristic 0 [Zar39]. The inequality of Noether and the proof by intersection numbers are classical, and [Cut04] uses the same tools. The finiteness of integral closure is [Eis95, Corollary 13.13]. None of the results of this section is in the Lean library. A Lean proof of Theorem 1.2.6 would need the intersection numbers and the branch formula, which are not in Mathlib.
