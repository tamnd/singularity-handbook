# Inseparable local uniformization

Temkin proved that each valuation of a function field has local uniformization after a finite purely inseparable extension of the function field [Tem13]. The theorem holds in each characteristic and in each dimension. In characteristic 0 it is the theorem of Zariski. In characteristic $p$ it is the strongest known result on local uniformization in dimension at least 4, and it is the starting point of route R2 of Volume 9, Chapter 2.

Section 4.1 states the theorem in the form of [Tem13], shows that it implies the Lean statement `Singularity.localUniformization_inseparable`, and describes the structure of the proof. Section 4.2 explains why the theorem does not give local uniformization of the valuation itself, with an example from Volume 4, and it connects the remaining problem with the conjectures of Volume 9, Section 2.3. Section 4.3 proves, over a perfect ground field, that the theorem of Temkin reduces local uniformization to one purely inseparable step of degree $p$. It proves this step when the equation of the step is monomial modulo $p$-th powers, and it states the missing input, weak log monomialization along a valuation, as a hypothesis that it does not prove. Section 4.4 replaces this hypothesis by a weaker one: the log Jacobian ideal of the equation, which does not change when we add a $p$-th power, is principal on a regular model along the valuation. It proves that this condition is sufficient for the step of degree $p$, and it says that the existence of such a model is open in transcendence degree 4 and more. Section 4.5 treats one case of the step of degree $p$ along a valuation with a defect. The base is an Abhyankar field of rank one, the field is an immediate extension of the base of transcendence degree one, and the equation has the form $z^p = g(x^p) + Gx + b$. It proves that the field is henselian rational over the base and that the valuation has local uniformization, with a lemma on lattice points in a cone. It does not treat a general equation, a general base or the transcendence degree 4.

We do not prove the theorem of Temkin. The proofs in this chapter deduce consequences from it.
