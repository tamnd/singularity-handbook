## Weak log monomialization and the step of degree $p$

Section 4.2 stated a remark of Temkin [Tem13, Remark 1.3.5]. It says that his theorem reduces local uniformization to purely inseparable steps of degree $p$, and Section 4.2 did not prove it. In this section we prove a form of this reduction when the ground field $k$ is perfect. The form is precise. We define one step of degree $p$ (Definition 4.3.1), and we prove that the theorem of Temkin and this step give local uniformization (Theorem 4.3.2). Then we study one step. A step is given by an equation $\theta^p = f$ over a regular local ring. We prove that the step holds when $f$ has a special form, which we call *monomial modulo $p$-th powers* (Theorem 4.3.14). The proof is toric: a change of monomial variables, as in Section 2.1, makes the new local ring regular. The missing input is a statement that brings $f$ to this form on a regular model of the smaller field. We call it *weak log monomialization along a valuation* (Definition 4.3.15). It is a hypothesis. This section does not prove it, and it is open in transcendence degree 4. So the open case of local uniformization, Volume 9, Conjecture 2.3.1, stays open. What this section proves is that it follows from weak log monomialization over a perfect field (Corollary 4.3.17).

### Regular centers and the reduction to one step of degree $p$

We use the affine form of local uniformization (Volume 3, Proposition 4.1.6). Let $K/k$ be a field extension, let $v$ be a valuation of $K$ with valuation ring $\mathcal{O}_v$ and maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}_v$, and let $S \subseteq K$. We say that *$v$ has a regular center on an affine model that contains $S$* if there is an affine model $A$ of $K/k$ with $S \subseteq A \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$ such that the local ring $A_{\mathfrak{m}_v \cap A}$ is regular. An affine model is a finitely generated $k$-algebra $A \subseteq K$ with fraction field $K$. So the condition implies that $K/k$ is finitely generated and that $k \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$, that is, $v$ is trivial on $k$. In Lean this condition is `Singularity.HasRegularCenter`. By Volume 3, Proposition 4.1.6, a valuation $v$ of a function field $K/k$ has local uniformization if and only if it has a regular center on an affine model that contains $S$, for each finite set $S \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$.

The theorem of Temkin gives one regular center on one model of a field $L$ above $K$. To go down from $L$ to $K$ we need a step that turns one regular center below into one regular center above. The next definition states this step for an extension of degree $p$.

**Definition 4.3.1 (the step of degree $p$).** Let $k$ be a field of characteristic $p > 0$, and let $n \ge 0$. *The step of degree $p$ holds in transcendence degree $n$ over $k$* if the following is true. Let $E$ be a field extension of $k$ with $\operatorname{trdeg}_k E = n$, let $E'/E$ be a purely inseparable extension of degree $p$, let $w$ be a valuation of $E'$, and let $v$ be the restriction of $w$ to $E$. Let $S \subseteq E$ be finite. If $v$ has a regular center on an affine model of $E/k$ that contains $S$, then $w$ has a regular center on an affine model of $E'/k$ that contains $S$.

The step is a hypothesis. For $n \le 3$ it holds: in this case local uniformization is known in transcendence degree $n$ (Volume 3, Proposition 4.1.7 for $n \le 1$, Volume 5, Proposition 3.1.2 for $n = 2$, and the cited Volume 3, Theorem 4.1.5 for $n = 3$). Local uniformization of $w$, applied to the finite set $S \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v \subseteq \mathcal{O}_w$, gives the conclusion of the step at once. For $n = 4$ the step is open.

We use two facts on fields of characteristic $p$. First, the map $c \mapsto c^p$ is an injective ring homomorphism. Second, let $F$ be a field of characteristic $p$, and let $c \in F$ be an element that is not a $p$-th power in $F$. Then the polynomial $X^p - c$ is irreducible over $F$. Here is the proof. In a splitting field let $\gamma^p = c$. Then $X^p - c = (X - \gamma)^p$. A monic factor of $X^p - c$ in $F[X]$ of degree $j$ is $(X - \gamma)^j$, by unique factorization over the splitting field. If $0 < j < p$, its coefficient of $X^{j-1}$ is $-j\gamma$, and it lies in $F$. Since $j$ is a unit in $F$, we get $\gamma \in F$ and $c = \gamma^p$, which is false. So there is no such factor.

**Theorem 4.3.2 (the reduction).** Let $k$ be a perfect field of characteristic $p > 0$, and let $n \ge 0$. Assume that the step of degree $p$ holds in transcendence degree $n$ over $k$. Then for each function field $K/k$ of transcendence degree $n$, each valuation of $K/k$ has local uniformization.

The proof uses the theorem of Temkin (Theorem 4.1.1), which the handbook cites and does not prove. In Lean the theorem of Temkin is the hypothesis `Singularity.localUniformization_inseparable`.

Here is the idea. Temkin gives a regular center in a field $L$ above $K$. A power $q$ of $p$ maps $L$ into $K$, and the map $y \mapsto y^q$ is a ring isomorphism from $L$ onto a subfield $K_0$ of $K$. Since $k$ is perfect, $K_0$ contains $k$, and the isomorphism carries the regular center to a regular center for the restriction of $v$ to $K_0$. Now $K/K_0$ is a tower of steps of degree $p$, and we go up the tower with the hypothesis. At the top we have a regular center for $v$, but only on a model that contains the $q$-th powers of the given elements. A regular local ring is integrally closed, so it contains the elements themselves.

*Proof.* Let $v$ be a valuation of $K/k$, and let $S \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$ be finite. By Volume 3, Proposition 4.1.6, it is enough to find a regular center of $v$ on an affine model of $K/k$ that contains $S$.

The theorem of Temkin. By Theorem 4.1.1 in the form of Proposition 4.1.2, there are a finite purely inseparable extension $L/K$, the extension $w$ of $v$ to $L$, and an affine model $B$ of $L/k$ with $S \subseteq B \subseteq \mathcal{O}_w$, such that $B_\mathfrak{q}$ is regular, where $\mathfrak{q} = \mathfrak{m}_w \cap B$. We have $\mathcal{O}_w \cap K = \mathcal{O}_v$.

The Frobenius descent. By Volume 1, Lemma 2.4.1, there is a power $q = p^N$ with $y^q \in K$ for each $y \in L$. Let $\varphi \colon L \to K$ be the map $\varphi(y) = y^q$. It is an injective ring homomorphism. Let $K_0 = \varphi(L)$. It is a subfield of $K$, and $\varphi \colon L \to K_0$ is a ring isomorphism. Let $c \in k$. Since $k$ is perfect, $c = d^q$ for some $d \in k$, so $c = \varphi(d) \in K_0$. Thus $k \subseteq K_0$. The map $\varphi$ is not $k$-linear, because $\varphi(cy) = c^q\varphi(y)$, but it maps $k$ onto $k$. For each $x \in K$ we have $x^q = \varphi(x) \in K_0$. So $K/K_0$ is purely inseparable. The field $K$ is generated over $k$ by finitely many elements $t_1, \dots, t_m$, so $K = K_0(t_1, \dots, t_m)$, and each $t_i$ is a root of $X^q - t_i^q \in K_0[X]$. So $K/K_0$ is finite. In particular $\operatorname{trdeg}_k K_0 = \operatorname{trdeg}_k K = n$.

Let $v_0$ be the restriction of $v$ to $K_0$. Its valuation ring is $\mathcal{O}_{v_0} = \mathcal{O}_w \cap K_0$. We show that $\varphi(\mathcal{O}_w) = \mathcal{O}_{v_0}$. Let $y \in L$. Then $\varphi(y) = y^q$ lies in $K_0$, so $\varphi(y) \in \mathcal{O}_{v_0}$ if and only if $y^q \in \mathcal{O}_w$. In a valuation ring, $y^q \in \mathcal{O}_w$ implies $y \in \mathcal{O}_w$: otherwise $y^{-1} \in \mathfrak{m}_w$, so $y^{-q} \in \mathfrak{m}_w$ and $y^q \notin \mathcal{O}_w$. So $\varphi(y) \in \mathcal{O}_{v_0}$ if and only if $y \in \mathcal{O}_w$. A ring isomorphism of local rings maps the maximal ideal onto the maximal ideal, so $\varphi(\mathfrak{m}_w) = \mathfrak{m}_{v_0}$.

Let $B_0 = \varphi(B)$. Write $B = k[b_1, \dots, b_m]$. Since $\varphi(k) = k$, the ring $B_0$ is the $k$-algebra $k[\varphi(b_1), \dots, \varphi(b_m)]$. Its fraction field is $\varphi(L) = K_0$. So $B_0$ is an affine model of $K_0/k$, and $B_0 \subseteq \varphi(\mathcal{O}_w) = \mathcal{O}_{v_0}$. The center of $v_0$ on $B_0$ is $\mathfrak{m}_{v_0} \cap B_0 = \varphi(\mathfrak{q})$, and $\varphi$ induces a ring isomorphism $B_\mathfrak{q} \cong (B_0)_{\varphi(\mathfrak{q})}$. So this local ring is regular. For $s \in S$ we have $s \in B$, so $s^q = \varphi(s) \in B_0$. Let $S^q = \{s^q : s \in S\}$. We have shown: $v_0$ has a regular center on an affine model of $K_0/k$ that contains $S^q$.

The tower. We build fields $K_0 = M_0 \subsetneq M_1 \subsetneq \dots \subsetneq M_r = K$ with $[M_i : M_{i-1}] = p$. Let $M_{i-1}$ be built, with $K_0 \subseteq M_{i-1} \subseteq K$, and assume that $M_{i-1} \ne K$. Choose $a \in K \setminus M_{i-1}$. Since $a^q \in K_0 \subseteq M_{i-1}$, there is a least $e \ge 1$ with $a^{p^e} \in M_{i-1}$. Let $b = a^{p^{e-1}}$. Then $b \notin M_{i-1}$ and $b^p \in M_{i-1}$. The element $b^p$ is not a $p$-th power in $M_{i-1}$: if $b^p = c^p$ with $c \in M_{i-1}$, then $b = c$ by the injectivity of $c \mapsto c^p$. So $X^p - b^p$ is irreducible over $M_{i-1}$, and $M_i = M_{i-1}(b)$ has degree $p$ over $M_{i-1}$. It is purely inseparable over $M_{i-1}$, because each $x \in K$ has $x^q \in K_0$. Since $[K : K_0]$ is finite, the construction stops with $M_r = K$. Each $M_i$ contains $K_0$, so $K$ is algebraic over $M_i$ and $\operatorname{trdeg}_k M_i = n$.

Let $v_i$ be the restriction of $v$ to $M_i$. We show by induction on $i$ that $v_i$ has a regular center on an affine model of $M_i/k$ that contains $S^q$. For $i = 0$ this is the Frobenius descent. Let $i \ge 1$. The extension $M_i/M_{i-1}$ is purely inseparable of degree $p$, the field $M_{i-1}$ has transcendence degree $n$ over $k$, the restriction of $v_i$ to $M_{i-1}$ is $v_{i-1}$, and $S^q \subseteq K_0 \subseteq M_{i-1}$ is finite. By the induction hypothesis and the step of degree $p$, the valuation $v_i$ has a regular center on an affine model of $M_i/k$ that contains $S^q$. For $i = r$ we get an affine model $A$ of $K/k$ with $S^q \subseteq A \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$ such that $R = A_\mathfrak{p}$ is regular, where $\mathfrak{p} = \mathfrak{m}_v \cap A$.

The roots. The fraction field of $R$ is $K$, because $A \subseteq R \subseteq K$. Let $s \in S$. Then $s^q \in A \subseteq R$, so $s \in R$ by Volume 1, Corollary 1.3.13. Let $A' = A[S]$. It is a finitely generated $k$-algebra with $A \subseteq A' \subseteq K$, so it is an affine model of $K/k$, and $S \subseteq A'$. The elements of $A \setminus \mathfrak{p}$ are units of $\mathcal{O}_v$, so $R \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$. Thus $A \subseteq A' \subseteq A_\mathfrak{p} \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$. By Volume 5, Lemma 1.1.1, the local ring of $A'$ at the center of $v$ is $A_\mathfrak{p} = R$, which is regular. $\square$

The hypothesis that $k$ is perfect enters only once, in the proof that $K_0$ contains $k$. In Section 4.2 this was the point where the remark of Temkin needs the passage to the compositum $k \cdot L^{p^n}$ and the hypothesis that $[k : k^p]$ is finite. Over a perfect field the compositum is not needed.

### The element $f$ and the monomial form

From now on we study one step. Let $E'/E$ be purely inseparable of degree $p$, and let $A$ be an affine model of $E/k$. The next lemma chooses an equation for $E'$ over $A$.

**Lemma 4.3.3 (the choice of $\theta$ and $f$).** Let $E'/E$ be a purely inseparable extension of degree $p$ of fields of characteristic $p$, and let $A \subseteq E$ be a subring with fraction field $E$.

1. There is an element $\theta \in E' \setminus E$. For each $\theta \in E' \setminus E$ we have $\theta^p \in E$ and $E' = E(\theta)$.
2. For each $\theta \in E' \setminus E$ there is $b \in A$ with $b \ne 0$ such that $(b\theta)^p \in A$. The element $b\theta$ is again in $E' \setminus E$.
3. Let $\theta \in E' \setminus E$, and let $f = \theta^p$. Then $f$ is not a $p$-th power in $E$.

*Proof.* Part 1. Since $[E' : E] = p > 1$, there is $\theta \in E' \setminus E$. Let $\theta$ be such an element. The extension is purely inseparable, so there is a least $e \ge 1$ with $\theta^{p^e} \in E$. Let $b = \theta^{p^{e-1}}$. Then $b \notin E$ and $b^p \in E$. As in the proof of Theorem 4.3.2, the element $b^p$ is not a $p$-th power in $E$, so $X^p - b^p$ is irreducible over $E$ and $[E(b) : E] = p$. Thus $E(b) = E'$. Each $y \in E'$ has the form $y = \sum_{i < p} c_ib^i$ with $c_i \in E$, so $y^p = \sum_i c_i^pb^{ip} \in E$. In particular $\theta^p \in E$. The degree $[E(\theta) : E]$ divides $p$ and is not 1, so $E(\theta) = E'$.

Part 2. Write $\theta^p = a/b$ with $a, b \in A$ and $b \ne 0$. Then $(b\theta)^p = b^pa/b = ab^{p-1} \in A$. If $b\theta \in E$, then $\theta \in E$, which is false.

Part 3. Assume that $f = g^p$ with $g \in E$. In characteristic $p$ we have $(\theta - g)^p = \theta^p - g^p = 0$, so $\theta = g \in E$, which is false. $\square$

So each step has the form $E' = E(\theta)$ with $\theta^p = f \in A$, where $f$ is not a $p$-th power in $E$. The next definition gives the form of $f$ that the toric step can treat.

**Definition 4.3.4 (monomial modulo $p$-th powers).** Let $R$ be a regular local ring of characteristic $p$, with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ and residue field $\kappa$. An element $f \in R$ is *monomial modulo $p$-th powers in $R$* if there are elements $h, g, u \in R$, a regular system of parameters $x_1, \dots, x_d$ of $R$ and integers $a_1, \dots, a_d \ge 0$ such that $u$ is a unit, $g \ne 0$,
$$f = h^p + g^p\, u\, x_1^{a_1} \cdots x_d^{a_d},$$
and one of these two conditions holds:

1. (the ramified type) $p \nmid a_i$ for some $i$;
2. (the residual type) $a_i = 0$ for all $i$, and the class $\bar{u}$ of $u$ in $\kappa$ is not a $p$-th power in $\kappa$.

*Remark (the names of the two types).* Let $f = \theta^p$ with $\theta$ in an extension field $E'$ of the fraction field $E$ of $R$, and let $z = (\theta - h)/g$. Then $z^p = u x_1^{a_1} \cdots x_d^{a_d}$. In the ramified type, the order of $z^p$ along the divisor $x_i = 0$ is $a_i$, and it is not divisible by $p$ (this order is defined in the proof of Lemma 4.3.7). So each extension of this order to $E'$ gives $z$ the value $a_i/p$, and its ramification index is $p$: the extension $E'/E$ is ramified along $x_i = 0$. In the residual type, $z^p = u$ is a unit, and Corollary 4.3.9 shows that $R[z]$ is a regular local ring with residue field $\kappa[X]/(X^p - \bar{u}) = \kappa(\bar{u}^{1/p})$. This is a purely inseparable extension of $\kappa$ of degree $p$. So the step is seen by the residue field.

*Remark (the end cases of Volume 7).* Volume 7, Theorem 3.5.3, finds the equations $z^p + F = 0$ over $R = K[[x_1, \dots, x_n]]$, with $K$ algebraically closed, at the end of its route. After a change of the coordinates, $F = x^r$ with $p \nmid r_i$ for some $i$, or $F = x^rx_j$ with $r_j = 0$. Here $f = -F$. Both cases are of the ramified type, with $h = 0$, $g = 1$ and $u = -1$. In the second case the exponent of $x_j$ is 1. The residual type does not occur when the residue field $\kappa$ is perfect, because then each element of $\kappa$ is a $p$-th power. For example, it does not occur at a closed point of a variety over a perfect field. It occurs when the center is not a closed point, as the next example shows.

The next two examples show why the definition has the residual type and the factor $g^p$. Both take place in a discrete valuation ring with a residue field that is not perfect. Let $k$ be a field of characteristic $p$, and let $R = k[x, t]_{(x)}$ be the local ring of the plane $\mathbb{A}^2_k$ at the generic point of the line $x = 0$. It is a discrete valuation ring with parameter $x$ and residue field $k(t)$. We write $\operatorname{ord}$ for its valuation. The element $t$ is not a $p$-th power in $k(t)$: if $t = (P/Q)^p$ with polynomials $P, Q \in k[t]$, then $tQ^p = P^p$, and the two sides have degrees $1 + p\deg Q$ and $p \deg P$, which differ modulo $p$. The regular systems of parameters of $R$ are the elements $x' = \varepsilon x$ with $\varepsilon$ a unit.

**Example 4.3.5 (the residual type is needed).** Let $f = t + x^p$. Then $f = x^p + 1^p \cdot t$ with $h = x$, $g = 1$ and $u = t$, a unit of $R$. The class of $u$ is $t$, which is not a $p$-th power in $k(t)$. So $f$ is of the residual type. It is not of the ramified type. In the ramified type $f = h^p + g^pu(x')^a$ with $p \nmid a$, so $a \ge 1$ and $f \equiv h^p$ modulo $x$. Then the class $t$ of $f$ is the $p$-th power of the class of $h$ in $k(t)$, which is false.

**Example 4.3.6 (the factor $g^p$ is needed).** Let $f = x^pt$. Then $f = 0^p + x^p \cdot t$ with $h = 0$, $g = x$ and $u = t$. So $f$ is of the residual type, with $g$ in the maximal ideal. We show that $f$ has no form of Definition 4.3.4 with a unit $g$. First, $\operatorname{ord}(f - h^p)$ is divisible by $p$ for each $h \in R$. If $\operatorname{ord}(h) = 0$, then $\operatorname{ord}(f - h^p) = 0$, since $\operatorname{ord}(f) = p$. If $\operatorname{ord}(h) \ge 1$, write $h = xh_1$. Then $f - h^p = x^p(t - h_1^p)$, and the class of $t - h_1^p$ in $k(t)$ is not 0, because $t$ is not a $p$-th power. So $\operatorname{ord}(f - h^p) = p$. In the ramified type, $\operatorname{ord}(f - h^p) = p\operatorname{ord}(g) + a$ with $p \nmid a$, so this type is impossible. In the residual type with a unit $g$, the element $f - h^p = g^pu$ is a unit, so $\operatorname{ord}(h) = 0$. Then $g^pu \equiv -h^p$ modulo $x$, and the class of $u$ is the $p$-th power of the class of $-h/g$, so the condition of the residual type fails. Thus $f$ is monomial modulo $p$-th powers, but only with $g \in \mathfrak{m}$.

*Remark (discrete valuation rings).* Let $R$ be a discrete valuation ring that is a localization of a finitely generated algebra over a field of characteristic $p$, with fraction field $E$ and parameter $x$, and let $f \in R$ be an element that is not a $p$-th power in $E$. Then $f$ is monomial modulo $p$-th powers in $R$. We only sketch the proof. Let $N$ be the supremum of $\operatorname{ord}(f - h^p)$ over $h \in R$. Assume first that $N = \infty$. Then there are $h_n \in R$ with $\operatorname{ord}(f - h_n^p) \to \infty$. Since $(h_n - h_m)^p = (f - h_m^p) - (f - h_n^p)$, the sequence $(h_n)$ is a Cauchy sequence, and its limit $\hat{h}$ in the completion $\hat{R}$ has $\hat{h}^p = f$. The ring $R$ is excellent [Mat, Section 32], so the fraction field of $\hat{R}$ is separable over $E$. Then $E(f^{1/p}) \otimes_E \operatorname{Frac}(\hat{R})$ would be reduced, but it contains the nilpotent element $f^{1/p} \otimes 1 - 1 \otimes \hat{h}$, unless $f^{1/p} \in E$. So $N$ is finite, and $N = \operatorname{ord}(f - h^p)$ for some $h$. Write $f - h^p = ux^N$ with a unit $u$. If $p \nmid N$, then $f$ is of the ramified type, with $g = 1$. If $N = pm$, then the class of $u$ is not a $p$-th power: if $\bar{u} = \bar{c}^p$, then $f - (h + cx^m)^p = (u - c^p)x^{pm}$ has order larger than $N$. So $f$ is of the residual type, with $g = x^m$. Examples 4.3.5 and 4.3.6 are the cases $N = 0$ and $N = p$. The handbook treats discrete valuations in full later. We do not use this remark in the proofs of this section.

### The normal form

In the ramified type, a change of the variable $\theta$ and of one parameter brings the equation to a normal form. The idea is to divide $\theta - h$ by $g$, then take a power of the result to make the exponent of one parameter congruent to 1 modulo $p$, and divide by the $p$-th powers that remain.

**Lemma 4.3.7 (the normal form).** Let $R$ be a regular local ring of dimension $d$ with residue field $\kappa$ and fraction field $E$ of characteristic $p$. Let $E'/E$ be an extension of degree $p$, let $\theta \in E' \setminus E$, and assume that $f = \theta^p$ lies in $R$ and is monomial modulo $p$-th powers in $R$, with $h$, $g$, $u$, $x_1, \dots, x_d$ and $a_1, \dots, a_d$ as in Definition 4.3.4.

1. If $f$ is of the residual type, let $z = (\theta - h)/g$. Then $z^p = u$, $z \notin E$ and $E' = E(z)$.
2. If $f$ is of the ramified type, then $d \ge 1$, and there are a regular system of parameters $y_1, \dots, y_d$ of $R$, integers $0 \le r_i < p$ for $2 \le i \le d$ and an element $z \in E'$ with
$$z^p = y_1\, y_2^{r_2} \cdots y_d^{r_d}, \qquad z \notin E, \qquad E' = E(z).$$

*Proof.* Part 1. In characteristic $p$ we have $(\theta - h)^p = \theta^p - h^p = g^pu$, so $z^p = u$. If $z \in E$, then $\theta = h + gz \in E$, which is false. Since $[E' : E] = p$ is prime and $E \subsetneq E(z) \subseteq E'$, we get $E(z) = E'$.

Part 2. Some $a_i$ is not divisible by $p$, so $d \ge 1$. After we change the order of the parameters, $p \nmid a_1$. Let $z_0 = (\theta - h)/g$. As in part 1, $z_0^p = u x_1^{a_1} \cdots x_d^{a_d}$. Since $a_1$ is invertible modulo $p$, there is an integer $b$ with $1 \le b \le p - 1$ and $a_1b \equiv 1$ modulo $p$. Write $a_1b = 1 + pm$ with $m \ge 0$, and for $2 \le i \le d$ write $a_ib = pq_i + r_i$ with $0 \le r_i < p$. Let
$$z = \frac{z_0^b}{x_1^m x_2^{q_2} \cdots x_d^{q_d}}.$$
Then
$$z^p = \frac{u^b x_1^{a_1b} x_2^{a_2b} \cdots x_d^{a_db}}{x_1^{pm} x_2^{pq_2} \cdots x_d^{pq_d}} = u^b x_1\, x_2^{r_2} \cdots x_d^{r_d}.$$
Let $y_1 = u^bx_1$ and $y_i = x_i$ for $i \ge 2$. Since $u$ is a unit, $y_1, \dots, y_d$ generate the same ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ as $x_1, \dots, x_d$. So they are a regular system of parameters, and $z^p = y_1y_2^{r_2} \cdots y_d^{r_d}$.

The order along $y_1$. We show that $z \notin E$, with a discrete valuation that measures the order along the divisor $y_1 = 0$. The class of $y_1$ in $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ is not 0, so $y_1 \notin \mathfrak{m}^2$. By Volume 1, Proposition 1.2.6, the ring $R/(y_1)$ is a regular local ring, so it is a domain (Volume 1, Theorem 1.3.3). Thus $y_1$ is a prime element of the domain $R$. Let $R_1$ be the localization of $R$ at the prime ideal $(y_1)$. It is a Noetherian local domain with fraction field $E$, and its maximal ideal is generated by $y_1 \ne 0$. So $R_1$ is not a field, and $\dim R_1 \le 1$ by Volume 1, Corollary 1.1.3. Thus $\dim R_1 = 1$, and $R_1$ is a discrete valuation ring by Volume 1, Theorem 1.4.2. Let $\operatorname{ord}_1$ be its valuation. Then $\operatorname{ord}_1(y_1) = 1$, and $\operatorname{ord}_1(c) = 0$ for each unit $c$ of $R$. For $i \ge 2$ we have $y_i \notin (y_1)$. Indeed, if $y_i = cy_1$ with $c \in R$, then $\mathfrak{m}$ is generated by the $d - 1$ elements $y_l$ with $l \ne i$, and this contradicts $\dim R = d$ (Volume 1, Corollary 1.1.3). So $y_i$ is a unit of $R_1$, and $\operatorname{ord}_1(y_i) = 0$. Hence $\operatorname{ord}_1(z^p) = 1$. If $z \in E$, then $\operatorname{ord}_1(z^p) = p\operatorname{ord}_1(z)$ is divisible by $p$. This is false, so $z \notin E$. As in part 1, $E(z) = E'$. $\square$

The same argument works for each parameter. For a regular system of parameters $x_1, \dots, x_d$ and each $i$, the localization of $R$ at the prime $(x_i)$ is a discrete valuation ring with fraction field $E$. Its valuation $\operatorname{ord}_i$, the *order along $x_i = 0$*, has $\operatorname{ord}_i(x_i) = 1$, $\operatorname{ord}_i(x_l) = 0$ for $l \ne i$, and $\operatorname{ord}_i(c) = 0$ for each unit $c$ of $R$.

*Remark (an element of the monomial form is not a $p$-th power).* Let $R$ be a regular local ring with fraction field $E$ of characteristic $p$, and let $f \in R$ be monomial modulo $p$-th powers in $R$. Then $f$ is not a $p$-th power in $E$. So the hypothesis of Definition 4.3.15 below, that $f$ is not a $p$-th power, is necessary. Here is the proof. Assume that $f = c^p$ with $c \in E$. Then $(c - h)^p = g^pux^a$, so $ux^a = e^p$ with $e = (c - h)/g \in E$, and $e \ne 0$. In the ramified type, choose $i$ with $p \nmid a_i$. Then $\operatorname{ord}_i(ux^a) = a_i$, while $\operatorname{ord}_i(e^p)$ is divisible by $p$. In the residual type, $u = e^p$ lies in $R$, so $e \in R$ by Volume 1, Corollary 1.3.13. Then $\bar{u} = \bar{e}^p$ in $\kappa$, which is false. The reduction does not use this remark.

### The end cases

Two kinds of equations give a regular ring at once: an equation whose reduction to the residue field is irreducible, and the extraction of a root of a parameter. They are the end cases of the step. We first prove the algebra, and then we add the valuation.

**Theorem 4.3.8 (an irreducible reduction).** Let $(R, \mathfrak{m}, \kappa)$ be a regular local ring of dimension $d$. Let $P \in R[X]$ be a monic polynomial of degree $n \ge 1$ such that its image $\bar{P} \in \kappa[X]$ is irreducible. Then $B = R[X]/(P)$ is a regular local ring of dimension $d$, and its maximal ideal is $\mathfrak{m}B$.

*Proof.* Each element of $R[X]$ is $QP + F$ with $\deg F < n$, in a unique way, because $P$ is monic. So $B$ is a free $R$-module with the basis $1, X, \dots, X^{n-1}$. In particular the map $R \to B$ is injective, and $B$ is integral over $R$, because it is generated by the class of $X$, which is a root of $P$. The ring $B$ is Noetherian.

Let $\mathfrak{n}$ be a maximal ideal of $B$. Since $B$ is integral over $R$, the ideal $\mathfrak{n} \cap R$ is maximal [AM69, Chapter 5], so it is $\mathfrak{m}$, and $\mathfrak{n} \supseteq \mathfrak{m}B$. Now $B/\mathfrak{m}B = \kappa[X]/(\bar{P})$, and this is a field because $\bar{P}$ is irreducible. So $\mathfrak{m}B$ is a maximal ideal, and each maximal ideal contains it. Thus $B$ is local with the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}B$. This ideal is generated by the $d$ elements of a regular system of parameters of $R$. So $\dim B \le d$ by Volume 1, Corollary 1.1.3. Conversely, let $\mathfrak{p}_0 \subsetneq \dots \subsetneq \mathfrak{p}_d$ be a chain of primes of $R$. By lying over and going up [AM69, Chapter 5] there is a chain $\mathfrak{q}_0 \subseteq \dots \subseteq \mathfrak{q}_d$ of primes of $B$ with $\mathfrak{q}_i \cap R = \mathfrak{p}_i$, and the inclusions are strict because the $\mathfrak{p}_i$ are distinct. So $\dim B \ge d$. Thus $\dim B = d$, and the maximal ideal has $d$ generators, so $B$ is regular (Volume 1, Definition 1.2.1). $\square$

**Corollary 4.3.9 (a residue that is not a $p$-th power).** Let $(R, \mathfrak{m}, \kappa)$ be a regular local ring of dimension $d$ whose residue field has characteristic $p$. Let $u \in R$ be a unit whose class $\bar{u}$ in $\kappa$ is not a $p$-th power. Then $R[X]/(X^p - u)$ is a regular local ring of dimension $d$, with maximal ideal generated by $\mathfrak{m}$.

Now we add the valuation. We fix the setting for the rest of the section.

*The setting.* Let $k$ be a field of characteristic $p > 0$, let $E/k$ be a function field, and let $E'/E$ be a purely inseparable extension of degree $p$. Let $w$ be a valuation of $E'$, and let $v$ be its restriction to $E$, so that $\mathcal{O}_v = \mathcal{O}_w \cap E$ and $\mathfrak{m}_v = \mathfrak{m}_w \cap E$. We write $\Gamma_w$ for the value group of $w$. Let $A$ be an affine model of $E/k$ with $A \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$, let $\mathfrak{p} = \mathfrak{m}_v \cap A$ be the center of $v$, and assume that $R = A_\mathfrak{p}$ is regular, with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m} = \mathfrak{p}R$, residue field $\kappa$ and dimension $d$. The elements of $A \setminus \mathfrak{p}$ are units of $\mathcal{O}_v$, so $R \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v \subseteq \mathcal{O}_w$. The ideal $\mathfrak{m}_w \cap R$ of $R$ is proper and contains $\mathfrak{m}$, so $\mathfrak{m}_w \cap R = \mathfrak{m}$. In particular $w(x) > 0$ for each $x \in \mathfrak{m}$.

We use one more fact in this setting, a form of Volume 5, Lemma 1.1.1 for two fields. Lemma 1.1.1 itself compares two models of the same field, so we prove the form that we need. Let $B$ be a $k$-subalgebra of $E'$ with $A \subseteq B \subseteq \mathcal{O}_w$, let $\Sigma = A \setminus \mathfrak{p}$, and let $T = \Sigma^{-1}B$. Then $T \subseteq \mathcal{O}_w$, because the elements of $\Sigma$ are units of $\mathcal{O}_w$. Let $\mathfrak{q} = \mathfrak{m}_w \cap B$ and $Q = \mathfrak{m}_w \cap T$. Then $\Sigma \cap \mathfrak{q} = \emptyset$, and an element $b/s$ of $T$, with $b \in B$ and $s \in \Sigma$, lies in $\mathfrak{m}_w$ if and only if $b \in \mathfrak{q}$. So $Q = \Sigma^{-1}\mathfrak{q}$, and
$$B_\mathfrak{q} = T_Q. \tag{L}$$
In words: the local ring of $B$ at the center of $w$ is the local ring of $T$ at the center of $w$. Also $R[t_1, \dots, t_m] = \Sigma^{-1}A[t_1, \dots, t_m]$ for elements $t_1, \dots, t_m$ of $\mathcal{O}_w$.

### The toric step

Now we treat the ramified type in general. By Lemma 4.3.7 we have $z^p = x_1x_2^{r_2} \cdots x_d^{r_d}$ with $0 \le r_i < p$. If all $r_i$ are 0, Proposition 4.3.11, case 2, applies. In general the ring $R[z]$ is not regular. For example, $R[z] \cong R[X]/(X^p - x_1x_2)$ for $p = 2$ and $d = 2$ has the maximal ideal $(z, x_1, x_2)$, and $z^2 - x_1x_2$ lies in the square of this ideal, so it is singular. So we must change the variables. The relation $z^p = x_1 x_2^{r_2} \cdots x_d^{r_d}$ is a relation between monomials. So we work in the group of Laurent monomials in $z, x_2, \dots, x_d$, and we choose a new basis of this group with the lattice lemma of Section 2.1, as in the proof of Theorem 2.1.5. The new basis gives a chart $T = R[U_1, \dots, U_d]$. The ring $T$ is defined by binomial equations over $R$, and the main work of the proof is to show that its local ring at the center of $w$ is regular. We do not compute the dimension of this local ring, and we do not need it.

**Lemma 4.3.12 (the toric step).** In the setting, assume that $d \ge 1$, let $x_1, \dots, x_d$ be a regular system of parameters of $R$, and let $z \in E' \setminus E$ with
$$z^p = x_1\, x_2^{r_2} \cdots x_d^{r_d}, \qquad 0 \le r_i < p.$$
Then there are elements $U_1, \dots, U_d \in \mathcal{O}_w$, each a Laurent monomial in $z, x_2, \dots, x_d$, with these properties. Each of $z, x_1, \dots, x_d$ is a monomial in $U_1, \dots, U_d$ with exponents in $\mathbb{N}$. The ring $T = R[U_1, \dots, U_d]$ has a regular local ring $T_Q$ at the prime $Q = \mathfrak{m}_w \cap T$. The ring $A[U_1, \dots, U_d]$ is an affine model of $E'/k$ with $A \subseteq A[U_1, \dots, U_d] \subseteq \mathcal{O}_w$, and its local ring at the center of $w$ is $T_Q$.

The proof has nine steps. Steps 1 to 3 choose the monomials $U_j$ with the lattice lemma. Steps 4 to 6 write $T$ as a quotient of a polynomial ring over $R$ by $d$ binomials, and show that the local ring of this quotient is regular: the binomials are part of a regular system of parameters of the local ring of the polynomial ring. Steps 7 and 8 show that the quotient has the same local ring as $T$, by a comparison of the fields of fractions. Step 9 passes to the affine model.

*Proof.* For $c \in \mathbb{N}^d$ we write $U^c = U_1^{c_1} \cdots U_d^{c_d}$, and in the same way $Y^c$ for variables $Y_1, \dots, Y_d$.

Step 1 (the lattice). Let $M$ be the free abelian group with the basis $e_z, e_2, \dots, e_d$. The elements $z, x_2, \dots, x_d$ are not 0, so there is a group homomorphism $\chi \colon M \to (E')^\times$ with $\chi(e_z) = z$ and $\chi(e_i) = x_i$ for $i \ge 2$. Let
$$e_1 = pe_z - \sum_{i \ge 2} r_ie_i.$$
Then $\chi(e_1) = z^p/(x_2^{r_2} \cdots x_d^{r_d}) = x_1$. So $\chi(e_i) = x_i$ for $1 \le i \le d$. Let $\ell = w \circ \chi \colon M \to \Gamma_w$. It is a group homomorphism. Let $F = \{e_z, e_1, e_2, \dots, e_d\}$. We have $\ell(e_i) = w(x_i) > 0$, because $x_i \in \mathfrak{m}$. Also $p\,\ell(e_z) = w(z^p) > 0$, and $\Gamma_w$ is torsion free, so $\ell(e_z) > 0$. Thus $\ell > 0$ on $F$.

Step 2 (an order on $M$). The lattice lemma needs an order on $M$, and $\ell$ need not be injective. So we break the ties with a second order. Fix a total order $\succ$ of $M$ that is compatible with the addition, for example the lexicographic order of the coordinates in the basis $e_z, e_2, \dots, e_d$. Let $P$ be the set of $a \in M$ with $\ell(a) > 0$, or with $\ell(a) = 0$ and $a \succ 0$. The set $P$ is closed under addition. Indeed, let $a, b \in P$. Then $\ell(a) \ge 0$ and $\ell(b) \ge 0$. If one of them is positive, then $\ell(a + b) > 0$. If both are 0, then $\ell(a + b) = 0$ and $a + b \succ 0$. Also, for each $a \ne 0$ exactly one of $a$ and $-a$ lies in $P$. If $\ell(a) \ne 0$, then exactly one of $\ell(a)$ and $\ell(-a)$ is positive. If $\ell(a) = 0$, then exactly one of $a \succ 0$ and $-a \succ 0$ holds. And $0 \notin P$. So the rule $a < b$ if and only if $b - a \in P$ makes $M$ an ordered group, with $P$ as the set of positive elements. Each element of $F$ is positive. If $a > 0$, then $\ell(a) \ge 0$.

Step 3 (the lattice lemma). By Volume 5, Lemma 2.1.4, applied to the ordered group $M \cong \mathbb{Z}^d$ and the finite set $F$, there is a basis $u_1, \dots, u_d$ of $M$ such that each $u_j$ is positive and each element of $F$ is a combination of $u_1, \dots, u_d$ with coefficients in $\mathbb{N}$. Write
$$e_z = \sum_j c_{z,j}u_j, \qquad e_i = \sum_j c_{i,j}u_j \quad (1 \le i \le d),$$
with all $c_{z,j}, c_{i,j} \in \mathbb{N}$, and let $c_z = (c_{z,j})_j$ and $c_i = (c_{i,j})_j$. Let $U_j = \chi(u_j)$. It is a Laurent monomial in $z, x_2, \dots, x_d$. We have $w(U_j) = \ell(u_j) \ge 0$, so $U_j \in \mathcal{O}_w$. We apply $\chi$ and get
$$z = U^{c_z}, \qquad x_i = U^{c_i} \quad (1 \le i \le d).$$
The rest of the proof uses only these properties of $u_1, \dots, u_d$: they form a basis of $M$, $\ell(u_j) \ge 0$, and the elements of $F$ are combinations of them with coefficients in $\mathbb{N}$.

Step 4 (the rings). Let $T = R[U_1, \dots, U_d]$. Then $T \subseteq \mathcal{O}_w$, and $z \in T$. Let $Q = \mathfrak{m}_w \cap T$. It is a prime ideal, and $Q \cap R = \mathfrak{m}_w \cap R = \mathfrak{m}$. Let $P_Y = R[Y_1, \dots, Y_d]$ be the polynomial ring, and let $\pi \colon P_Y \to T$ be the surjective homomorphism of $R$-algebras with $\pi(Y_j) = U_j$. Let $Q_Y = \pi^{-1}(Q)$, a prime ideal of $P_Y$, and let $B = (P_Y)_{Q_Y}$, a Noetherian local ring with the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{n} = Q_YB$ and residue field $\kappa_B$. For $1 \le i \le d$ let
$$g_i = x_i - Y^{c_i} \in P_Y.$$
Then $\pi(g_i) = x_i - U^{c_i} = 0$. Let $C = P_Y/(g_1, \dots, g_d)$, let $\bar{\pi} \colon C \to T$ be the induced surjective map, and let $Q_C = \bar{\pi}^{-1}(Q)$. Since localization commutes with quotients, $C_{Q_C} = B/(g_1, \dots, g_d)B$. The induced map $C_{Q_C} \to T_Q$ is surjective. Indeed, an element of $T_Q$ is $t/s$ with $t \in T$ and $s \in T \setminus Q$, and lifts of $t$ and $s$ to $C$ give an element of $C_{Q_C}$ that maps to $t/s$. We show that $C_{Q_C}$ is a regular local ring and that the map $C_{Q_C} \to T_Q$ is injective.

Step 5 ($B$ is regular). Let $J$ be the set of the indices $j$ with $w(U_j) > 0$. Then $Y_j \in Q_Y$ if and only if $U_j \in Q$, that is, if and only if $j \in J$. The ideal $Q_Y$ contains $\mathfrak{m}P_Y$, because $\pi(\mathfrak{m}P_Y) = \mathfrak{m}T \subseteq Q$. We show that $x_1, \dots, x_d$, followed by the $Y_j$ with $j \in J$ in some order, is a regular sequence in $\mathfrak{n}$. For $1 \le i \le d$, the ring $R_i = R/(x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})$ is a regular local ring by Volume 1, Corollary 1.2.7, so it is a domain. The ring $B/(x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})B$ is the localization of the polynomial ring $R_i[Y_1, \dots, Y_d]$ at the image of $Q_Y$, so it is a domain. The class of $x_i$ in $R_i$ is not 0: otherwise $\mathfrak{m}$ is generated by $d - 1$ elements, which contradicts $\dim R = d$. So $x_i$ is a nonzerodivisor on $B/(x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})B$. Next, $B/(x_1, \dots, x_d)B$ is the localization of $\kappa[Y_1, \dots, Y_d] = P_Y/\mathfrak{m}P_Y$ at the image of $Q_Y$. After we divide by some of the $Y_j$ with $j \in J$, we get the localization of a polynomial ring over $\kappa$ in the remaining variables at a prime ideal, which is a domain, and the next $Y_j$ is not 0 in it. So the sequence is regular. The last quotient
$$\bar{B} = B/(x_1, \dots, x_d, Y_j : j \in J)B$$
is the localization of the polynomial ring $\kappa[Y_j : j \notin J]$ at a prime ideal. A polynomial ring over a field is a regular ring [Mat, Section 19], so $\bar{B}$ is a regular local ring. Let $e = \dim \bar{B}$. Now we apply Volume 1, Proposition 1.2.8, once for each element of the regular sequence, from the last one to the first one. It shows that $B$ is a regular local ring of dimension $d + |J| + e$. The ideal $\mathfrak{n}$ is generated by $x_1, \dots, x_d$, the $Y_j$ with $j \in J$, and $e$ lifts of generators of the maximal ideal of $\bar{B}$. These are $\dim B$ elements, so they form a regular system of parameters of $B$. In particular, the classes of $x_1, \dots, x_d$ and of the $Y_j$ with $j \in J$ in $\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{n}^2$ are linearly independent over $\kappa_B$.

Step 6 (the binomials are parameters). Let $1 \le i \le d$. Since $\pi(Y^{c_i}) = x_i \in Q$, we have $Y^{c_i} \in Q_Y$. Let $\delta_i = \sum_{j \in J} c_{i,j}$. If $\delta_i = 0$, then $Y^{c_i}$ is a product of variables $Y_j$ with $j \notin J$. These are not in the prime ideal $Q_Y$, so their product is not in $Q_Y$, which is false. So $\delta_i \ge 1$. The $Y_j$ with $j \notin J$ are units of $B$. If $\delta_i \ge 2$, then $Y^{c_i}$ is a unit times a product of at least two elements of $\mathfrak{n}$, so $Y^{c_i} \in \mathfrak{n}^2$. If $\delta_i = 1$, then $Y^{c_i} = \varepsilon_iY_{j(i)}$ with a unit $\varepsilon_i$ of $B$ and an index $j(i) \in J$. So the class of $g_i$ in $\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{n}^2$ is $[x_i]$ or $[x_i] - \bar{\varepsilon}_i[Y_{j(i)}]$. Assume that $\sum_i \lambda_i[g_i] = 0$ with $\lambda_i \in \kappa_B$. In the independent classes of Step 5, the coefficient of $[x_i]$ in this sum is $\lambda_i$. So all $\lambda_i$ are 0, and the classes of $g_1, \dots, g_d$ are linearly independent. We extend them to a basis of $\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{n}^2$ and lift the basis. By Volume 1, Lemma 1.1.1, we get a set of $\dim B$ generators of $\mathfrak{n}$ that begins with $g_1, \dots, g_d$. By Volume 1, Corollary 1.2.7, the ring $C_{Q_C} = B/(g_1, \dots, g_d)B$ is a regular local ring. In particular it is a domain.

Step 7 (the generic fiber). We show that $C \otimes_R E \cong E'$, by $Y_j \mapsto U_j$. We have $C \otimes_R E = E[Y_1, \dots, Y_d]/(g_1, \dots, g_d)$. First, each $Y_j$ is a unit in this ring. Let $M_1 \subseteq M$ be the subgroup generated by $e_1, \dots, e_d$. Since $pe_z = e_1 + \sum_{i \ge 2} r_ie_i$, we have $pM \subseteq M_1$, so $pu_j \in M_1$. If $c_{i,j} = 0$ for all $i$, then the $j$-th coordinate in the basis $u_1, \dots, u_d$ of each element of $M_1$ is 0, but the $j$-th coordinate of $pu_j$ is $p$. So $c_{i,j} \ge 1$ for some $i$. In $C \otimes_R E$ we have $Y^{c_i} = x_i$, which is a unit, so the factor $Y_j$ of $Y^{c_i}$ is a unit. Thus
$$C \otimes_R E = E[Y_1^{\pm 1}, \dots, Y_d^{\pm 1}]/(Y^{c_1} - x_1, \dots, Y^{c_d} - x_d).$$
The ring of Laurent polynomials is the group algebra $E[M]$, where $Y^a$ corresponds to $\sum_j a_ju_j$. In the basis $e_z, e_2, \dots, e_d$ the same group algebra is $E[Z^{\pm 1}, X_2^{\pm 1}, \dots, X_d^{\pm 1}]$, where $Z$ corresponds to $e_z$ and $X_i$ to $e_i$. The monomial $Y^{c_i}$ corresponds to $e_i$. So the relations become $X_i = x_i$ for $i \ge 2$ and $Z^pX_2^{-r_2} \cdots X_d^{-r_d} = x_1$. After we use the first relations, the last one is $Z^p = x_1x_2^{r_2} \cdots x_d^{r_d} = z^p$. Thus
$$C \otimes_R E \cong E[Z^{\pm 1}]/(Z^p - z^p).$$
The element $z^p$ is not a $p$-th power in $E$, by Lemma 4.3.3, part 3. So $Z^p - z^p$ is irreducible over $E$, and $E[Z]/(Z^p - z^p)$ is a field of degree $p$ over $E$. In this field $Z$ is a unit, so the field is also $E[Z^{\pm 1}]/(Z^p - z^p)$. The homomorphism $E[M] \to E'$ that maps each $a \in M$ to $\chi(a)$ kills the relations, since $\chi(e_i) = x_i$. It maps $Z$ to $z$ and $Y_j$ to $\chi(u_j) = U_j$. It induces a homomorphism of fields from $C \otimes_R E$ to $E'$ over $E$. Both have degree $p$ over $E$, so it is an isomorphism.

Step 8 (injectivity). The composite $R \to C_{Q_C} \to T_Q \subseteq E'$ is the inclusion of $R$ in $E'$, so $R \to C_{Q_C}$ is injective. Since $C_{Q_C}$ is a domain, each nonzero element of $R$ is a nonzerodivisor on $C_{Q_C}$, so the map $C_{Q_C} \to C_{Q_C} \otimes_R E$ is injective. The ring $C_{Q_C} \otimes_R E$ is a localization of $C \otimes_R E$, which is a field by Step 7, and it is not 0, because it contains $C_{Q_C}$. A localization of a field that is not 0 is the field itself. So we get an injective map $C_{Q_C} \to C \otimes_R E \cong E'$. It is a homomorphism of $R$-algebras that maps the class of $Y_j$ to $U_j$. The composite $C_{Q_C} \to T_Q \subseteq E'$ has the same properties. Two homomorphisms from the localization $C_{Q_C}$ that agree on $C$ are equal, so the two maps are equal. Thus $C_{Q_C} \to T_Q$ is injective. With Step 4, it is an isomorphism, and $T_Q$ is a regular local ring.

Step 9 (the model). Let $A'' = A[U_1, \dots, U_d]$. It is a finitely generated $k$-algebra with $A \subseteq A'' \subseteq \mathcal{O}_w$. Its fraction field contains $E$ and $z = U^{c_z}$, so it is $E(z) = E'$. So $A''$ is an affine model of $E'/k$ that contains $A$. We have $T = \Sigma^{-1}A''$, so by (L) the local ring of $A''$ at the center of $w$ is $T_Q$. $\square$

The proof uses the valuation only at two points: in Step 3, to choose the basis with $w(U_j) \ge 0$, and in the definition of $Q$. The regularity of $T_Q$ comes from the algebra of Steps 5 and 6. This is the same mechanism as in Theorem 2.1.5, where the chart $k[u_1, \dots, u_r]$ of the lattice lemma is a polynomial ring. Here the chart is not a polynomial ring over $R$, but its local ring is cut out by binomials that are part of a regular system of parameters.

**Example 4.3.13 (a toric step for $p = 3$).** In the setting, let $p = 3$ and $d = 2$, and let $z^3 = x_1x_2$. So $r_2 = 1$. Assume that $w(x_1) = 1$ and $w(x_2) = 2$, in an ordered group that contains $\mathbb{Z}$. Then $3w(z) = 3$, so $w(z) = 1$. The ring $R[z]$ is not regular: its maximal ideal is $(z, x_1, x_2)$, and the relation $z^3 - x_1x_2$ lies in the square of this ideal. We follow the proof of Lemma 4.3.12.

The lattice is $M = \mathbb{Z}e_z \oplus \mathbb{Z}e_2$, with $\ell(e_z) = 1$ and $\ell(e_2) = 2$. The element $e_1 = 3e_z - e_2$ has $\ell(e_1) = 1$. Let $u_1 = 3e_z - e_2$ and $u_2 = e_2 - 2e_z$. The matrix of their coordinates in the basis $e_z, e_2$ has the rows $(3, -1)$ and $(-2, 1)$, and its determinant is $3 - 2 = 1$. So $u_1, u_2$ is a basis of $M$. We have $\ell(u_1) = 1$ and $\ell(u_2) = 0$, and
$$e_z = u_1 + u_2, \qquad e_1 = u_1, \qquad e_2 = 2u_1 + 3u_2.$$
So the basis has the properties that the end of Step 3 lists. (The basis is positive for the order of Step 2 if the tie-break order $\succ$ is the lexicographic order in the basis $e_2, e_z$, because $u_2$ has the coordinate $1$ at $e_2$. For the lexicographic order in the basis $e_z, e_2$ the element $u_2$ is negative. The rest of the proof needs only the properties of Step 3.) Then
$$U_1 = \chi(u_1) = x_1, \qquad U_2 = \chi(u_2) = \frac{x_2}{z^2},$$
and indeed $z = U_1U_2$ and $x_2 = U_1^2U_2^3$, since $U_1^2U_2^3 = x_1^2x_2^3/z^6 = x_2$. So $T = R[U_1, U_2] = R[U_2]$, with $w(U_2) = 0$, so $U_2$ is a unit of $\mathcal{O}_w$, and $J = \{1\}$.

The ring $C$ is $R[Y_1, Y_2]/(x_1 - Y_1, x_2 - Y_1^2Y_2^3) \cong R[Y_2]/(x_2 - x_1^2Y_2^3)$, and $C/x_1C = \kappa[Y_2]$. The ideal $Q_C$ is the kernel of the map from $C$ to the residue field of $w$, and it contains $x_1$. So $Q_C/x_1C$ is the kernel of the map $\kappa[Y_2] \to \mathcal{O}_w/\mathfrak{m}_w$ that maps $Y_2$ to the class $\bar{U}_2$ of $U_2$. There are two cases. If $\bar{U}_2$ is transcendental over $\kappa$, this kernel is 0. Then $T_Q/x_1T_Q \cong C_{Q_C}/x_1C_{Q_C} = \kappa(Y_2)$ is a field, so the maximal ideal of $T_Q$ is generated by $x_1$. The ring $T_Q$ is regular by Lemma 4.3.12, and it has dimension 1, so it is a discrete valuation ring. If $\bar{U}_2$ is algebraic over $\kappa$ with the minimal polynomial $\mu$, the kernel is $(\mu)$, and $T_Q/x_1T_Q$ is the discrete valuation ring $\kappa[Y_2]_{(\mu)}$. Since $T_Q$ is a domain and $x_1 \ne 0$, Volume 1, Proposition 1.2.8, shows that $T_Q$ has dimension 2. Its maximal ideal is generated by $x_1$ and $P(U_2)$, where $P \in R[Y_2]$ is a monic lift of $\mu$.

Here is a case where all the hypotheses hold. Let $k$ be a field of characteristic 3, and let $E' = k(z, s)$ be a field of rational functions in two variables. Let $x_2 = s$, $x_1 = z^3/s$, $E = k(x_1, x_2)$ and $A = k[x_1, x_2]$. Then $E = k(z^3, s)$, and $[E' : E] = 3$, with $z^3 = x_1x_2 \in E$, so $E'/E$ is purely inseparable of degree 3. Let $w$ be the monomial valuation of $k(z, s)$ with $w(z) = 1$ and $w(s) = 2$ (Volume 3, Lemma 1.6.1). Then $w(x_1) = 1$ and $w(x_2) = 2$, the center of $v$ on $A$ is $(x_1, x_2)$, and $R = k[x_1, x_2]_{(x_1, x_2)}$ is regular of dimension 2 with $\kappa = k$. The class of $U_2 = s/z^2$ is transcendental over $k$. Indeed, let $P = \sum_{b \le D} c_bX^b$ be a monic polynomial over $k$ of degree $D$. Then $z^{2D}P(U_2) = \sum_b c_b s^bz^{2(D - b)}$ is a sum of distinct monomials, all of value $2D$, so $w(P(U_2)) = 0$. So we are in the first case, and $T_Q$ is a discrete valuation ring with the parameter $x_1$.

### The theorems

Now we put the pieces together. First, the monomial form gives the step.

**Theorem 4.3.14 (the monomial step).** In the setting, with $k$ any field of characteristic $p > 0$, let $\theta \in E' \setminus E$ with $f = \theta^p \in R$, and assume that $f$ is monomial modulo $p$-th powers in $R$. Then $w$ has a regular center on an affine model of $E'/k$ that contains $A$.

Lean: the statement is the hypothesis `Singularity.MonomialInseparableStep` of the library. Two cases are proved: `Singularity.hasRegularCenter_of_eq_add_unit` (the residual type) and `Singularity.hasRegularCenter_of_eq_add_parameter` (the ramified type where the monomial is one parameter with exponent 1). The general ramified type has no Lean proof yet.

*Proof.* If $f$ is of the residual type, Lemma 4.3.7, part 1, gives $z \in E' \setminus E$ with $z^p = u$, a unit of $R$ whose class in $\kappa$ is not a $p$-th power. Proposition 4.3.11, case 1, gives the claim. If $f$ is of the ramified type, Lemma 4.3.7, part 2, gives $d \ge 1$, a regular system of parameters $y_1, \dots, y_d$ of $R$, and $z \in E' \setminus E$ with $z^p = y_1y_2^{r_2} \cdots y_d^{r_d}$ and $0 \le r_i < p$. Lemma 4.3.12 gives an affine model $A[U_1, \dots, U_d]$ of $E'/k$ that contains $A$, lies in $\mathcal{O}_w$, and has a regular local ring at the center of $w$. When all $r_i$ are 0, Proposition 4.3.11, case 2, gives the same conclusion directly. $\square$

The missing piece is a statement that brings $f$ to the monomial form. We state it as a hypothesis.

**Definition 4.3.15 (weak log monomialization).** Let $k$ be a field of characteristic $p > 0$, and let $n \ge 0$. *Weak log monomialization along a valuation holds in transcendence degree $n$ over $k$*, and we write $\mathrm{WLM}(n, k)$, if the following is true. Let $E$ be a field extension of $k$ with $\operatorname{trdeg}_k E = n$, let $v$ be a valuation of $E$, and let $A$ be an affine model of $E/k$ with $A \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$ whose local ring at the center of $v$ is regular. Let $f \in A$ be an element that is not a $p$-th power in $E$. Then there is an affine model $A'$ of $E/k$ with $A \subseteq A' \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$ whose local ring $R'$ at the center of $v$ is regular, such that $f$ is monomial modulo $p$-th powers in $R'$.

Lean: `Singularity.WeakLogMonomialization`

The word *weak* means that the statement asks for less than a monomialization of $f$: it allows a $p$-th power $h^p$ and a factor $g^p$, it asks for a monomial form only in one local ring, along one valuation, and it allows the residual type. The word *log* refers to the monomial $x_1^{a_1} \cdots x_d^{a_d}$ in a regular system of parameters, which is a normal crossings divisor at the center. We do not prove $\mathrm{WLM}(n, k)$. It is a hypothesis, and it is open for $n = 4$.

**Theorem 4.3.16 (the step from weak log monomialization).** Let $k$ be a field of characteristic $p > 0$, let $n \ge 0$, and assume $\mathrm{WLM}(n, k)$. Then the step of degree $p$ holds in transcendence degree $n$ over $k$.

Lean: `Singularity.regularCenterInseparableStep_of_weakLogMonomialization` (the Lean theorem takes the monomial step `Singularity.MonomialInseparableStep` as a second hypothesis)

*Proof.* Let $E$, $E'$, $w$, $v$ and $S$ be as in Definition 4.3.1, and assume that $v$ has a regular center on an affine model $A_0$ of $E/k$ that contains $S$. Then $E/k$ is a function field, and $S \subseteq A_0 \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$. By Lemma 4.3.3 there is $\theta \in E' \setminus E$ with $f = \theta^p \in A_0$, and $f$ is not a $p$-th power in $E$. By $\mathrm{WLM}(n, k)$ there is an affine model $A'$ of $E/k$ with $A_0 \subseteq A' \subseteq \mathcal{O}_v$ whose local ring $R'$ at the center of $v$ is regular, such that $f$ is monomial modulo $p$-th powers in $R'$. Also $f \in A_0 \subseteq R'$. Theorem 4.3.14, applied to $A'$ and $\theta$, gives a regular center of $w$ on an affine model of $E'/k$ that contains $A'$, so it contains $S$. $\square$

**Corollary 4.3.17.** Let $k$ be a perfect field of characteristic $p > 0$, let $n \ge 0$, and assume $\mathrm{WLM}(n, k)$. Then for each function field $K/k$ of transcendence degree $n$, each valuation of $K/k$ has local uniformization.

Lean: `Singularity.hasLocalUniformization_of_weakLogMonomialization` (the Lean theorem takes the theorem of Temkin `Singularity.localUniformization_inseparable` and the monomial step `Singularity.MonomialInseparableStep` as hypotheses)

*Proof.* By Theorem 4.3.16 the step of degree $p$ holds in transcendence degree $n$ over $k$. Theorem 4.3.2 gives the claim. $\square$

The proof of Corollary 4.3.17 uses the theorem of Temkin, through Theorem 4.3.2.

### What is known and what is open

We state the status of each part of this section in plain words.

- The theorem of Temkin (Theorem 4.1.1) is a theorem of another author [Tem13]. The handbook does not prove it. The Lean library has it as a hypothesis.
- Theorems 4.3.2, 4.3.14 and 4.3.16 and Corollary 4.3.17 are proved in this section, with full proofs, from the theorem of Temkin. Corollary 4.3.17 is conditional: it assumes $\mathrm{WLM}(n, k)$. In Lean, Theorem 4.3.2 is proved from the theorem of Temkin and the step of degree $p$, and Theorem 4.3.16 is proved from $\mathrm{WLM}(n, k)$ and the monomial step. The monomial step, Theorem 4.3.14, is a hypothesis in Lean. The Lean library proves the end cases (Theorems 4.3.8 and 4.3.10, Corollary 4.3.9 and Proposition 4.3.11), and with them the monomial step for the residual type and for the ramified type with one parameter of exponent 1. The normal form (Lemma 4.3.7, part 2) and the toric step (Lemma 4.3.12) are not in Lean.
- Weak log monomialization $\mathrm{WLM}(n, k)$ is a hypothesis. This section does not prove it for any $n \ge 1$ in general. For $n \le 3$ local uniformization is a theorem: for $n \le 2$ by Volume 3, Proposition 4.1.7, and Volume 5, Proposition 3.1.2, and for $n = 3$ by the work of Cossart and Piltant [CP08, CP09] (Volume 3, Theorem 4.1.5, cited). Abhyankar proved the cases $n = 2$ and, for $p > 5$ over an algebraically closed field, $n = 3$ [Abh56s, Abh66]. These results give the step of degree $p$ for $n \le 3$, as we said after Definition 4.3.1. They do not give $\mathrm{WLM}(n, k)$ as stated, because $\mathrm{WLM}(n, k)$ asks for a special form of $f$ in the local ring, and not only for a regular center. For $n = 1$ each regular local ring at a center is a field or a discrete valuation ring, so the remark on discrete valuation rings, with a full proof, gives $\mathrm{WLM}(1, k)$. We gave only a sketch of that remark.
- For $n = 4$, $\mathrm{WLM}(4, k)$ is open, and local uniformization in transcendence degree 4 in characteristic $p$ is open (Volume 9, Conjecture 2.3.1). This section does not prove the open case. It shows that over a perfect field the open case follows from $\mathrm{WLM}(4, k)$.
- The difficulty is the defect. Assume that the step $E'/E$ has defect $p$ at $w$, in the sense of the subsection "The defect in the tower" of Section 4.2 and of Chapter 3. Then $e = f = 1$ for $w$ over $v$, that is, the extension is immediate. In this case the set of the values $w(\theta - c)$ with $c \in E$ has no largest element. Indeed, assume that $\gamma = w(\theta - c)$ is the largest value. Since $\Gamma_w = \Gamma_v$, there is $b \in E$ with $v(b) = \gamma$. Then $(\theta - c)/b$ is a unit of $\mathcal{O}_w$. Since the residue fields are equal, there is $c' \in \mathcal{O}_v$ with the same class, and $w(\theta - c - bc') > \gamma$, which is false. Since $f - h^p = (\theta - h)^p$, also the values $w(f - h^p)$ with $h \in E$ increase without a maximum. In the remark on discrete valuation rings the monomial form came from an $h$ with the largest order of $f - h^p$. Along a valuation with a defect there is no such $h$ for the valuation itself, and a proof of $\mathrm{WLM}(n, k)$ must find another way. This is where the problem of dimension 4 lies.

### Notes and references

The reduction of local uniformization to purely inseparable steps of degree $p$ is a remark of Temkin [Tem13, Remark 1.3.5], under the hypothesis that $[k : k^p]$ is finite. Theorem 4.3.2 proves a form of it for a perfect ground field, with the step of degree $p$ in the form of Definition 4.3.1, which turns one regular center into one regular center. The proof uses Theorem 4.1.1 [Tem13, Theorem 1.3.2], the Frobenius isomorphism, and the fact that a regular local ring is integrally closed (Volume 1, Theorem 1.3.12, in the form of Corollary 1.3.13). The toric step, Lemma 4.3.12, uses the lattice lemma of Section 2.1 (Lemma 2.1.4). The facts on integral extensions are in [AM69, Chapter 5], and the regularity of polynomial rings over a field and the excellence of finitely generated algebras are in [Mat]. The end cases of Volume 7, Section 3.5, are of the ramified type. The defect is treated in Volume 3, Section 3.2, and in Chapter 3 of this volume. The names of the Lean statements of this section are in the Lean library. Part 2 of Lemma 4.3.7, Lemma 4.3.12 and the general case of Theorem 4.3.14 have no Lean proof.
